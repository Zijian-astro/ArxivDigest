EvoPINN: Agentic Discovery of Executable Algorithms for Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Peng Yin , Kai Li , Yifan Zhang , Jian Cheng

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem of manually engineering neural representations and loss formulations for Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) that solve partial differential equations (PDEs). The main idea is EvoPINN, an agentic framework that uses an LLM to iteratively propose and verify programmatic modifications to PINN architectures, ensuring scientific validity through strict structural verification and PDE evaluation. For astronomy, this could be highly relevant for automatically discovering specialized neural network architectures for solving PDEs in astrophysics (e.g., radiative transfer, magnetohydrodynamics) or for fitting complex models to JWST data. A concrete workflow could involve using EvoPINN to discover a novel architecture for SED fitting that incorporates physical constraints from stellar population synthesis models. However, the paper focuses on PDEs and may require adaptation for astronomical inverse problems that are not purely PDE-based.

Reason

Proposes an LLM agentic framework for automated discovery of algorithms for Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), directly relevant to automated scientific discovery and AI for physics.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the framework can handle inverse problems or data-fitting tasks beyond forward PDE solving.
  • Look for experiments on PDEs relevant to astrophysics (e.g., radiative transfer, Poisson equation).
  • Assess the computational cost of the agentic search for large-scale problems.
  • Check if the paper provides code or guidelines for adapting the search space to new physical domains.
Abstract

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for solving partial differential equations (PDEs), yet their performance heavily relies on the manual, trial-and-error engineering of neural representations, loss formulations, and optimization dynamics. While Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a promising avenue for automated design, unconstrained code generation often yields mathematically invalid or numerically unstable solutions under strict scientific computing constraints. To bridge this gap, we propose \textbf{EvoPINN}, an agentic framework that reformulates PINN development from labor-intensive manual design into a rigorous, execution-grounded algorithm discovery problem. EvoPINN navigates a modular search space by decoupling neural representations from training programs, utilizing an LLM agent to iteratively propose memory-conditioned programmatic modifications. To ensure scientific validity, all candidates undergo strict structural verification and budget-matched PDE evaluation. Extensive experiments across diverse PDE regimes (oscillatory, elliptic, dissipative, and nonlinear transport) demonstrate that EvoPINN discovers PDE-specialized learning algorithms that significantly reduce relative $L_{2}$ error compared to baselines. Crucially, EvoPINN autonomously invented SLRC-PINN, a novel architecture whose performance gains persist under rigorous parameter-matched comparisons, establishing the viability of execution-grounded agents for discovering genuinely new scientific computing mechanisms.

TreeCCA: Canonical Correlation Analysis via Gradient-Boosted Trees

James Chapman

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces TreeCCA, a method to perform canonical correlation analysis using gradient-boosted trees instead of linear or neural network encoders. The key innovation is a loss function that allows standard tree libraries (XGBoost, LightGBM) to be trained end-to-end for CCA, providing nonlinear feature interactions while retaining interpretability through feature importance scores. This is highly relevant to astronomy because CCA is a natural tool for finding correlations between different wavelength observations (e.g., optical vs. X-ray) or between photometric and spectroscopic features. The interpretability is a major advantage over neural CCA, as it can reveal which physical features drive the correlation. A concrete workflow would be using TreeCCA to find the most important photometric bands for predicting spectroscopic properties of high-redshift galaxies or AGN. A limitation is that the current implementation may not scale to extremely high-dimensional data (e.g., full spectra) without feature engineering.

Reason

Provides a nonlinear, interpretable method for canonical correlation analysis (CCA) on tabular data, directly applicable to multi-wavelength astronomical catalogs.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for experiments on datasets with similar dimensionality and sparsity to astronomical catalogs (e.g., many bands, missing data).
  • Check whether the method handles missing values natively, as astronomical catalogs often have incomplete multi-wavelength coverage.
  • Examine the interpretability outputs: are the gain importances stable and physically meaningful?
Abstract

Gradient-boosted trees dominate tabular machine learning, yet canonical correlation analysis has always relied on linear or neural encoders. We propose \textbf{TreeCCA}, the first method to train gradient-boosted tree ensembles end-to-end as CCA encoders, inheriting their plug-and-play reliability: no architecture design, familiar hyperparameters, and strong performance with defaults. The technical enabler is the Eckart-Young (EY) loss, which supplies closed-form per-sample gradients that slot directly into any standard GBT library (XGBoost, LightGBM) as a custom objective. TreeCCA is the first CCA method to combine nonlinear accuracy with native interpretability: every tree split selects one feature, so gain importances reveal which inputs drive cross-view correlation at no extra cost. We demonstrate these properties on synthetic benchmarks, where TreeCCA matches or exceeds Deep CCA (2.61 vs.\ 2.43 on Signed Power; 2.93 vs.\ 2.89 on Hermite), and on a sparse benchmark with zero linear cross-view covariance, where TreeCCA recovers the true support with $\text{Precision@}S = 1.00$ at $p=50$ while PMD finds no signal. On the UCI HAR sensor-fusion benchmark, TreeCCA achieves comparable accuracy to Deep CCA at $5\times$ lower cost, while XGBoost gain importances directly validate a physics-motivated hypothesis about the data --- an interpretation not readily available with neural encoders. Across five popular tabular multi-view datasets, TreeMCCA consistently matches or exceeds linear CCA in both nonlinear correlation extraction and downstream classification accuracy.

SciFigAlign: Scoring Scientific Figures by Fine-tuned Alignment of Visuals with Manuscript Evidence

Chuanzhi Xu , Zihan Deng , Huiqi Liang , Chengkun Yue , Zhanlin Cui , Pengfei Ye , Weidong Cai

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces a method for scoring scientific figures based on their alignment with manuscript evidence, going beyond generic image quality or image-text similarity. It fine-tunes a multimodal model (CLIP + SciBERT) with cross-attention and ranking loss to evaluate figures on clarity, relevance, informativeness, and structure. This is highly relevant to astronomy because it could be used to automatically assess the quality of figures in astronomical papers, e.g., to find papers with well-presented JWST spectra or to build a literature review agent that prioritizes papers with informative figures. A concrete workflow would be using SciFigAlign to rank figures from a large corpus of astronomy papers for a given topic (e.g., 'little red dots'). A limitation is that the model is trained on computer science conference papers, so its performance on astronomy-specific figure types (e.g., spectra, SEDs, multi-panel comparison plots) may be lower without fine-tuning.

Reason

Directly addresses automated assessment of scientific figures, which could be used to evaluate figures in astronomical papers or to build tools for literature review.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the model's performance is evaluated on figure types common in astronomy (e.g., spectra, color-magnitude diagrams, multi-wavelength cutouts).
  • Look for the dataset composition: what fraction of figures are from astronomy or related physical sciences?
  • Examine the ablation study to see if the model relies on caption text or citing paragraphs, which may be structured differently in astronomy papers.
Abstract

Scientific figure assessment in peer review differs fundamentally from general image quality evaluation: a figure must be visually legible, faithfully support the manuscript's claims, and communicate evidence with a clear visual hierarchy. However, if we apply traditional image assessment methods to scientific figure quality assessment, limitations emerge: classic IQA models capture perceptual quality or aesthetics but cannot judge whether a figure serves the paper's scientific argument; CLIP-based methods assess generic image-text correspondence, yet lack understanding of manuscript context; and zero-shot LLM/VLM judges, when repurposed for figure scoring, often yield overly concentrated scores with limited fusion of visual and textual evidence. We introduce an annotated dataset of 3,857 scientific figures from peer-reviewed conference papers, each rated along four peer-review-oriented dimensions: Clarity, Relevance, Informativeness, and Structure. We propose SciFigAlign, a fine-tuned multimodal scorer that grounds figure quality assessment in manuscript evidence. Given a figure crop, caption, citing paragraphs, and light paper context, SciFigAlign fine-tunes CLIP and SciBERT end-to-end with per-modality cross-attention and CubeMLP fusion, jointly optimizing SmoothL1 regression with a within-paper ranking hinge loss. Under paper-level splits, SciFigAlign achieves a macro MAE of 0.3524 and a within-paper pairwise accuracy of 81.64% on the test set with n = 396, a 59% relative error reduction over the best LLM-as-judge baseline with MAE 0.864. Ablations confirm that manuscript-grounded inputs, citing-context denoising, and ranking supervision are all critical, showing that scientific figure assessment requires learned alignment between visual content and manuscript evidence rather than prompting alone, even with state-of-the-art VLMs.

Sky sphere representation in language models

Aleksandr Berdnikov , Yevgeny Liokumovich

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether large language models (~100B parameters) have an internal representation of the night sky map that can be decoded from their residual stream. The authors find that most open-source models do encode such a representation, with median angular errors of 12-21 degrees and up to 65-85% of variance explained in leave-one-out testing. This is the first demonstration of a curved high-dimensional irreducible feature manifold in LLMs. For astronomy, this suggests that LLMs may already contain useful spatial knowledge about the night sky that could be leveraged for tasks like source identification, cross-matching, or planning observations. An astronomer could potentially decode this representation to query an LLM for nearby objects or constellations without external databases. The main caveat is that the representation is coarse (12-21 degree errors) and may not be accurate enough for precise astronomical work.

Reason

Directly investigates whether LLMs encode a representation of the night sky; highly relevant for astronomy-AI intersection.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the angular error distribution: is it uniform across the sky or worse in certain regions?
  • Look for any analysis of whether the representation includes magnitude, color, or other astrophysical properties beyond position.
  • Examine the leave-one-out testing methodology to understand how robust the decoding is to different prompts and model architectures.
Abstract

We analyze whether language models of size ~100B have a representation of the night sky map that is decodable from their residual stream. We find that most of the considered open-source models do have such a representation, and it often even surfaces to the top principal components on prompts that ask questions like ``what is close to this object in the night sky''. In all but one model this representation showed significant scores in LOO testing, containing up to 65-85% of variance ($R^2$-score) and having median angular error down to $12^\circ-21^\circ$. We verify that our representation is not a simple leak from a correlated flat representation. SeasonStereo: Robust Dense Stereo Matching for Multi-Date Satellite Imagery via Generative AI

Álvaro Díaz-Laureano , Roger Marí , Elías Masquil , Pablo Arias , Gabriele Facciolo

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem of dense stereo matching from diachronic satellite images with varying seasonal and illumination conditions. It proposes SeasonStereo, which trains on synthetic image pairs with controlled seasonal variation and uses zero-shot geometric priors from foundation models. This is directly relevant to astronomy because multi-epoch astronomical images (e.g., from JWST, HST, or Rubin) also suffer from varying conditions (e.g., dither patterns, PSF variations). The method could be adapted for deblending, astrometric alignment, or 3D reconstruction of astronomical scenes. A limitation is that the paper uses satellite imagery (not astronomical) and relies on synthetic training data, which may need careful domain adaptation for astronomical images.

Reason

Dense stereo matching for multi-date satellite imagery is directly relevant to astronomical image analysis, especially for JWST, HST, or survey data where multi-epoch images have varying conditions.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the synthetic training pipeline can be adapted to astronomical image characteristics (e.g., Poisson noise, PSF).
  • Look for the specific foundation model used for zero-shot geometric priors and whether it is publicly available.
  • Assess the computational cost of the framework for large-scale survey data.
  • Check if the method handles non-rigid scene changes (e.g., moving sources in astronomy).
Abstract

Accurate 3D reconstruction from satellite imagery typically relies on near-simultaneous stereo pairs, limiting its applicability to diachronic settings where multi-date images exhibit varying seasonal and illumination conditions. Training dense stereo matching models robust to appearance changes is a long-standing challenge, as aligned multi-date imagery and ground-truth geometry are costly to obtain at scale. We propose SeasonStereo, a scalable framework that addresses disparity estimation from diachronic satellite images by training on synthetic image pairs with controlled seasonal appearance variation, while leveraging zero-shot geometric priors from foundation models. SeasonStereo matches the accuracy of state-of-the-art LiDAR-supervised models, while producing sharper geometric details without requiring aligned real multi-date training products or LiDAR-derived labels. As a result, SeasonStereo offers a practical path toward large-scale 3D reconstruction from heterogeneous satellite images with reduced supervision cost.

Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies

Peter Kirgis , Sayash Kapoor , Andrew Schwartz , Stephan Rabanser , David Africa , Konstantinos Voudouris , Viet Nguyen , Toby Pilditch , Magda Dubois , Harry Coppock , Cozmin Ududec , Nitya Nadgir , Matilda Orona , Tilman Bayer , Derrick Chan-Sew , Yue Ling , Abhishek Shetty , Helen Toner , Gillian Hadfield , Seth Lazar , Steve Newman , Shoshannah Tekofsky , Rishi Bommasani , Arvind Narayanan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper evaluates whether AI agents can conduct open-ended AI research by having them tackle the central research question of two unpublished NeurIPS papers. The agents completed all engineering tasks but failed to make substantial progress on the research questions, leading to unambiguous rejection by the original authors. Five recurring failure modes are identified: poor judgment of publishable research bar, uncreative responses to shortcomings, ineffective backtracking, poor resource awareness, and instruction drift. For astronomy, this is directly relevant to building research agents that could assist with literature review, data analysis, or hypothesis generation. A concrete workflow would be testing an agent on a well-defined but open-ended astronomical analysis task, such as characterizing a sample of little red dots from JWST data. A key caveat is that the study used frontier models with high compute budgets, and the failure modes may be even more pronounced with smaller models or lower budgets.

Reason

Directly evaluates AI agents for open-ended AI research, a core interest in automated scientific discovery; failure modes are highly instructive for building astronomy research agents.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for detailed descriptions of the five failure modes and whether they are likely to apply to astronomical research workflows.
  • Check the agent architecture and scaffolding used; is it open-source or reproducible?
  • Examine the expert reviews and survey responses for specific criticisms that could inform better agent design for science.
  • Look for any discussion of whether the agents could have succeeded with more time, different prompting, or different tools.
Abstract

Forecasts of explosive AI progress hinge on AI agents automating AI research. But evidence on whether agents can carry out open-ended AI research is thin. Current evaluations either test agents on narrow, verifiable tasks, which excludes open-ended research, or submit AI-generated papers to blind peer review, which is overstretched, stochastic, and suffers from poor review quality. We introduce a third way to measure progress towards AI R\&D automation. An agent takes on the central, open-ended research question of a high-quality unpublished paper, and the paper's original authors grade its output. We call these shadow evaluations. We ran shadow evaluations on two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions, giving frontier agents six days and thousands of dollars of compute. The agents completed all of the engineering without human help, yet could not make substantial progress towards answering the research questions. As a result, both papers were unambiguously rejected by the authors. We identify five recurring failure modes: poor judgment about the bar for publishable research, uncreative responses to shortcomings in the research design, ineffective backtracking from dead ends, poor resource awareness, and instruction drift. A robustness check with a second model and scaffold reproduced these failures. We release the expert reviews, survey responses, agent repositories, and logs. Our results provide early evidence that today's agents can do the engineering of AI research, but struggle with critical parts of the research lifecycle.
