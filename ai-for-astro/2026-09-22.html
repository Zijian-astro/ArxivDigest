Beyond the Text: Verifying That Agent-Written Papers Are Backed by Their Artifacts

Qiuhong Shen , Benlong Wu , Hanjin Liu , Yuang Qi , Kejiang Chen

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem that LLM agents can now write research papers, but nobody reliably checks whether the reported results actually match the code and experiments behind them. It introduces ReAgent, an auditing framework that extracts structured scientific claims from a document and then checks the associated repository both statically (does the code implement the claimed methods and configs?) and dynamically (do the experiments actually reproduce the reported numbers?). The core idea is to combine two complementary views, because some inconsistencies only appear when you compare what the text claims against what the code does versus what running the code produces. For astronomy, this matters because as we build LLM agents that run pipelines, do SED fitting, or draft analysis notes, we need automated ways to catch hard-coded values, unimplemented steps, or unsupported claims before trusting outputs. A concrete workflow would be wrapping an agent that processes JWST imaging or spectra with a ReAgent-style audit that verifies each claimed measurement traces back to executable code and real data. The main caveat is that it relies on a curated benchmark of document-repository pairs and assumes the repository is available and runnable, which may not hold for many real astronomy projects.

Reason

Directly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents, addressing verification of agent-generated research claims against code and experiments. Highly applicable to building trustworthy LLM research agents for astronomy workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check what fraction of claims can be verified when the repository is incomplete or uses non-standard environments
  • Look for the benchmark construction details and whether any astronomy or physical-science examples are included
  • Examine how dynamic auditing handles stochastic or expensive experiments
  • Check whether the audit report format could be adapted to trace astronomical measurement provenance
Abstract

Large language model agents are increasingly capable of conducting research autonomously, producing research documents alongside the code and experiments that ostensibly support them. Yet whether the reported findings are consistently supported by corresponding implementations and execution evidence remains largely unexplored: existing review practices primarily assess textual quality and cannot reliably identify inconsistencies such as hard-coded metrics, unimplemented methods, or unsupported experimental results. We present ReAgent, an automated auditing framework for assessing the consistency between agent-generated research documents and their associated repositories. ReAgent constructs structured representations of scientific claims from research documents and uses them to guide repository analysis and evidence collection. Static auditing examines whether claimed methodologies, implementations, and experimental configurations are consistently reflected in the repository, while dynamic auditing executes relevant experiments and collects execution evidence to assess empirical findings. By combining static analysis with dynamic evidence, ReAgent identifies inconsistencies that may remain hidden under either perspective alone, such as experiments that reproduce reported numbers while deviating from the claimed methodology. The collected evidence and audit decisions are organized into a structured repository-level audit report, enabling transparent evidence traceability. We evaluate ReAgent on a manually curated benchmark of agent-generated research document--repository pairs and compare it against representative static and reproduction-based baselines. Experimental results demonstrate that ReAgent effectively identifies inconsistencies between reported research findings and their supporting repository evidence.

ScholarStack: Layered Research Asset Orchestration and Cross-Task Reuse for Scientific Agents

ScholarSeed AI Team : Ao Zhang , Caoqinwei Gong , Guanglei Wang , Haifan Zhang , Hanwei Zhang , Jiayi Sheng , Jihai Zhang , Kai Ying , Liyun Dai , Tingyu Zhu , Wei Chen , Wei Luo , Wenkai Fang , Xiaoyu Qiu , Xue Jiang , Yi Wang , Yuan Cao , Zheng Yu , Wotao Yin

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses a practical inefficiency in scientific agents: most systems are built around single tasks, so the same papers are repeatedly retrieved, segmented, and interpreted, and understanding gained in one task is not reused in the next. ScholarStack proposes a layered research asset framework that compiles a paper collection into reusable, versioned, provenance-preserving assets at three levels: source-grounded paper-level statements, domain-level organization, and evidence-grounded cross-paper syntheses. A common access interface returns task-specific views at the evidence granularity each task needs, preserving study conditions, source traceability, and verification status. The authors instantiate this on four task families spanning ten settings and find that quality gains concentrate on tasks requiring cross-paper evidence, such as multi-paper question answering and literature review generation, while query-time token cost falls because assets are compiled once and reused. For astronomy, this is directly useful: a research agent that builds a persistent, provenance-tracked knowledge base over JWST and high-redshift literature could support literature reviews, claim checking, and cross-paper synthesis without re-reading the same papers repeatedly. A concrete workflow would be to compile an astronomy paper collection into ScholarStack-style assets and test it on tasks like summarizing evidence for a specific galaxy-population claim or tracking how a measurement has been revised across papers. The main caveat is that the paper is a systems framework, so I would need to check how well it handles the specialized terminology, figures, and tables common in astronomy papers, and whether the asset compilation is reproducible and auditable.

Reason

This paper directly addresses scientific agents and reusable research assets for literature-based tasks, which is highly relevant to my interest in LLM agents for literature review and automated scientific discovery in astronomy. The layered asset framework and cross-task reuse are exactly the kind of infrastructure that could support astronomy research workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the framework handles figures, tables, and equations, which are central in astronomy papers
  • Look for details on provenance tracking and versioning, since reproducibility matters for scientific claims
  • Check whether the evaluation includes domain-specific scientific literature or only general benchmarks
  • Assess the cost and effort of compiling assets for a large astronomy corpus and whether the interface supports incremental updates
Abstract

Scientific agents support a range of literature-based research tasks, such as retrieval, question answering, evidence-grounded generation, and claim assessment. Most existing systems, however, are organized around individual tasks: the same papers are repeatedly retrieved, segmented, and interpreted, and the understanding built in one task is difficult to reuse in the next. We present ScholarStack, a layered research asset framework that compiles a paper collection into reusable, versioned, and provenance-preserving assets at three complementary levels: source-grounded paper-level statements, domain-level organization, and evidence-grounded cross-paper syntheses. A common access interface returns task-specific views at the evidence granularity each task requires, preserving study conditions, source traceability, and verification status. We instantiate the framework on four task families spanning ten task settings, comparing agents that use the compiled assets with task-specific baselines under matched base models. Quality gains concentrate on tasks that require cross-paper evidence, such as multi-paper question answering and literature review generation, and query-time token cost falls on every task where it is measured, with assets compiled once and reused across tasks. These results suggest that layered research assets can serve as shared infrastructure for scientific agents, shifting literature-based assistance from isolated document processing toward cumulative, evidence-grounded workflows.

APEXA: Execution-Integrity Enforcement for Multi-Agent LLM Automation of Synchrotron Data Reduction

Pawan K. Tripathi , Hemant Sharma , Andrew Chuang , Mathew J. Cherukara

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of automating synchrotron data reduction, a multi-step expert bottleneck, using LLM agents. The main AI idea is execution-integrity enforcement: a deterministic tool-layer guard that only surfaces results backed by an actually executed tool call, preventing the agent from fabricating calibration reports. They deploy a multi-agent framework with 61 tools, release a benchmark of facility tasks, and validate on real beamline data. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because JWST and other facilities face similar risks when LLM agents drive calibration or reduction pipelines. A concrete workflow would be applying the same guard and benchmark design to JWST pipeline steps such as detector calibration, spectral extraction, or image combination. The caveat is that the paper is a preliminary deployment report and large-scale agent scoring is left to future work.

Reason

Presents a deployed multi-agent LLM framework for synchrotron data reduction with execution-integrity enforcement, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and LLM agents for facility-scale data pipelines.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the execution-integrity guard is implemented and whether it can wrap existing astronomy pipeline tools
  • Look for the benchmark taxonomy separating wasted compute from instrument damage and whether it maps to JWST operations
  • Check the real beamline validation details and whether the natural-language prompt interface is reproducible
  • Look for released traces or code that could be adapted to an astronomy data-reduction agent
Abstract

Synchrotron data reduction, detector calibration followed by azimuthal integration of terabyte-scale diffraction series, is a multi-step, expert-bound bottleneck that increasingly limits the science rate of user facilities. LLM agents promise to collapse it, but driving a real pipeline with a stochastic model creates a failure mode chat benchmarks cannot see: an agent can report a calibration that was never computed. Correctness here is a property of what executed, not of the transcript. We present APEXA, a deployed multi-agent framework (61 tools over heterogeneous compute, run as a single reasoning loop) automating calibration and integration from natural language at a major light source. We make three contributions. First, execution-integrity enforcement: a deterministic tool-layer guard that refuses to surface any result not backed by an executed tool call, with a parser tolerant of cross-model tool-call format drift: in deployment, a frontier model fabricated a complete calibration-comparison report for commands that never ran, which the guard converts to an explicit non-result; the same code gates an optional motor-control surface at 0/200 adversarial violations against a simulated IOC, versus 15/200 for an equivalent safety prompt. Second, we release APEXA-Bench, an evaluation harness of 58 facility tasks (50 base plus an 8-task cross-detector slice) organized by a four-class physical-consequence taxonomy, the first benchmark axis we know of separating a wasted compute cycle from a damaged instrument; its cross-detector grading against NIST-traceable lattice constants surfaced two latent pipeline bugs. Large-scale agent scoring is left to a full-length study. Third, we validate APEXA on real beamline data: from one natural-language prompt it recovers detector geometry and integrates a full attenuation/exposure sweep. We release the framework, harness and traces.

Taramandal-GPT: Enhancing Astrodynamics Problem-Solving with Knowledge Retrieval and Structured Thinking

Akhil Sharma , Jatin Gupta , Ali Imam Abidi

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the limited effectiveness of general LLMs in specialized astronomy and astrodynamics tasks, especially multi-step reasoning, symbolic manipulation, and domain terminology. It presents Taramandal-GPT, built on Qwen3-8b with a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline and a fallback mechanism, and evaluates it on a 299-question astrodynamics benchmark using numeric and semantic scoring. The results show competitive performance against larger open and closed models, with particular strength on thinking-intensive tasks. For astronomy, this is directly relevant as a template for building domain-specific LLM assistants that combine retrieval over textbooks, papers, or documentation with structured reasoning. A concrete workflow would be to adapt the same RAG and fallback design to JWST spectroscopy, SED fitting, or literature review for high-redshift galaxies. The main caveat is that the benchmark is astrodynamics-focused, so you should check whether the retrieval corpus and evaluation transfer to observational astronomy and whether the model handles numerical or tabular data well.

Reason

This paper directly builds a domain-adapted LLM with retrieval-augmented generation for astrodynamics problem-solving, which is highly relevant to astronomy-focused LLM agents, RAG, and scientific workflow automation. It is one of the closest matches to the stated interests.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the composition and source of the retrieval corpus and whether it includes observational astronomy literature
  • Look for details on the fallback mechanism and how it handles retrieval failures
  • Check whether the evaluation includes numerical reasoning, unit handling, or table-based questions relevant to astronomy
  • Look for code or model release that would allow adaptation to JWST or survey data workflows
Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have shown remarkable progress in natural language understanding, yet their effectiveness in specialized fields like astronomy and astrodynamics remains limited due to challenges in multi-step reasoning, symbolic manipulation, and domain-specific terminology. To address this, we present Taramandal-GPT (Constellation-GPT), a domain-adapted framework built on the Qwen3-8b backbone, enhanced with a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline and a fallback mechanism for improved contextual precision. We evaluate it on the Astrodynamics Problems Benchmark (APBench), a dataset of 299 questions covering foundational to advanced levels of space science. Using a dual evaluation method - numeric margin-based scoring and semantic similarity assessment - Taramandal-GPT achieves competitive performance against state-of-the-art open- and closed-source models, with notable strength in thinking-intensive tasks. These results highlight the value of specialized LLMs for domains demanding accuracy and interpretability, positioning Taramandal-GPT as a step toward reliable Artificial Intelligence (AI) assistants for astrophysics, spacecraft engineering, and space exploration.

DeepInstructor: An Agentic AI Instructor for Experience-Driven Idea Evaluation

Rongcan Pei , Fang Guo , Qinglin Qi , Qi Zhu , Yun Luo , Jianhao Yan , Minjun Zhu , Qiujie Xie , Dehong Zheng , Yue Zhang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the bottleneck in automated scientific discovery: LLMs can generate many research ideas, but evaluating them well is harder. It proposes DeepInstructor, an agentic framework that builds an Experience Graph from tens of thousands of peer reviews and uses a ReAct-based agent to retrieve dimension-specific evidence for traceable evaluation. It also introduces a dataset with controlled pairwise comparisons across novelty, significance, and feasibility, and reports substantial improvements in alignment with human judgments. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because idea evaluation is a core part of proposal review, research prioritization, and agentic discovery workflows. A concrete workflow would be using a similar experience-graph agent to evaluate JWST proposal ideas or to triage research directions in high-redshift galaxy studies. The caveat is that the experience graph is built from peer reviews in a specific domain, so check whether the approach generalizes to astronomy and whether the evaluation data is accessible.

Reason

It is an agentic framework for scientific idea evaluation using structured scholarly experience, which is directly relevant to automated scientific discovery and LLM research agents. The method could transfer to evaluating astronomy research ideas or proposals.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check what domains the peer reviews come from and whether astronomy is included
  • Look for whether the Experience Graph construction is reproducible and adaptable
  • Assess whether the evaluation metrics align with expert judgment in physical sciences
  • Determine whether the agent can cite specific evidence for its evaluations
Abstract

As automated scientific discovery advances, Large Language Models (LLMs) can now generate research ideas at an unprecedented scale, shifting the bottleneck from idea generation to idea evaluation. Existing evaluators mainly rely on parametric LLM knowledge or unstructured retrieval, producing judgments that lack the experience-grounded reasoning used by human instructors. To address this, we propose DeepInstructor, an agentic framework that formulates idea evaluation as reasoning over structured scholarly experience. DeepInstructor constructs an Experience Graph from 58,607 peer reviews and employs a ReAct-based agent to retrieve dimension-specific evidence for traceable evaluation. We further introduce DeepInstruct, a dataset with controlled pairwise comparisons across novelty, significance, and feasibility. Experiments show that DeepInstructor substantially outperforms existing baselines, improving Hit@1 and Hit@2 alignment with human judgments by 24.4% and 29.7%, respectively. Our findings suggest that scientific idea evaluation can be grounded in explicit reasoning over structured scholarly experience

Success Leaves Detours: Learning Executable Walkthroughs for Long-Horizon Agents

Kaijie Chen , Chenyu Fang , Liang Yan , Bo Li , Bo Zhang , Peng Ye

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that self-improving agents learn from past trajectories, but sparse-reward trajectories contain failures, loops, and detours, and simple summaries lose the state conditions and action dependencies needed to actually execute the procedure. It proposes Trace, which detects progress anchors from rewards and persistent state changes, propagates credit to find valuable transitions, and estimates action prerequisites from cross-episode evidence, then uses backward dependency slicing to extract dependency-consistent action chains. The result is executable Walkthrough Memory encoding entry conditions, ordered state-action-effect steps, and completion/failure predicates that support reuse and programmatic verification. For astronomy, this is directly applicable to agents that learn to run JWST reduction pipelines, SED fitting, or catalog cross-matching, because it would let them extract and reuse verified multi-step procedures rather than raw logs. A concrete workflow would be having an agent learn executable walkthroughs for photometric redshift estimation or AGN selection from many past runs. The caveat is that it is tested on J-TTL, WebShop, and ScienceWorld, so transfer to real astronomical software environments needs validation.

Reason

Directly relevant to long-horizon LLM agents and executable procedure learning, which is central to building astronomy research agents that perform multi-step data analysis. The dependency-grounded walkthrough extraction could help agents reuse successful analysis pipelines.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how progress anchors and persistent state changes are detected in practice
  • Look at the backward dependency slicing algorithm and whether it handles long tool chains
  • Examine the ScienceWorld results for any physical-science-like task transfer
  • Check whether Walkthrough Memory can be verified programmatically in a real software environment
Abstract

Test-time self-evolving agents improve by reusing past experience, yet sparse-reward trajectories contain failures, loops, and detours, while summaries often omit the state conditions and action dependencies needed for execution. We study executable Walkthrough induction from sparse-reward trajectories: extracting compact, state-conditioned, and verifiable procedures. Our key observation is that delayed credit identifies actions associated with progress but cannot determine whether they produce facts required by later actions. We propose Trace, a credit-guided, dependency-grounded framework that compiles noisy trajectories into executable Walkthrough Memory. It detects progress anchors from rewards and persistent state changes, propagates credit to identify valuable transitions, and estimates action prerequisites from cross-episode success and failure evidence. Backward dependency slicing then traces required facts to their producers, extracting dependency-consistent action chains while removing irrelevant loops and detours. The resulting Walkthroughs encode entry conditions, ordered state--action--effect steps, and completion and failure predicates, supporting reuse, intermediate-state resumption, and programmatic verification. Experiments on J-TTL, WebShop, and ScienceWorld with three open-source LLMs show that Trace consistently outperforms eight test-time learning and memory baselines. Compared with the strongest baseline, it improves average AUC and Final-$3$ by $30.0%$ and $40.5%$, respectively, while using fewer inference tokens. These results show that long-horizon interaction benefits more from state-conditioned executable procedures than from complete trajectories or abstract summaries.

SALSA: Semi-Autonomous Literature Summarization Assistant

William Schertzer , Sonakshi Gupta , Rampi Ramprasad

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper introduces SALSA, an open-source platform for extracting structured scientific datasets from multimodal literature sources. It combines document parsing, large language models, optical character recognition, computer vision, and figure digitization with user-guided correction tools to recover information from text, tables, figures, and captions. Users can configure extraction stages, define dataset schemas, intervene on digitized figures, and export verified data for downstream analysis and machine learning. The design goal is to automate repetitive literature curation while preserving expert oversight where judgment is needed. For astronomy, this is directly applicable to building datasets from the literature, such as compiling galaxy properties, spectral measurements, or instrument configurations from JWST papers. A concrete workflow would be to use SALSA to digitize plots and tables from high-redshift galaxy papers and export a structured catalog for meta-analysis. The caveat is that the paper is demonstrated on materials research, so you should check whether the figure digitization and schema tools handle astronomical plot types and units.

Reason

This paper presents a human-in-the-loop platform for extracting structured scientific datasets from multimodal literature, which is highly relevant to LLM agents for literature review and automated scientific discovery in astronomy. It combines document parsing, LLMs, OCR, computer vision, and figure digitization.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the figure digitization supports astronomical plot types such as spectra, SEDs, and color-magnitude diagrams
  • Look for the schema definition interface and whether it can encode astronomical units and uncertainties
  • Assess how much human correction is needed and whether the workflow scales to hundreds of papers
  • See whether the exported data format is compatible with astronomical catalog tools
Abstract

SALSA (Semi-Autonomous Literature Summarization Assistant) is an open- source, human-in-the-loop platform for extracting structured scientific datasets from multimodal literature sources. The software combines document parsing, large language models, optical character recognition, computer vision, figure digitization, and user-guided correction tools to recover structured information from text, tables, figures, and captions. Users can configure extraction stages, define dataset schemas, perform interventions on digitized figures, and export verified data for downstream analysis and machine learning. SALSA is designed to automate repetitive literature curation tasks while preserving oversight where expert judgment is required. By supporting customizable extraction workflows across diverse input types, the software provides a flexible framework for scalable, reliable data curation for materials research, and potentially across several disciplines. Users are responsible for ensuring that all inputs, extraction workflows, and downstream uses comply with applicable publisher agreements, copyright and licensing terms, institutional policies, and data-use requirements.

RS-Claw-Evolution: Environment-Feedback-Driven Evolution for Lightweight Remote Sensing Agents in Long-Horizon Tasks

Kai Ouyang , Dongyang Hou , Liangtian Liu , Zeyuan Wang , Ziyu Li , Chengfu Liu , Zichao Tang , Xuezhi Cui , Shengwu Ouyang , Wentao Yang , Hanwen Yu , Haifeng Li

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem that lightweight LLM agents based on compact models struggle with multi-step remote sensing tasks because they lose long-horizon state, underuse environmental feedback, and receive sparse optimization signals. RS-Claw-Evolution improves these agents in three stages: interaction evolution uses executable code to control observations and maintain intermediate states, experience evolution learns from failure-recovery trajectories while masking faulty actions, and decision evolution uses reinforcement learning with multi-dimensional environment rewards and turn-level advantage protection. The result is that a 4B-parameter agent reaches 65.9% accuracy on Earth-Bench, beating a 32B untrained baseline and approaching GPT-5. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because remote sensing agents and astronomical survey agents share the same structure: multi-step tool use over large image and catalog datasets with long horizons and sparse rewards. A concrete workflow could be adapting this framework to build a lightweight agent that plans and executes JWST image reduction, source detection, and catalog cross-matching steps. The caveat is that the domain is remote sensing, so I should check whether the environment rewards and tool interfaces are general enough to be redefined for astronomical data.

Reason

It develops environment-feedback-driven evolution for lightweight LLM agents on long-horizon remote sensing tasks, which is closely analogous to astronomical survey analysis workflows. The techniques for maintaining long-horizon state, learning from failure trajectories, and credit assignment in tool-use sequences are directly transferable to JWST or Roman data-analysis agents.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the three evolution stages are implemented and whether the environment-feedback interface is domain-agnostic
  • Look for the failure-aware trajectory generation and error-turn masking details, and whether they require labeled success/failure signals
  • Check whether the reinforcement learning reward design could be adapted to astronomical task metrics
  • Look for any discussion of transferring the framework to other geospatial or scientific domains
Abstract

Large language model-driven remote sensing (RS) agents offer a promising approach to automating geospatial analysis. However, lightweight RS agents based on compact language models struggle with multi-step interactive tasks due to loss of long-horizon states, inefficient environmental feedback utilization, and sparse optimization signals. We propose RS-Claw-Evolution, an environment-feedback-driven framework that progressively improves lightweight agents through three stages. Interaction evolution uses executable code to control observations, maintain intermediate states, and reduce context redundancy. Experience evolution combines failure-aware trajectory generation with error-turn masking to learn from informative failure-recovery experiences without imitating faulty actions. Decision evolution uses reinforcement learning with multi-dimensional environment rewards and turn-level advantage protection to optimize tool-use behaviors and improve credit assignment in long sequences. On Earth-Bench, the optimized Qwen3-4B-based agent achieves 65.9% accuracy in Autonomous Planning mode, outperforming the untrained Qwen3-32B baseline (43.8%) and DeepSeek-V3.1 (60.8%), while approaching GPT-5 (71.6%). These results demonstrate that learning from environmental feedback can improve lightweight agents and narrow their performance gap with larger models in long-horizon RS tasks.
