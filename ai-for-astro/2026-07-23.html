MOF-Sleuth: Tool-Grounded Reward Alignment for Explainable Fine-Grained MOF CIF Auditing

Yu Liu , Zhiwei Yang , Diandian Guo , Kun Peng , Fangfang Yuan , Cong Cao , Chaozhuo Li , Zhiyuan Ma , Yanbing Liu , Guobin Zhao

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem of detecting subtle chemical and structural errors in large metal-organic framework (MOF) databases, which can compromise downstream simulation and machine learning results. The core idea is MOF-Sleuth, a reinforcement-guided CIF auditing agent with two modules: a deterministic Forensic Lab that derives composition, geometry, connectivity, occupancy, coordination, and charge evidence, and a Sleuth reasoning engine that uses this evidence to produce an evidence-grounded explanation, error types, and a binary decision. Reward-guided reinforcement learning turns tool measurements into chemical explanation-level supervision, rewarding not only the final answer but also cited chemical evidence and evidence-supported diagnoses. For astronomy, this approach could be directly adapted to validate JWST spectroscopic or imaging data products, detect anomalies in high-redshift galaxy catalogs, or audit SED fitting results with evidence-grounded explanations. An astronomer could imagine using a similar agent to check for subtle errors in photometric redshift estimates, spectral line identifications, or source classifications in large survey catalogs. The main caveat is that the method is designed for MOF-specific CIF files and would require adaptation to astronomical data formats and domain-specific validators.

Reason

This paper directly applies LLM agents to scientific data auditing (MOF CIF files), which is a clear example of automated scientific discovery. The tool-grounded reward alignment and evidence-grounded explanation approach could transfer to astronomical data validation.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the specific reward functions and how they incorporate tool measurements into the RL training loop.
  • Check whether the Forensic Lab module is domain-agnostic or requires MOF-specific chemistry knowledge.
  • Examine the Chem-GD metric to see if it could be adapted for astronomical data validation.
  • Look for any discussion of transferability to other scientific domains or data formats.
Abstract

Large metal-organic framework (MOF) databases support simulation, screening, and machine learning through crystallographic information files (CIFs). Subtle chemical and structural errors in these inputs can compromise downstream results and hinder manual inspection. LLM advances in computational chemistry offer paths beyond predictive screening toward fine-grained diagnosis with evidence-grounded explanations. However, two challenges remain: (i) limited fine-grained attribution: MOF-specific validators and machine-learning models scale detection but provide fixed checks, readiness scores, or coarse labels rather than evidence-grounded explanations; and (ii) unreliable CIF reasoning: direct LLM auditing is costly and unreliable because chemical evidence is implicit across atom-site records and requires geometric, connectivity, occupancy, and charge calculations. Both stem from weak coupling between chemical evidence and language-model explanation. We introduce MOF-Sleuth, a reinforcement-guided CIF auditing agent with two modules: a deterministic Forensic Lab and a Sleuth reasoning engine. The Lab derives composition, geometry, connectivity, occupancy, coordination, and charge evidence, and Sleuth uses this evidence to produce an evidence-grounded explanation, error types, and a binary decision. Reward-guided reinforcement learning (RL) turns tool measurements into chemical explanation-level supervision, rewarding not only the final answer but also cited chemical evidence and evidence-supported diagnoses. We introduce Chemically Grounded Diagnosis (Chem-GD), a metric that assesses whether a correct diagnosis is explained by factual, relevant CIF-derived evidence. Across four benchmarks, MOF-Sleuth establishes state-of-the-art performance among LLM-based approaches and MOF-specific machine-learning methods, demonstrating gains in detection, attribution, and grounded explanation quality.

PercepCap: Video Captioner with Structured Spatio-Temporal Perception

Yifan Xu , Zihao Wang , Zhixiao Wang , Jiaming Zhang , Yichun Yang , Desen Meng , Yuanxing Zhang , Pengfei Wan , Limin Wang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how to make video captioning models produce explicit, verifiable perceptual evidence (object trajectories and temporal events) before generating the final caption, rather than just outputting a description from a black-box model. The main idea is a 'perceive-describe' chain where the model first outputs a structured perception trace (e.g., 'object A moves from left to right at time t1-t2') and then generates the caption conditioned on that trace, trained with a two-stage supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning approach. For astronomy, this could be adapted to generate structured descriptions of astronomical phenomena from time-domain surveys (e.g., 'a transient brightens in the southern arm of the galaxy between frames 10 and 20'), or to produce captions for JWST images that explicitly mention detected sources and their positions. A concrete workflow would be feeding a sequence of JWST NIRCam images of a supernova into such a model to get a caption like 'a point source appears at (x,y) in band F200W and fades over 3 epochs'. A limitation is that the method is designed for natural videos with clear object trajectories, and astronomical images may have very different noise, resolution, and semantic structure.

Reason

The paper proposes a video captioning framework that makes spatiotemporal perception explicit before generating captions, which directly relates to multimodal models that combine visual and textual data, a key interest for astronomical image analysis and automated description.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the perception trace (object trajectories and events) is grounded with bounding boxes and timestamps, and how this grounding is obtained for training data.
  • Look for details on the reinforcement learning reward function and whether it could be adapted to domain-specific metrics (e.g., accuracy of source positions or flux measurements).
  • See if the paper discusses generalization to videos with many small, faint objects (like astronomical sources) versus a few salient objects.
Abstract

Video captioning requires fine-grained spatio-temporal understanding of videos, including spatial perception of where objects are located and temporal perception of when events occur. Existing MLLMs usually generate captions directly from video inputs without exposing the perceptual evidence behind descriptions. As a result, mistakes in spatiotemporal perception are only observed in the final caption, making it difficult to identify the underlying perceptual errors directly. To address these issues, we present PercepCap, a perception-aware video captioning framework that makes perceptual evidence explicit before producing the final caption. Specifically, PercepCap follows a perceive-describe generation chain, where the model first produces a spatiotemporal perception trace comprising object trajectories and temporal events, and then generates the final caption conditioned on the perceived evidence. To support this, we design a two-stage training strategy. Perceive-then-Describe Supervised Fine-tuning adapts the model from caption-only generation to the proposed perceive-describe chain, while Perception-Grounded Reinforcement Learning optimizes perception trace and caption quality with joint rewards over perception chain and the final caption. To support our two-stage training, we introduce Caption-Anchored Perception Data Construction. This pipeline builds the SFT and RL training data by first generating a caption-only description, extracting the objects and events it mentions, and grounding them back in the video with boxes and timestamps. This yields caption-aligned perception data that provides solid training ground truth, ensuring that the explicit perception trace and final caption refer to the same objects and events. Across direct caption and caption-to-QA evaluation, PercepCap consistently improves upon the Qwen3-VL baseline and demonstrates leading caption quality.

SoftReason: A Fully Differentiable Neuro-Soft-Symbolic Deductive Reasoning Architecture over High-Dimensional Perceptual Data

Wael AbdAlmageed

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how to perform deductive reasoning when premises are not given as discrete symbols but must be inferred from high-dimensional inputs like images, and when background knowledge is available from a knowledge graph. The main idea is SoftReason, a fully differentiable architecture that represents the deductive state as a 'soft interpretation tensor' over candidate constants and predicates, allowing perception (e.g., from a vision model) to propose probabilistic facts and the knowledge graph to provide high-confidence evidence, all while keeping the entire reasoning chain trainable via gradient descent. For astronomy, this could be transformative: one could build a system that takes a JWST image, detects sources (perception), queries a knowledge graph of known AGN or high-redshift galaxy properties, and then performs differentiable reasoning to classify the source or infer its redshift. A concrete workflow would be feeding a cutout of a 'little red dot' into a vision encoder, getting probabilistic facts like 'object is compact' and 'object has red color', then using a knowledge graph of AGN signatures to deduce 'object is likely an AGN' with end-to-end training. A limitation is that the paper instantiates the framework only on Knowledge-aware Visual Question Answering (KVQA), a dataset of natural images and questions, and scaling to astronomical images with their unique noise and resolution may require significant adaptation.

Reason

The paper presents a differentiable neuro-symbolic reasoning architecture that can handle high-dimensional perceptual data and knowledge graph evidence, which directly aligns with interests in automated scientific discovery and multimodal models for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the framework supports continuous or probabilistic predicates (e.g., 'redshift > 5') or only categorical ones, as many astronomical properties are continuous.
  • Look for details on how the knowledge graph is constructed and whether it can be dynamically updated with new astronomical discoveries.
  • See if the paper discusses computational scalability to large knowledge graphs or high-resolution images, as JWST data can be large.
  • Examine the training data requirements: does the method need a large set of (image, question, answer) triples, or can it leverage existing astronomical catalogs and knowledge bases?
Abstract

In many reasoning problems, the premises are not observed as discrete symbols, but must be inferred from high-dimensional inputs. Further, the predicate vocabulary, argument structure, and trusted evidence are supplied by a Knowledge Graph (KG), or rule definitions. Classical neuro-symbolic pipelines have a discrete interface between perception and deduction. We present a neuro-soft-symbolic architecture for differentiable deductive reasoning over latent perceptual facts and knowledge-provided predicates. SoftReason removes the gradient gap by representing the deductive state as a local soft interpretation tensor over candidate constants and predicates. Perception proposes probabilistic base facts, KG triples enter as high-confidence soft evidence, and every query anchor, predicate choice, and closure update remains differentiable. Our core innovation is a learned differentiable lift of the immediate-consequence operator. It uses predicate-definition embeddings and latent composition channels to form soft body-predicate mixtures, aggregate over all possible witnesses, propose query-conditioned head facts, and update the interpretation through a monotone probabilistic OR. We instantiate the framework on Knowledge-aware Visual Question Answering (KVQA), and demonstrates how SoftReason supports end-to-end perceptual grounding, KG evidence injection, and differentiable deductive closure in one trainable architecture.

Expert-Guided Forecast Editing for Time-Series Foundation Models

Hung Le , Minh Hoang Nguyen , Manh Nguyen , Huu Hiep Nguyen , Dai Do

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces DEFT, a framework for editing forecasts from frozen time-series foundation models using sparse expert feedback. It balances exploiting the model's predictive distribution with structured exploration in a trend-seasonal decomposition space. For astronomy, this could be applied to light curve forecasting for variable stars, AGN, or exoplanet transits, where an astronomer could provide expert feedback on a few candidate forecasts. An astronomer could imagine using DEFT to refine light curve predictions for a high-redshift supernova or to forecast the next outburst of a tidal disruption event. The main caveat is that the paper evaluates on general forecasting benchmarks and a molecular dynamics case study, not on astronomical light curves, so the method's performance on irregularly sampled, noisy astronomical data is unknown.

Reason

Time-series foundation models and expert-guided forecast editing are directly relevant to astronomical time-domain analysis, such as light curve forecasting for variable stars, AGN, or transients. The method could be applied to JWST time-series observations.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the trend-seasonal decomposition is appropriate for astronomical light curves with irregular sampling and measurement noise
  • Look for discussion of how the method handles missing data or non-periodic variability, which are common in astronomy
Abstract

Time-series foundation models can forecast across heterogeneous domains without task-specific training, but their forecasts are fixed once produced and cannot directly incorporate task-specific expert feedback. We study expert-guided forecast editing: a frozen foundation model generates candidate future trajectories, and an expensive expert evaluator scores them to guide forecast revision. Under a tight query budget, two natural strategies sit at opposite ends: best-of-$N$ purely exploits the foundation model's predictive distribution, while optimization approaches mostly explore the forecast horizon as an unstructured high-dimensional vector. Each extreme is individually sub-optimal. We introduce \textbf{DEFT}, an expert-guided forecast editing framework that balances the two by first exploiting the foundation model's predictive samples in a decomposed trend--seasonal space, then exploring around them via component-wise refinement. DEFT queries the expert only on complete trajectories, then reuses scores for the trend and seasonal components that appeared in the queried recombinations. This lets each expert query provide structured component-level feedback while keeping the foundation model frozen. We compare DEFT against direct search approaches, including best-of-$N$, cross-entropy methods, and Bayesian optimization, under matched expert-query budgets. Across two forecasting benchmarks consisting of 78 datasets, three time-series foundation models, four feedback types, and seven query budgets, DEFT consistently improves the effectiveness of expert guidance. A molecular-dynamics case study further suggests that the same principle extends to more physically grounded feedback, supporting the hypothesis that sparse test-time guidance should be spent balancing prior exploitation with structured exploration.

Reference-Free Evaluation of Reasoning in Open-Ended Question Answering

Guneet Singh Kohli , Yuxiang Zhou , Michael Sejr Schlichtkrull , Gregory E Dean , Maria Liakata

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper proposes a reference-free framework for auditing LLM reasoning traces by decomposing them into segments, labeling premise-target relations using Natural Language Inference, and organizing these into a hypergraph for backward AND-OR search. It is evaluated on mathematical reasoning and a new medical reasoning benchmark. For astronomy, this could be used to verify the reasoning of LLM agents that analyze JWST spectra or generate scientific conclusions from multi-wavelength data. An astronomer could imagine using this to audit an LLM agent's step-by-step reasoning when classifying a little red dot or estimating a photometric redshift. The main caveat is that the framework is evaluated on deductive and medical reasoning, and its performance on astronomy-specific reasoning with domain knowledge (e.g., SED fitting, spectral line identification) is not tested.

Reason

Reference-free evaluation of reasoning in LLM outputs is directly relevant to building research agents for astronomy that need to verify their own reasoning. The hypergraph-based audit framework could be applied to evaluating LLM-generated scientific analyses.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the NLI component can handle domain-specific astronomical terminology and concepts
  • Look for discussion of how the hypergraph audit handles probabilistic or uncertain reasoning, which is common in astronomy
Abstract

AI-generated answers in high-stakes domains are often fluent but difficult to verify, especially when they contain multi-step reasoning rather than a single final answer. We propose a reasoning-based, reference-free framework for auditing LLM-generated outputs. The method decomposes a generated reasoning trace into segments, labels local premise-target relations using Natural Language Inference (NLI), and organizes these relations into a hypergraph. A deterministic backward AND-OR search then assigns segment-level audit labels that indicate how each segment is grounded within the generated response. We evaluate the framework in two settings: deductive mathematical reasoning with Hard2Verify, and open-ended medical reasoning with UroReason, a new physician-annotated benchmark of LLM reasoning traces from real clinical cases. Across these settings, our NLI-hypergraph audit provides a more reliable reference-free evaluation signal than direct LLM-as-judge baselines. In the clinical setting, state-of-the-art LLM judges often fail to identify problematic reasoning segments, over-accepting fluent but weakly grounded responses. Our results show that QA evaluation should account for how inferential relations compose across a reasoning trace, rather than relying only on final answers or LLMs as verifiers. UroReason will be made available through an API, and our code will be released as open source.
