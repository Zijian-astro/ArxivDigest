## 1. RAGA: Reading-And-Graph-building-Agent for Autonomous Knowledge Graph Construction and Retrieval-Augmented Generation

- arXiv ID: `2605.17072`
- Authors: Chengrui Han , Zesheng Cheng
- Score: 9
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17072
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17072
- Subjects: Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI) ; Computation and Language (cs.CL)
- Reason: Directly relevant to retrieval-augmented generation and knowledge graph construction for scientific literature, which can be applied to astronomy research.

Short digest:
This paper proposes RAGA, an LLM-based autonomous framework for knowledge graph construction and retrieval-augmented generation. It provides an atomic toolset for full KG lifecycle operations and embeds a Read-Search-Verify-Construct cognitive constraint into a ReAct tool loop. A KG-vector synchronization mechanism enables hybrid symbolic-vector retrieval with evidence-anchored verification. For astronomy, this could automate building knowledge graphs from JWST papers and enable RAG systems for literature review. The framework is tested on the QASPER scientific QA dataset. A caveat is that preliminary experiments are on a subset of data, and scaling to full astronomical literature needs validation.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check the quality of entity disambiguation and cross-chunk semantic relation capture in the KG construction.
- Look for the evidence-anchored verification mechanism and how it ensures provenance.
- Examine the hybrid retrieval performance on scientific QA tasks.

## 2. ConflictRAG: Detecting and Resolving Knowledge Conflicts in Retrieval Augmented Generation

- arXiv ID: `2605.17301`
- Authors: Chenyu Wang , Yingmin Liu , Yang Shu
- Score: 9
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17301
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17301
- Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL) ; Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)
- Reason: Directly relevant: conflict-aware RAG framework for detecting and resolving knowledge conflicts, crucial for reliable retrieval-augmented generation in astronomy literature review and data analysis.

Short digest:
This paper addresses the problem that RAG systems assume retrieved documents are mutually consistent, which often fails in practice. It proposes ConflictRAG, a framework that detects, classifies, and resolves knowledge conflicts before answer generation, using a two-stage detection (embedding MLP + selective LLM), an Entropy-TOPSIS source credibility assessment, and a Conflict-Aware RAG Score (CARS) for diagnostics. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because astronomical literature and databases often contain conflicting measurements (e.g., different redshifts for the same object, varying flux calibrations). An astronomer could use ConflictRAG to build a literature review agent that identifies and resolves contradictions in published results, or a data analysis agent that cross-checks multiple catalogs. A key caveat is that the framework is evaluated on general benchmarks, not on scientific or astronomical data; the source credibility assessment may need adaptation to scientific contexts (e.g., journal reputation, telescope reliability).

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Look for the two-stage conflict detection: what is the MLP classifier trained on, and how is the LLM refinement triggered?
- Check the Entropy-TOPSIS framework: how are source credibility features defined, and could they be adapted to astronomical data sources (e.g., JWST vs. ground-based)?
- Examine the CARS metric: does it capture conflict-handling capabilities relevant to scientific workflows?
- Look for any experiments on domain-specific datasets (e.g., scientific literature, databases).

## 3. Vector RAG vs LLM-Compiled Wiki: A Preregistered Comparison on a Small Multi-Domain Research

- arXiv ID: `2605.18490`
- Authors: Theodore O. Cochran
- Score: 9
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.18490
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.18490
- Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL) ; Information Retrieval (cs.IR)
- Reason: Directly compares RAG and LLM-compiled wiki for research synthesis, which is relevant to automated literature review and scientific discovery.

Short digest:
This paper compares Vector RAG and an LLM-compiled markdown wiki for answering questions over a small research corpus (24 papers). The wiki was better at connecting findings across papers, while RAG was better for single-fact lookup. Claim-level citation checking favored the wiki, but a decomposition-based RAG variant recovered most of the wiki's advantage at lower cost. This is directly relevant to building automated literature review tools for astronomy. For example, one could use such a system to synthesize results across JWST papers on high-redshift galaxies. The paper highlights that no single architecture is best on all metrics (organization, citation support, cost). The findings are based on a small corpus (24 papers), so scalability to larger astronomy literature needs testing.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check the decomposition-based RAG variant's implementation details for potential adaptation.
- Look for the specific LLM judge prompts used for scoring, to assess bias.
- See if the corpus size and domain (research papers) are similar to a typical astronomy literature review.
- Check the cost analysis: token usage for wiki vs. RAG, and whether the upfront build cost is justified.

## 4. MLReplicate: Benchmarking Autonomous Research Systems for Machine Learning Reproducibility

- arXiv ID: `2605.16616`
- Authors: Sasi Kiran Gaddipati , Diyana Muhammed , Farhana Keya , Gollam Rabby , Sören Auer
- Score: 8
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16616
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16616
- Subjects: Machine Learning (cs.LG)
- Reason: Directly benchmarks autonomous research systems for ML reproducibility, highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents for astronomy.

Short digest:
This paper asks whether current autonomous research systems can reliably reproduce machine learning papers. It introduces MLReplicate, a benchmark built from ICML 2025 outstanding papers, and evaluates 6 systems (e.g., AI Scientist, Agent Laboratory) on manuscript generation. Key findings: automated reviews accepted 10/37 submissions, but human reviewers found methodological flaws and hallucinated results in 59% of accepted automated reviews; computational cost did not predict quality. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because similar autonomous agents could be used to reproduce or extend astrophysical analyses, e.g., reproducing JWST data reduction pipelines or SED fitting results. A concrete workflow: use an agent to re-run a published galaxy classification pipeline on new JWST data and generate a reproducible report. Caveat: the benchmark is limited to ML papers, and the systems may not yet handle domain-specific astronomy software (e.g., CASA, astropy) or large astronomical datasets.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check whether any evaluated system can handle domain-specific scientific software (e.g., astronomy libraries) or only generic ML frameworks.
- Look for details on how human reviewers detected hallucinated results—this informs how to audit agent outputs in astronomy.
- Examine the input specification format to see if it could be adapted to astronomy workflows (e.g., specifying a data reduction + analysis pipeline).

## 5. In-context learning enables continental-scale subsurface temperature prediction from sparse local observations

- arXiv ID: `2605.16665`
- Authors: Daniel O'Malley , Christopher W. Johnson , Javier E. Santos , Pablo Lara , Sandro Malusà , Bharat Srikishan , John Kath , Arnab Mazumder , Mohamed Mehana , David Coblentz , Nathan DeBardeleben , Earl Lawrence , Hari Viswanathan
- Score: 8
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16665
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16665
- Subjects: Machine Learning (cs.LG) ; Geophysics (physics.geo-ph)
- Reason: Directly applies in-context learning with transformers to a geoscience problem (subsurface temperature prediction), demonstrating a method that could transfer to astronomical regression tasks with sparse observations.

Short digest:
This paper asks whether a transformer model can predict subsurface temperature at continental scale from sparse borehole measurements, without retraining for new regions. It introduces In-Context Earth, a transformer that uses sparse local borehole observations as 'in-context examples' to predict temperature-at-depth fields with calibrated uncertainty. The model achieves 4.7 °C MAE in the contiguous US, outperforming physics-informed and other baselines, and adapts to new regions (Alberta, Australia, UK) with only 20 local observations at inference time. For astronomy, this is highly relevant: the same in-context learning approach could be applied to predict photometric redshifts, stellar parameters, or galaxy properties from sparse spectroscopic calibration data, without per-survey retraining. A concrete workflow would be using a few hundred spectroscopic redshifts as in-context examples to predict redshifts for millions of galaxies in a new survey. However, the paper tests on a 1D regression task (temperature vs. depth), not on high-dimensional astronomical data (e.g., multi-band photometry, spectra), and the uncertainty calibration method may need adaptation.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Look for details on how the in-context examples are formatted and fed to the transformer (e.g., as a sequence of (x, y) pairs).
- Check whether the uncertainty calibration method (Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic) is described and could be applied to astronomical regression.
- Examine the model architecture: is it a standard causal transformer, and how does it handle variable-length context?
- Look for any discussion of scaling to higher-dimensional inputs (e.g., multi-band photometry instead of 1D depth).

- Authors: Haofei Yu , Jiaxuan You , Peter Clark , Bodhisattwa Prasad Majumder , Kyle Richardson
- Score: 8
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.16902
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16902
- Subjects: Machine Learning (cs.LG)
- Reason: Directly addresses automatic discovery of state-of-the-art models using graph neural networks and LLM agents, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents.

Short digest:
This paper asks how to automatically discover the state-of-the-art model for a given dataset by leveraging scientific artifacts on platforms like HuggingFace. The main idea is a two-stage framework: first, use graph neural networks or graph-augmented LLMs to rank unobserved model-dataset links, then use LLM-based coding agents to verify top-ranked links. This matters for astronomy because it could automate the process of finding the best model for a specific astronomical dataset or task, such as source classification or redshift estimation. A concrete workflow would be using ArtifactLinker to automatically identify the best pre-trained model for JWST image segmentation or spectral fitting. A limitation is that the benchmark focuses on HuggingFace artifacts, which may not yet include many astronomy-specific models.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check the benchmark composition: what fraction of artifacts are from scientific domains vs. general computer vision/NLP?
- Look for the success rate of the LLM-based verification agents on scientific tasks.
- Examine whether the graph structure captures meaningful relationships between astronomical datasets and models.

- Reason: Directly evaluates AI coding agents on ML tasks including tabular prediction and time-series, which could transfer to astronomical data analysis and automated scientific discovery.

Short digest:
This paper introduces 1GC-7RC, a benchmark for evaluating AI coding agents on seven ML tasks including language modeling, image classification, semantic segmentation, graph learning, tabular prediction, time-series forecasting, and text classification. Agents must design, implement, and train models from scratch on a single GPU with no pretrained weights or internet access. The benchmark tests implicit ML knowledge, planning, and time-budget management. For astronomy, this could help evaluate agents that automate data analysis pipelines for JWST or survey data. The modular design allows adding new tasks like astronomical source classification or SED fitting. A caveat is that the benchmark currently lacks astronomy-specific tasks, but the framework is extensible.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check if the benchmark tasks (tabular prediction, time-series) are similar to astronomical regression/classification problems.
- Look for the modular extension mechanism and whether new tasks can be added easily.

## 8. State-of-the-Art Claims Require State-of-the-Art Evidence

- arXiv ID: `2605.17273`
- Authors: YongKyung Oh
- Score: 8
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17273
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17273
- Subjects: Machine Learning (cs.LG) ; Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)
- Reason: Critically examines SOTA claims in AI/ML benchmarks, which is directly relevant to evaluating models for astronomical tasks.

Short digest:
This paper identifies a widespread claim-evidence gap in AI benchmarking, showing that SOTA claims often rest on fragile evidence like marginal mean score improvements. The main finding is that in over half of top-model comparisons, at least one assumed property of superiority (e.g., consistency, robustness) does not hold. For astronomy, this is crucial when evaluating models for source classification or photometric redshifts, as benchmark performance may not generalize. One could use this framework to critically assess claims about new models for JWST data analysis. However, the paper is meta-analytical and does not propose new methods. Prerequisites include familiarity with benchmark evaluation practices.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check if the paper provides specific examples of fragile SOTA claims in domains relevant to astronomy.
- Look for the proposed guidelines for more honest reporting of results.
