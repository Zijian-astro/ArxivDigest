Title: Multimodal-Multiresolution Foundation Model for Lunar Remote Sensing

Barker , Andrew Annex , Vishnu Viswanathan , Zachary Morse , Ethan I. Schaefer , Nikolaos Dionelis , Ankur Kumar , Campbell D. Watson , Manil Maskey , Rebekah I. Score: 9
Reason: This is a multimodal, multiresolution foundation model for lunar remote sensing, directly relevant to my interest in foundation models for astronomical survey data, multimodal fusion, and transfer to JWST/Roman/Euclid-style imaging.
Short digest: The paper presents a multimodal foundation model for lunar remote sensing, pretrained from scratch on SomBench, a corpus of nearly two million co-registered tile bundles spanning 11 modalities at two spatial scales (1 m/pixel and 100 m/pixel). The main AI idea is to adapt the TerraMind masked-token architecture with two lunar-specific extensions: acquisition geometry is provided as explicit context, and meter- and hundred-meter-scale tiles are trained jointly so one set of weights covers both resolutions. FlexiViT patch embeddings allow adaptation to different patch sizes without retraining, and modality-wise inputs enable flexible multimodal fine-tuning. This matters for astronomy because it is a concrete example of pretraining a foundation model on multi-instrument, multi-resolution remote sensing data, which is exactly the kind of approach that could be adapted to JWST, Roman, Euclid, or Rubin multi-band imaging. A concrete workflow could be pretraining a similar masked-token model on co-registered JWST NIRCam and MIRI tiles, or on multi-survey optical-to-infrared cutouts, then fine-tuning for source classification, anomaly detection, or photometric redshift estimation. The caveat is that the domain is lunar remote sensing, so the modality set and benchmark tasks differ from astrophysics, and I should check whether the pretraining corpus and compute scale are reproducible for astronomical data volumes.
Full-text inspection checklist: Check the SomBench corpus construction, especially how co-registered tile bundles across 11 modalities and two resolutions are assembled; Look for the TerraMind masked-token architecture details and how acquisition geometry is injected as explicit context; Examine the LoRA vs full fine-tuning comparison and whether the label-efficiency gains are robust across tasks; Check whether the released checkpoint, benchmark datasets, and fine-tuning code could be adapted to astronomical multi-band imaging

Title: Adapting Open-Weight MLLMs to Generate Point Prompts for Electron Microscopy Segmentation
Authors: Samia Mohinta , Albert Cardona
arXiv ID: 2609.14080
Score: 9
Reason: This paper adapts open-weight multimodal LLMs to generate point prompts for electron microscopy segmentation, which is highly relevant to multimodal models combining images and language for segmentation, with clear transfer to JWST source segmentation and promptable segmentation workflows.
Short digest: The paper asks whether open-weight multimodal large language models can generate point prompts for a frozen promptable segmenter from natural-language requests, removing the need for manual clicks. The main idea is to convert masks from mitochondria datasets into training examples that pair images and instructions with centroid coordinates, then train LoRA adapters while freezing both the MLLM backbone and the microSAM segmenter. In plain terms, the language model learns to point at the right object in an image, and a separate segmentation model turns that point into a mask. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because promptable segmentation models like SAM are being used for JWST source extraction, and an MLLM that generates prompts from text could enable language-directed segmentation of galaxies, AGN, or artifacts. A concrete workflow would be to fine-tune an open-weight MLLM to output point prompts for JWST cutouts, then feed those points to a frozen astronomical segmenter for source masks. The caveat is that the paper uses electron microscopy data, so I should check whether the approach transfers to low-surface-brightness, blended, or multi-wavelength astronomical images.
Full-text inspection checklist: Check whether the LoRA fine-tuning recipe and data conversion pipeline are described in enough detail to reproduce; Look for robustness tests under unseen natural-language formulations and whether they resemble astronomical queries; Check whether the method handles blended or overlapping sources, which is critical for JWST deblending; Verify whether the paper compares against supervised point detectors and discusses failure modes

Title: Operational Range Bounding in Spectroscopy: A Safety Cage Framework for Machine Learning Models
Authors: Nikki Grens , Luís F. Simões , Kai Hou Yip , Theresa Lueftinger
arXiv ID: 2609.13514
Score: 8
Reason: This paper directly addresses reliable ML for spectroscopy under domain shift, with a safety-cage framework for exoplanet transmission spectra, which is highly relevant to JWST spectral fitting and uncertainty estimation.
Short digest: The paper tackles the problem of trusting black-box ML models in safety-critical space missions when ground truth is unavailable. It proposes a modular safety cage that runs in parallel to a prediction model and monitors uncertainty quantification, out-of-domain detection, and influence functions to constrain the model to a verified operational domain. The key result is that no single indicator catches all failure modes, but fusing indicators and rejecting about 20 percent of data reduces errors by 45 to 65 percent across domains. For astronomy, this is directly applicable to JWST and Ariel spectral retrieval, where instrument anomalies, stellar activity, and domain shifts can silently corrupt ML predictions. A concrete workflow would be wrapping a spectral fitting or transmission-spectrum extraction model with a safety cage that flags out-of-domain or high-influence predictions before they enter a science pipeline. The caveat is that the framework requires a validated in-domain reference and careful tuning of rejection thresholds, which may be costly for small datasets.
Full-text inspection checklist: Look for the specific uncertainty quantification and out-of-domain detection methods used and whether they are model-agnostic; Check the coverage-risk curves and how the 20 percent rejection translates to different scientific metrics; Look for whether the framework handles spectral data with noise, systematics, or missing wavelength coverage; Check whether the paper provides guidance on choosing indicator combinations for new instruments
