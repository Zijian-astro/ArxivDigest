## 2. LLMs as Acquisition Policies for Finite-Pool Materials Optimization: A Controlled Study

- Authors: Dino-Rober Demir , Florian Le Bronnec , Rio Yokota
- Score: 8
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19790
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19790
- Subjects: Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)
- Reason: Directly evaluates LLMs as acquisition policies for materials optimization, which is analogous to using LLMs for active learning in astronomy.

Short digest:
The paper tests whether LLMs can serve as acquisition policies in active learning for materials discovery, comparing to Gaussian processes. The main idea is to use LLMs to select next candidates based on prior observations. This is highly relevant for astronomy, where active learning could optimize follow-up observations or parameter searches, and the paper provides a controlled study of LLM reliability.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check if the method could be adapted to astronomical candidate selection (e.g., for spectroscopy follow-up).
- Look for details on how scientific context is presented to the LLM.

## 3. Far from the Crowd: Scalable Self-Supervised Learning via Geographic Isolation

- arXiv ID: `2608.19766`
- Authors: Daniele Rege Cambrin , Francesco Rossi , Mattia Varile
- Score: 7
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19766
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19766
- Subjects: Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (cs.CV) ; Machine Learning (cs.LG)
- Reason: Self-supervised learning on remote sensing with geographic isolation, directly transferable to astronomical surveys with spatial metadata.

Short digest:
The paper proposes a curriculum learning strategy for self-supervised pretraining on remote sensing imagery, ranking samples by geographic isolation. The main idea is to use geolocation metadata to prioritize diverse samples, improving efficiency and performance. This is relevant for astronomy, as surveys like JWST or Rubin have spatial metadata, and the method could be adapted to pretrain models on astronomical images.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check if the method is tested on multi-band or multi-wavelength data.
- Look for details on how geographic isolation is computed and whether it could be replaced by sky position.

## 4. Bringing analytic rigor to agentic AI for science: The Brain Researcher platform for neuroimaging data analysis

Mumford , Steven Dillmann , James Kent , Alejandro de la Vega , Sanmi Koyejo , Vince D. Calhoun , Joshua W. Buckholtz , Juan Helen Zhou , Steffen Bollmann , Russell A. Poldrack
- Score: 7
- Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19902
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19902
- Subjects: Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI) ; Neurons and Cognition (q-bio.NC)
- Reason: This paper presents an agentic AI harness for scientific data analysis with rigorous methodology, directly relevant to my interest in research agents for scientific workflows, though applied to neuroimaging rather than astronomy.

Short digest:
The paper addresses the problem of AI agents producing defensible scientific claims by introducing a harness that enforces admissible analyses and claim scoping. It uses LLM agents with rules and multiverse analyses to improve tool selection and grounding. This matters for astronomy because it provides a template for building rigorous AI agents for data analysis, which could be adapted to JWST or survey data. I could imagine using this framework to create an agent that performs SED fitting or spectral analysis with proper uncertainty and alternative checks. A key caveat is that the framework is tailored to neuroimaging, so I need to assess how easily its rules and checks transfer to astronomical data formats and analysis pipelines.

Full-text inspection checklist:
- Check how the rules for admissible analyses are defined and whether they are domain-agnostic or need heavy customization for astronomy.
- Look for details on the multiverse analysis implementation and whether it can handle astronomical data types like spectra or images.
- Assess the integration with existing scientific Python ecosystems (e.g., astropy) and whether the harness can run on typical astronomical computing environments.
