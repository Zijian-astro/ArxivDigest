Trust or Abstain? A Self-Aware RAG Approach

Xi Zhu , Ziqi Wang , Kai Mei , Wujiang Xu , Minghao Guo , Bangji Yang , Jiajun Fan , Dimitris N. Metaxas

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of knowledge conflicts in RAG, where retrieved evidence and the LLM's internal knowledge disagree or are both unreliable, which is a key challenge for scientific question answering. The authors introduce SABER, a self-aware belief estimator that requires no LLM fine-tuning, combining a self-prior with conditional reasoning representations from multi-trace inference to decide whether to trust parametric knowledge, retrieved knowledge, either, or abstain. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because an LLM agent analyzing JWST data might retrieve conflicting information from the literature (e.g., different redshift estimates for the same source) and needs a principled way to decide what to trust. A concrete workflow would be using SABER in a research agent that queries ADS for papers about a specific high-redshift galaxy and must reconcile conflicting claims about its properties. A caveat is that SABER requires constructing a knowledge-conflict benchmark specific to the LLM backbone, which may be non-trivial for astronomy-specific models.

Reason

Directly addresses knowledge conflicts in RAG, which is critical for building reliable LLM agents that combine parametric knowledge (e.g., from training) with retrieved evidence (e.g., from papers or databases) in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the 4-cell decision (trust PK, trust CK, trust either, abstain) is implemented and whether it can be adapted to astronomy-specific knowledge sources.
  • Look for the construction of the knowledge-conflict benchmark and assess whether a similar benchmark could be built from astronomical datasets (e.g., conflicting redshift measurements).
  • Examine the risk-coverage curve and abstention mechanism to see if it can be tuned for high-stakes scientific decisions where false positives are costly.
Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves large language models (LLMs) by incorporating external evidence, but it also introduces knowledge conflicts when retrieved contextual knowledge (CK) and parametric knowledge (PK) disagree or are both unreliable. Existing approaches mainly coordinate which source to use, without explicitly asking whether each answer path is correct. We argue that faithful RAG requires LLM self-awareness, namely the ability to recognize the limits of its own knowledge and reasoning. To ground this problem, we construct a model-specific, ground-truth-aligned knowledge-conflict benchmark by evaluating LLM backbones on PK-only and CK-conditioned answer paths over approximately 69K query-context instances per backbone, drawn from five conflict-QA datasets. We then introduce SABER, a Self-Aware Belief Estimator for RAG that requires no LLM fine-tuning. SABER combines a self-prior with PK-side and CK-side conditional reasoning representations from multi-trace inference, then estimates reliability beliefs with two lightweight predictors to drive a 4-cell decision over trust PK, trust CK, trust either, or abstain. Across four LLM backbones, SABER improves end-to-end accuracy and conflict-specific faithfulness over ten inference-time and fine-tuning baselines, with the largest gains on conflict-heavy datasets. Under abstention, SABER's risk-coverage curve Pareto-dominates every prompt-based abstainer, providing a tunable balance between coverage and answer risk. Our code is available at this https URL .

How Far Are We From True Auto-Research?

Zhengxin Zhang , Ning Wang , Sainyam Galhotra , Claire Cardie

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks how close current AI agents are to producing truly acceptable research papers autonomously. The authors built ResearchArena, a minimal system that lets LLM agents (Claude, GPT, Kimi) complete the full research loop—ideation, experiments, writing, and revision—with only light guidance. They generated 117 papers across computer science topics and evaluated them with automated review, artifact-aware peer review, and human meta-review. While automated scores looked promising, manual inspection revealed severe flaws: fabricated results, underpowered experiments, and mismatches between plans and executions. None of the 117 papers met the acceptance bar for a top venue. For astronomy, this work is a cautionary tale: it shows that current agents can produce plausible-looking papers but fail on experimental rigor, a critical issue if we want to use agents for JWST data analysis or literature synthesis. Before adopting such agents, one should check whether the agent's outputs include verifiable evidence (e.g., actual code outputs, data files) and whether the agent can be audited for fabrication. The main caveat is that the study uses computer science seeds, not astronomy; the failure modes may differ in data-driven fields.

Reason

Directly evaluates LLM agents for automated research, a core interest. The paper's focus on agent-generated papers, experimental rigor, and failure modes (fabrication, underpowered experiments) is highly relevant to building trustworthy research agents for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the taxonomy of failure modes (fabricated results, underpowered experiments, plan/execution mismatch) and see if they map to astronomy workflows.
  • Look for the artifact-aware peer review methodology: how does the agent's workspace get inspected alongside the manuscript?
  • Examine the SAR (manuscript-only reviewer) vs. artifact-aware PR score gap to understand how much automated review overestimates quality.
Abstract

Recent auto-research systems can produce complete papers, but feasibility is not the same as quality, and the field still lacks a systematic study of how good agent-generated papers actually are. We introduce ResearchArena, a minimal scaffold that lets off-the-shelf agents (Claude Code using Opus 4.6, Codex using GPT-5.4, and Kimi Code using K2.5) carry out the full research loop themselves (ideation, experimentation, paper writing, self-refinement) under only lightweight guidance. Across 13 computer science seeds and 3 trials per agent-domain pair, ResearchArena yields 117 agent-generated papers, each evaluated under three complementary lenses: a manuscript-only reviewer (SAR), an artifact-aware peer review (PR) in which agents inspect the workspace alongside the manuscript, and an human conducted meta-review. Under SAR alone the picture is optimistic: Claude Code obtains the highest score, outperforms Analemma's FARS, and matches the weighted-average human ICLR 2025 submission, suggesting that minimally scaffolded agents can produce papers that look competitive on manuscript-only review. Manual inspection, however, reveals this picture is overstated: SAR scores are poorly aligned with its actual acceptance decisions and reward plausible framing without verifying experimental substance. Under artifact-aware PR scores drop sharply, and manual auditing identifies experimental rigor as the major bottleneck, decomposing into three failure modes (fabricated results, underpowered experiments, and plan/execution mismatch) that are highly agent-dependent: Codex 5%/8% paper-vs-artifact mismatch / fabricated references versus Kimi Code 77%/72%, a $\sim$15$\times$ spread that tracks distinct research personas the agents develop. None of the 117 agent-generated papers reaches the acceptance bar of a top-tier venue. This suggests that we are still gapped from the true auto-research.

Query-Aware Flow Diffusion for Graph-Based RAG with Retrieval Guarantees

Zhuoping Zhou , Davoud Ataee Tarzanagh , Sima Didari , Wenjun Hu , Baruch Gutow , Oxana Verkholyak , Masoud Faraki , Heng Hao , Hankyu Moon , Seungjai Min

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of retrieving relevant subgraphs from a knowledge graph for question answering, where existing methods use heuristics or static exploration that ignore the query's meaning. The authors propose QAFD-RAG, a training-free method that dynamically weights graph edges by how well they align with the query's semantic embedding, guiding retrieval along relevant paths. This approach provides the first statistical guarantees for query-aware graph retrieval, converging exponentially fast with complexity scaling only with the subgraph size. For astronomy, this could enable an LLM agent to traverse a graph of papers, objects, and observations to answer complex multi-hop questions like 'Which JWST NIRSpec observations of little red dots have published SED fits?' A concrete workflow would be building a knowledge graph from ADS abstracts, JWST data products, and catalogs, then using QAFD-RAG to retrieve relevant subgraphs for a research agent. A caveat is that the method assumes a pre-existing graph with meaningful edge semantics, and building such a graph for astronomy is a significant upfront investment.

Reason

Directly addresses graph-based RAG, a method that could be applied to astronomical knowledge graphs for multi-hop reasoning over literature or multi-wavelength catalogs.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the paper provides an explicit algorithm for query-aware edge weighting that could be implemented on a custom graph.
  • Look for the signal-to-noise conditions required for the retrieval guarantee and assess whether astronomical data would meet them.
  • Examine the question-answering experiments to see if the method handles multi-hop queries that span different data types (e.g., images, spectra, catalogs).
Abstract

Graph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems leverage interconnected knowledge structures to capture complex relationships that flat retrieval struggles with, enabling multi-hop reasoning. Yet most existing graph-based methods suffer from (i) heuristic designs lacking theoretical guarantees for subgraph quality or relevance and/or (ii) the use of static exploration strategies that ignore the query's holistic meaning, retrieving neighborhoods or communities regardless of intent. We propose Query-Aware Flow Diffusion RAG (QAFD-RAG), a training-free framework that dynamically adapts graph traversal to each query's holistic semantics. The central innovation is query-aware traversal: during graph exploration, edges are dynamically weighted by how well their endpoints align with the query's embedding, guiding flow along semantically relevant paths while avoiding structurally connected but irrelevant regions. These query-specific reasoning subgraphs enable the first statistical guarantees for query-aware graph retrieval, showing that QAFD-RAG recovers relevant subgraphs with high probability under mild signal-to-noise conditions. The algorithm converges exponentially fast, with complexity scaling with the retrieved subgraph size rather than the full graph. Experiments on question answering and text-to-SQL tasks demonstrate consistent improvements over state-of-the-art graph-based RAG methods.

ReCrit: Transition-Aware Reinforcement Learning for Scientific Critic Reasoning

Wanghan Xu , Yuhao Zhou , Hengyuan Zhao , Shuo Li , Dianzhi Yu , Zhenfei Yin , Yaowen Hu , Fengli Xu , Wanli Ouyang , Wenlong Zhang , Lei Bai

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of LLMs abandoning correct scientific solutions after user criticism, which is especially risky in scientific reasoning. The authors propose ReCrit, a transition-aware reinforcement learning framework that decomposes Initial-to-Critic behavior into four quadrants (Correction, Sycophancy, Robustness, Boundary) and rewards correction and robustness while penalizing sycophancy. For astronomy, this is highly relevant for building research agents that interact with astronomers: an agent should accept valid corrections but resist incorrect criticism (e.g., a user claiming a redshift is wrong when it is correct). A concrete workflow would be an agent that presents a SED fit result and must decide whether to update its answer based on user feedback. A caveat is that the method requires training with a reward model that can distinguish correction from sycophancy, which may be difficult to construct for astronomy-specific tasks.

Reason

Directly addresses LLM critic interaction in scientific reasoning, which is crucial for building reliable research agents that can handle user feedback and correct mistakes without abandoning correct answers.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the four quadrants are defined and whether the reward model is task-specific or general.
  • Look for the dynamic asynchronous rollout mechanism and assess whether it can be applied to long scientific reasoning chains.
  • Examine the benchmarks (ChemBench, TRQA, EarthSE) to see if they include tasks similar to astronomy (e.g., interpreting spectra or images).
Abstract

Large language models can fail in critic interaction not only by answering incorrectly, but also by abandoning an initially correct scientific solution after user criticism. This is especially risky in scientific reasoning, where user criticism can turn a valid answer into an incorrect one. We frame critic interaction as an inter-turn correctness-transition problem rather than a final-answer accuracy problem, and identify three challenges: transition awareness, decoupling useful correction from harmful sycophancy, and scalable rollout. We propose ReCrit, a transition-aware reinforcement learning framework that decomposes Initial-to-Critic behavior into four quadrants: Correction, Sycophancy, Robustness, and Boundary. ReCrit rewards correction and robustness, penalizes sycophancy, and treats persistent errors as weak boundary signals. To make interaction training practical, ReCrit further uses dynamic asynchronous rollout with tail-adaptive completion to reduce rollout waiting. On three scientific reasoning benchmarks, ChemBench, TRQA, and EarthSE, ReCrit improves average Critic accuracy from 38.15 to 51.49 on Qwen3.5-4B and from 45.40 to 55.59 on Qwen3.5-9B. Ablations show that final-answer rewards provide little interaction-level gain, while transition-aware rewards and quadrant weighting produce more distinguishable training signals and larger net Critic-stage improvement. The code is available at this https URL .

Evaluating Memory Condensation Strategies for Coding Agents in Data-Driven Scientific Discovery

Renuka Chintalapati , Sid Raskar , Anurag Acharya , Jared Willard , Patrick Emami , Sameera Horawalavithana

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper systematically evaluates eight memory condensation strategies for coding agents on 60 scientific discovery tasks across six domains. It finds that no condenser significantly improves hypothesis quality, while LLM-based condensers increase token costs. Masking tool-call outputs achieves modest savings. The optimal strategy varies by domain and task length. For astronomy, this is directly relevant for designing coding agents that can perform long-running data analysis tasks (e.g., fitting SEDs, reducing JWST images). The main caveat is that the tasks are from DiscoveryBench, not astronomy-specific, so transferability needs verification.

Reason

Directly evaluates memory condensation strategies for coding agents in scientific discovery, highly relevant for building automated research agents for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the evaluation tasks include any astronomy or astrophysics problems.
  • Look for details on how memory condensation strategies handle domain-specific context (e.g., FITS files, catalogs).
  • Evaluate whether the findings generalize to astronomy-specific coding tasks (e.g., using astropy, JWST pipelines).
Abstract

Coding agents accumulate extensive context during long-running tasks, yet fixed context windows force practitioners to choose between truncation and task failure. While numerous memory condensation strategies have been proposed, from simple sliding windows to LLM-generated summaries, no systematic comparison exists to guide strategy selection, especially in scientific discovery tasks. We evaluate eight memory condensation strategies using GPT-4o on sixty DiscoveryBench tasks spanning six scientific domains (480 total evaluations). We find that no condenser significantly alters hypothesis quality, while LLM-based condensers increase token costs by 24-94 percent, and masking tool-call outputs achieves an 8.6 percent net savings. We also observe that the optimal condenser for data-driven scientific discovery varies by scientific domain and task length.

StruMPL: Multi-task Dense Regression under Disjoint Partial Supervision and MNAR Labels

Reza M. Asiyabi , Juan Alberto Molina-Valero , The SEOSAW Partnership , Steven Hancock , Casey M. Ryan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of estimating forest aboveground biomass from Earth observation data, where two label sources are structurally incompatible: spaceborne lidar provides canopy structure but no biomass, and ground plots provide biomass but are biased and sparse. The authors formalize this as multi-task dense regression under disjoint partial supervision with MNAR labels and physical constraints. They propose StruMPL, which uses a shared encoder with per-variable regression, imputation, and propensity heads for spatial MNAR correction, plus a learnable physics module enforcing inter-task constraints. The supervised loss uses Augmented IPW (AIPW) with stop-gradients, reducing high-AGB bias by ~54%. For astronomy, this directly maps to photometric redshift or SED fitting: we have spectroscopic redshifts (like ground plots) at biased locations, and photometric data (like lidar) everywhere. A concrete workflow would be jointly estimating redshift, stellar mass, and star formation rate from multi-band photometry, using sparse spectroscopy as partial labels. A key caveat is the method is validated on forest biomass, not astronomical data, and the physical constraints are allometric (biome-specific), which would need replacement with astrophysical models.

Reason

Directly addresses multi-task regression under partial supervision with missing-not-at-random labels, which is highly relevant to astronomical SED fitting and photometric redshift estimation where labels (spectra) are sparse and biased.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the AIPW pseudo-outcome formulation is described with enough detail to reimplement
  • Look for any discussion of how the physical constraint module is designed and whether it is learnable or fixed
  • Assess whether the method scales to high-dimensional outputs (e.g., full SEDs) or only scalar targets
  • Look for ablation studies showing the contribution of the propensity and imputation heads separately
Abstract

Estimating forest aboveground biomass (AGB) from Earth observation combines two structurally incompatible label sources: spaceborne lidar provides canopy structure at millions of locations but no biomass estimate, and ground-based plots provide biomass at thousands of biased locations but no metrics of structure. No single training sample carries labels for all target variables, plot labels are missing not at random (MNAR), and biomass is linked to the structural variables by known but biome-specific allometric laws. We formalise this as multi-task dense regression under heterogeneous disjoint partial supervision with MNAR labels and inter-task physical constraints, and propose StruMPL to address it jointly. A shared encoder feeds per-variable regression, imputation, and propensity heads for spatial MNAR correction, and a learnable physics module that evaluates the inter-task constraint on the model's own predictions at every pixel. The supervised loss uses an Augmented IPW (AIPW) pseudo-outcome with stop-gradients on the propensity and on the imputation baseline; we show analytically and empirically that both are necessary for joint optimisation to recover IPW-weighted stationary points while keeping the loss bounded. On two ecologically distinct biomes, StruMPL outperforms ablation variants and the closest published method on AGB RMSE and bias, with a stratified analysis showing AIPW reduces high-AGB bias by ~54%.

PEEK: Context Map as an Orientation Cache for Long-Context LLM Agents

Zhuohan Gu , Qizheng Zhang , Omar Khattab , Samuel Madden

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

1. The paper asks how LLM agents can efficiently reuse knowledge about a recurring external context (like a document corpus or codebase) across multiple invocations. 2. It introduces PEEK, a system that maintains a small, constant-sized 'context map' in the agent's prompt, which is updated by a programmable cache policy that distills, structures, and evicts information to keep the agent oriented. 3. For astronomy, this could enable an agent that repeatedly works with a large JWST data archive or a set of galaxy catalogs to avoid re-reading the same metadata and instead maintain a persistent, evolving summary of what is where. 4. A concrete workflow would be an LLM agent that performs iterative SED fitting or source classification across many targets from a large survey, using PEEK to remember which filters, templates, or prior results were useful. 5. The main caveat is that PEEK is designed for 'recurring same-context workloads' and may not help for one-shot queries; also, the paper tests on coding and reasoning benchmarks, not scientific data, so the transfer to astronomy-specific contexts would need validation.

Reason

PEEK directly addresses the problem of LLM agents operating over long and recurring external contexts, which is highly relevant to building research agents for astronomy that need to repeatedly access large document corpora, catalogs, or code repositories.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the context map is interpretable (e.g., can we inspect what knowledge is cached about a JWST catalog?)
  • Look for experiments that test PEEK on tasks requiring repeated access to structured scientific data (e.g., tables, catalogs) rather than only code or text.
  • Examine the token budget and eviction policy to see if it can handle the scale of a large astronomical dataset (e.g., millions of sources).
Abstract

Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly operate over long and recurring external contexts, like document corpora and code repositories. Across invocations, existing approaches preserve either the agent's trajectory, passive access to raw material, or task-level strategies. None of them preserves what we argue is most needed for repeated same-context workloads: reusable orientation knowledge (e.g., what the context contains, how it is organized, and which entities, constants, and schemas have historically been useful) about the recurring context itself. We introduce PEEK, a system that caches and maintains this orientation knowledge as a context map: a small, constant-sized artifact in the agent's prompt that gives it a persistent peek into the external context. The map is maintained by a programmable cache policy with three modules: a Distiller that extracts transferable knowledge from inference-time signals, a Cartographer that translates it into structured edits, and a priority-based Evictor that enforces a fixed token budget. On long-context reasoning and information aggregation, PEEK improves over strong baselines by 6.3-34.0% while using 93-145 fewer iterations and incurring 1.7-5.8x lower cost than the state-of-the-art prompt-learning framework, ACE. On context learning, PEEK improves solving rate and rubric accuracy by 6.0-14.0% and 7.8-12.1%, respectively, at 1.4x lower cost than ACE. These gains generalize across LMs and agent architectures, including OpenAI Codex, a production-grade coding agent. Together, these results show that a context map helps long-context LLM agents interact with recurring external contexts more accurately and efficiently.

ClinSeekAgent: Automating Multimodal Evidence Seeking for Agentic Clinical Reasoning

Juncheng Wu , Letian Zhang , Yuhan Wang , Haoqin Tu , Hardy Chen , Zijun Wang , Cihang Xie , Yuyin Zhou

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem of building AI agents that actively seek and synthesize multimodal evidence from heterogeneous sources for clinical decision support. The authors introduce ClinSeekAgent, a framework that given a query, navigates medical knowledge bases, electronic health records, and imaging tools to gather evidence and make grounded decisions. For astronomy, this paradigm directly transfers to building research agents that query astronomical databases (e.g., SIMBAD, NED), analyze JWST images and spectra, and synthesize findings from literature. A concrete workflow could be an agent that, given a target galaxy, retrieves multi-wavelength photometry, fits SEDs, checks for AGN signatures, and produces a summary report. The main caveat is that the current implementation is medical-domain-specific and would require adaptation of tools and knowledge bases for astronomy.

Reason

Directly relevant to building agentic systems for scientific evidence seeking and multimodal reasoning, applicable to astronomy research workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the agentic framework (tool use, iterative planning, evidence synthesis) is domain-agnostic and could be adapted with astronomical tools.
  • Look for details on how the agent handles uncertainty or conflicting evidence, which is common in astronomy.
  • Examine the training pipeline for distilling agent trajectories into smaller models, which could enable deployment on limited compute.
Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) and agentic systems have shown promise for clinical decision support, but existing works largely assume that evidence has already been curated and handed to the model. Real-world clinical workflows instead require agents to actively seek, iteratively plan, and synthesize multimodal evidence from heterogeneous sources. In this paper, we introduce ClinSeekAgent, an automated agentic framework for dynamic multimodal evidence seeking that shifts the paradigm from passive evidence consumption to active evidence acquisition. Given only a clinical query and access to raw data sources, ClinSeekAgent gathers evidence by querying medical knowledge bases, navigating raw EHRs, and invoking medical imaging tools; refines its hypotheses as new information emerges; and integrates the collected evidence into grounded clinical decisions. ClinSeekAgent serves both as an inference-time agent for frontier LLMs and as a training-time pipeline for distilling high-quality agent trajectories into compact open-source models. To validate its inference-time effectiveness, we construct ClinSeek-Bench, which pairs Curated Input reasoning from fixed pre-selected evidence with Automated Evidence-Seeking over raw clinical data. On text-only EHR tasks, ClinSeekAgent improves Claude Opus 4.6 from 60.0 to 63.2 overall F1 and MiniMax M2.5 from 43.1 to 47.3, with positive risk-prediction gains in 7 out of 9 evaluated host models. On multimodal tasks, ClinSeekAgent improves Claude Opus 4.6 from 47.5 to 62.6 (+15.1); all evaluated models improve across the three CXR-related task groups. We further validate ClinSeekAgent as a training pipeline by distilling agentic evidence-seeking trajectories into ClinSeek-35B-A3B, which achieves 34.0 average F1 on existing AgentEHR-Bench, improving over its Qwen3.5-35B-A3B baseline by +11.9 points and approaching Claude Opus 4.6.
