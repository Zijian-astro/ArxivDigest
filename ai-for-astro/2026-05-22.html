Toward AI VIS Co-Scientists: A General and End-to-End Agent Harness for Solving Complex Data Visualization Tasks

Haichao Miao , Zhimin Li , Kuangshi Ai , Kaiyuan Tang , Chaoli Wang , Peer-Timo Bremer , Shusen Liu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper presents an agentic system that, given only data and a high-level task description, autonomously designs and implements a custom interactive visualization application (VIS app). The key idea is a multi-agent harness that coordinates exploratory analysis, planning, implementation, validation, and evaluation, producing a functional single-page app with linked views. For astronomy, this could revolutionize how researchers explore complex datasets like JWST NIRSpec spectra or multi-wavelength catalogs, by automatically generating tailored visualizations. A concrete workflow would be providing a catalog of little red dots with photometry and spectroscopy, asking the system to 'create a visualization that shows SEDs, with filtering by redshift and color selection,' and receiving a functional app. A caveat is that the system is validated on IEEE SciVis Contest data, which may differ from astronomical data in structure and required visual encodings.

Reason

The end-to-end agent for designing custom visualization applications directly addresses a key need in astronomy: creating interactive visualizations for complex multi-dimensional data like JWST catalogs.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the system can handle astronomical data formats (e.g., FITS, VOTable) or requires conversion to CSV/JSON.
  • Look for evaluation of the generated visualizations' usability for domain experts, not just technical correctness.
Abstract

The ability to inspect, interpret, and communicate complex data is crucial for virtually any scientific endeavor, but often requires significant expertise outside the core domain ranging from data management and analysis to visualization design and implementation. We present an end-to-end agentic harness that, based on only the data and a high level description of the tasks, independently designs custom visual analysis applications (VIS apps). This represents an important step towards a general AI co-scientist envisioned by many as an autonomous system that can autonomously execute long horizon tasks based on high-level directions. Our proposed VIS co-scientist is an essential component of this broader AI co-scientist vision: a harness that can autonomously analyze data and design visualization solutions using a collection of agents and specialized skills that coordinate exploratory analysis, plan, configure the environment, implement, validate the interface, and most importantly evaluate the overall task completion. Each stage produces document and instruction artifacts that guide downstream work and enable iterative refinement. We validate this approach on IEEE SciVis Contests spanning multiple science and engineering fields. These contests serve as ideal proving grounds because they encode real-world complexity: ambiguous requirements, diverse data modalities, design trade-offs, and task-driven validation. Given only the data and target tasks, our system autonomously produces functional single-page VIS Apps with verified linked-view behavior, highly customized to domain experts' specified tasks and needs.

LABO: LLM-Accelerated Bayesian Optimization through Broad Exploration and Selective Experimentation

Zhuo Chen (equal contribution) (1 and 2), Xinzhe Yuan (equal contribution) (1 and 3), Jianshu Zhang (1 and 4), Jinzong Dong (1 and 5), Ruichen Zhou (6), Yingchun Niu (6), Tianhang Zhou (7), Yu Yang Fredrik Liu (8), Yuqiang Li (1), Nanyang Ye (1 and 4), Qinying Gu (1) ((1) Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, Shanghai, China, (2) School of Mechanical Engineering, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China, (3) Institute for Advanced Study in Mathematics, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin, China, (4) School of Computer Science, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China, (5) School of Automation, Central South University, Changsha, China, (6) College of New Energy and Materials, China University of Petroleum, Beijing, China, (7) College of Carbon Neutrality Future Technology, China University of Petroleum, Beijing, China, (8) DeepVerse PTE. LTD., Singapore)

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem of expensive and data-scarce scientific experiments by integrating LLM predictions into Bayesian optimization. The core idea is to use cheap LLM evaluations to broadly explore the search space and reserve costly real experiments only for high-uncertainty regions, dynamically balanced by a gating criterion. For astronomy, this could accelerate tasks like optimizing JWST observation parameters or fitting galaxy SEDs where each model evaluation is computationally expensive. A concrete workflow could be using LABO to optimize the parameters of a spectral fitting code (e.g., Prospector) for high-redshift galaxies, where the LLM suggests promising parameter regions based on literature knowledge. A key caveat is that the LLM's knowledge must be relevant to the specific astronomical domain, and the method's performance depends on the quality of the LLM's prior.

Reason

Proposes LLM-accelerated Bayesian optimization for scientific discovery, directly applicable to optimizing experimental parameters in astronomy (e.g., telescope scheduling, SED fitting).

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the gating criterion and how it balances LLM predictions vs. real experiments.
  • Look for the cumulative regret bound and how it formalizes the efficiency gain.
  • Examine the empirical results on diverse scientific tasks to see if any are analogous to astronomical optimization problems.
Abstract

The high cost and data scarcity in scientific exploration have motivated the use of large language models (LLMs) as knowledge-driven components in Bayesian optimization (BO). However, existing approaches typically embed LLMs directly into the sampling or surrogate modeling pipeline, without fully leveraging their significantly lower evaluation cost compared to real-world experiments. To address this limitation, we propose LLM-Accelerated Bayesian Optimization (LABO), a framework that combines LLM predictions with experimental observations within a single BO loop. LABO employs a gating criterion to dynamically balance the reliance on LLM predictions versus actual experiments. By leveraging inexpensive LLM evaluations to broadly explore the search space and reserving costly real experiments only for regions with high uncertainty, LABO achieves more sample-efficient optimization. We provide a theoretical analysis with a cumulative regret bound that formalizes this efficiency gain. Empirical results across diverse scientific tasks demonstrate that LABO consistently outperforms existing methods under identical experimental budgets. Our results suggest that LABO offers a practical and theoretically grounded approach for integrating LLMs into scientific discovery workflows.

Sibyl-AutoResearch: Autonomous Research Needs Self-Evolving Trial-and-Error Harnesses, Not Paper Generators

Chengcheng Wang , Qinhua Xie , Wei He , Jianyuan Guo , Shiqi Wang , Chang Xu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem that current autonomous research systems lack the ability to learn from trial-and-error experiences. It introduces Sibyl-AutoResearch, a framework built around Scientific Trial-and-Error Harnesses that preserve both positive and negative outcomes and route lessons into future planning, validation, and system updates. The key innovation is formalizing trial-to-behavior conversion, where signals from failed experiments change later research actions. For astronomy, this could enable an AI agent that iteratively improves JWST data reduction pipelines, tests different SED fitting approaches, or refines source classification methods by learning from failed runs. A concrete workflow would be an agent that proposes galaxy morphology classifications, runs them on survey data, inspects failures, and updates its classification strategy. The main caveat is that the paper presents a retrospective audit rather than a comparative performance benchmark, so the actual improvement over simpler baselines is not yet demonstrated.

Reason

Directly addresses autonomous research agents with trial-and-error learning, highly relevant to automated scientific discovery for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the eight high-confidence conversion events and whether they involve scientific reasoning or only code-level fixes
  • Check if the harness can be adapted to domain-specific tools like photometric redshift codes or spectral fitting libraries
  • Examine the recovered-failure registry for examples of failures that are common in astronomical data analysis (e.g., numerical instability, model overfitting)
Abstract

Autonomous research systems increasingly make the scientific workflow executable: agents can propose ideas, run code, inspect results, and draft papers. But executable workflows do not by themselves produce research judgment. We analyze where current systems lose trial experience: weak evidence becomes prose, pilot signals become broad claims, memory remains textual, and recurring process failures do not change later behavior. We introduce Sibyl-AutoResearch, a self-evolving AutoResearch framework built around Scientific Trial-and-Error Harnesses. A harness lets agents run bounded trials, preserve positive and negative outcomes, and route lessons into later planning, validation, claim scope, scheduling, critique, writing, and harness repair. We formalize this through two auditable conversion units: trial-to-behavior conversion, which links trial signals to later research actions, and trial-to-harness-behavior conversion, which links recurring process failures to system updates. We implement the framework in SIBYL, a file-backed autonomous research system that exposes the state, roles, memory, gates, and artifact traces needed to inspect these conversion paths. A retrospective audit identifies eight high-confidence conversion events, with a median latency of one iteration and a maximum latency of three iterations. A recovered-failure registry further shows how five naturally occurring failure classes, including duplicate results, stale numbers, and unsupported statistics, were blocked, downgraded, or routed into later repair. These traces do not establish a comparative performance claim; they show that the proposed conversion units are recoverable from realistic autonomous-research workspaces. The SIBYL framework and system are available at this https URL .

Teaching Language Models to Forecast Research Success Through Comparative Idea Evaluation

Srujan P Mule , Aniketh Garikaparthi , Manasi Patwardhan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether language models can forecast which research ideas will succeed before experiments are run. The authors train small LMs to compare pairs of ideas and predict which will achieve better benchmark performance, using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. This could help astronomers prioritize which analysis methods or hypotheses to pursue, especially when many AI-generated ideas are available. One could imagine using such a model to rank candidate galaxy classification schemes or SED-fitting approaches. The method is compute-efficient and uses small models, making it accessible. A caveat is that the training data comes from PapersWithCode benchmarks, not astronomy-specific tasks, so transfer may require domain adaptation.

Reason

Directly addresses automated scientific discovery and idea evaluation, which is relevant to research agents and scientific workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the dataset construction details and whether astronomy tasks could be included.
  • Check the out-of-distribution test results to assess robustness.
  • Examine the interpretable justifications generated by the RLVR model.
Abstract

As language models accelerate scientific research by automating hypothesis generation and implementation, a new bottleneck emerges: evaluating and filtering hundreds of AI-generated ideas without exhaustive experimentation. We ask whether LMs can learn to forecast the empirical success of research ideas before any experiments are run. We study comparative empirical forecasting: given a benchmark-specific research goal and two candidate ideas, predict which will achieve better benchmark performance. We construct a dataset of 11,488 idea pairs grounded in objective outcomes from PapersWithCode. While off-the-shelf 8B-parameter models struggle (30% acc.), SFT dramatically boosts performance to 77.1%, outperforming GPT-5 (61.1%). By framing evaluation as a reasoning task via Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), we train models to discover latent reasoning paths, achieving 71.35% acc. with interpretable justifications. Through additional ablations and out-of-distribution tests, we show robustness to surface-level heuristics and transfer to both a cross-domain time-split test set and an independently constructed test set. Our results demonstrate that compute-efficient small language models can serve as effective, objective verifiers, offering a scalable path for autonomous scientific discovery.

Beyond Scalar Objectives: Expert-Feedback-Driven Autonomous Experimentation for Scientific Discovery at the Nanoscale

Ralph Bulanadi , Jefferey Baxter , Arpan Biswas , Hiroshi Funakubo , Dennis Meier , Jan Schultheiß , Rama Vasudevan , Yongtao Liu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the limitation that Bayesian optimization for autonomous experiments relies on predefined scalar objectives that may miss scientifically important features. It proposes deep-kernel pairwise learning (DKPL), where an expert directly compares pairs of experimental outputs and the system learns a latent utility function from these judgments. For astronomy, this could be used to guide autonomous analysis of JWST images, where an expert's judgment of 'interesting' morphology (e.g., little red dots, disturbed galaxies) is hard to capture in a scalar metric. A concrete workflow would be using DKPL to prioritize which regions of a JWST NIRCam image to analyze with spectroscopy, based on expert pairwise comparisons of candidate targets. A caveat is that the method requires an expert to provide many pairwise comparisons, which may be time-consuming, and the learned utility function may not generalize across different science cases.

Reason

The expert-guided autonomous experimentation framework directly addresses a key challenge in astronomy: incorporating human expertise into automated analysis of complex data like JWST images.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how many pairwise comparisons are needed to learn a reliable utility function, and whether active learning can reduce this.
  • Look for experiments on data types similar to astronomical imaging (e.g., microscopy, which has analogous noise and resolution issues).
Abstract

Self-driving laboratories or autonomous experimentation are emerging as transformative platforms for accelerating scientific discovery. Bayesian optimization (BO) is among the most widely used machine learning frameworks for these purposes, but these BO-based frameworks rely on predefined scalar descriptors to guide experimentation. In many situations, the determination of an appropriate scalar descriptor can be challenging, and may fail to capture subtle yet scientifically important phenomena apparent to experts with interdisciplinary insight. To overcome this limitation, here we develop deep-kernel pairwise learning (DKPL), an approach for autonomous microscopy experiments which incorporates human expertise and interdisciplinary scientific knowledge into an active learning loop. Instead of relying on explicit scalar objectives, DKPL enables experts to directly evaluate which experimental output is more promising using interdisciplinary knowledge. DKPL then learns a latent utility function from these expert judgements to guide subsequent autonomous microscopy experiments. We demonstrate DKPL's performance in learning physically meaningful nanoscale structures while effectively prioritizing high-information measurement regions using an experimental model dataset with known ground truth. We further apply DKPL to analyze the character of ferroelectric domain walls, where we find DKPL capable of distinguishing between high and low characteristic domain-wall angles in bismuth ferrite, and able to discover both head-to-head and tail-to-tail domain-wall character in erbium manganite. This development establishes an approach to integrate expert knowledge into autonomous microscopy experiments and demonstrates a pathway toward expert-guided self-driving laboratories capable of addressing scientific problems beyond the limits of scalar-metrics-driven learning.

RADAR: Defending RAG Dynamically against Retrieval Corruption

Ziyuan Chen , Yueming Lyu , Yi Liu , Weixiang Han , Jing Dong , Caifeng Shan , Tieniu Tan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of adversarial attacks on RAG systems in dynamic web search environments, where retrieved context can be corrupted. The authors propose RADAR, a framework that models reliable context selection as a graph-based energy minimization problem solved via Max-Flow Min-Cut, and uses a Bayesian memory node to recursively update a belief state instead of storing raw historical documents. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because RAG systems are being used to query astronomical literature and databases, and robustness to noisy or adversarial retrieval is critical for reliable scientific analysis. A concrete workflow would be using RADAR to defend a RAG-based astronomy literature agent against corrupted or misleading retrieved papers. The main caveat is that the paper evaluates on a novel dynamic dataset, not on scientific data, so the method's effectiveness on astronomical text needs verification.

Reason

Directly addresses a key vulnerability in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems, which are increasingly used for scientific literature review and data analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the energy minimization formulation is tractable for large-scale astronomical knowledge bases.
  • Look for experiments on domain-specific (e.g., scientific) text, not just general web data.
  • Examine the Bayesian memory node update mechanism to assess its suitability for evolving astronomical knowledge (e.g., new survey results).
Abstract

While RAG systems are increasingly deployed in dynamic web search, temporal volatility amplifies their vulnerability to adversarial attacks. Existing static-oriented defenses struggle to handle evolving threats and incur prohibitive storage costs in dynamic settings. We propose RADAR, a framework that models reliable context selection as a graph-based energy minimization problem, solved exactly via Max-Flow Min-Cut. By incorporating a Bayesian memory node, RADAR recursively updates a belief state instead of archiving raw historical documents, effectively balancing stability against attacks with adaptability to genuine knowledge shifts. Experiments on a novel dynamic dataset show that RADAR achieves superior robustness and response quality with minimal storage overhead compared to the baselines.

Faithful-MR1: Faithful Multimodal Reasoning via Anchoring and Reinforcing Visual Attention

Changyuan Tian , Zhicong Lu , Huaxing Liu , Xiang Wang , Shuai Li , Yu Chen , Wenqian Lv , Zichuan Lin , Juncheng Diao , Deheng Ye

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the faithfulness challenge in multimodal LLMs, where models fail to perceive or use visual evidence correctly during reasoning. It introduces Faithful-MR1, a training framework with two stages: Anchoring, which supervises attention to image regions directly, and Reinforcing, which uses counterfactual image intervention to reward correct use of visual evidence. For astronomy, this could be used to build a multimodal agent that reliably identifies and uses features in JWST images (e.g., identifying little red dots and reasoning about their AGN nature). A concrete workflow could be training a model to answer questions about galaxy morphology from HST images, ensuring it attends to the correct regions. A key caveat is that the method is demonstrated on general multimodal benchmarks and may need adaptation for astronomical images with different noise and resolution characteristics.

Reason

Proposes a training framework for faithful multimodal reasoning, directly relevant to building reliable LMMs for astronomical image and spectral analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the Anchoring stage and how the <Focus> token's attention is supervised against image regions.
  • Look for the Reinforcing stage and the counterfactual image intervention details.
  • Examine the performance on visual reasoning tasks that require precise spatial attention.
Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a promising paradigm for advancing complex reasoning in large language models, and recent work extends RLVR to multimodal large language models (MLLMs). This transfer, however, surfaces a faithfulness challenge: faithful perception of task-relevant visual evidence and faithful use of that evidence during reasoning, leading to unsatisfactory gains on multimodal benchmarks. Specifically, existing perception supervision often operates on textual descriptions rather than natively on image regions, and faithful use is largely overlooked, exposing the perception-reasoning disconnect where correctly perceived evidence is dropped or contradicted during reasoning. To close these gaps, we propose Faithful-MR1, a training framework that anchors and reinforces visual attention to address both halves of faithful multimodal reasoning. The Anchoring stage turns perception into an explicit pre-reasoning subtask, supervising a dedicated <Focus> token's attention directly against image regions rather than through textual descriptions. The Reinforcing stage exposes faithful use through counterfactual image intervention, rewarding answer-correct trajectories that concentrate visual attention where vision causally matters. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Faithful-MR1 outperforms recent multimodal reasoning baselines on both Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct 3B and 7B backbones while using substantially less training data.
