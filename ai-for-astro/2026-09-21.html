An Interpretable Memory Decision Controller for LLM Agents Based on Three-Signal Complementarity: Decoupling Confidence and Consistency

Yiming Zhang , Jinghong Zhang , Haoran Zhao , Yiren Ma , Chunlei Zhao

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles a practical failure mode of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): when a memory store contains conflicting information, standard RAG blindly injects retrieved text and can amplify hallucinations. The authors propose a Memory Decision Layer, a zero-parameter controller placed between retrieval and generation that combines three signals, relevance, reliability, and task risk, using geometric operations to produce an interpretable trust score. It explicitly separates confidence from consistency and can abstain or invert risk when memories conflict. For astronomy, this is interesting because LLM research agents often retrieve conflicting literature or catalog measurements, and a fast white-box gate could flag when not to trust retrieved context. A concrete workflow would be wrapping an astronomy literature-review or SED-interpretation agent with this controller to reduce hallucinated citations or parameter claims. The main caveat is that the evaluation is on general open-source datasets, not scientific or multimodal data, so transfer to astronomical text and tables remains to be tested.

Reason

Directly addresses retrieval-augmented generation reliability and abstention, which maps onto LLM agents for literature review and data analysis in astronomy. The zero-parameter, interpretable trust controller could be adapted to decide when retrieved astronomical literature or catalog entries should be trusted.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the three signals are defined in a way that could accept astronomical metadata such as catalog flags or measurement uncertainties
  • Look for ablation results showing how much each signal contributes and whether the zero-parameter claim holds under distribution shift
  • Check the latency and integration details to see whether it can sit inside a real astronomy agent pipeline
  • Look for any evaluation on scientific, technical, or multimodal text rather than only general QA datasets
Abstract

Memory systems for large language models have focused predominantly on efficient retrieval, whereas the decision of whether retrieved memories should be trusted has received comparatively little attention. When the memory store contains conflicting positions, standard retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) blindly injects memories and amplifies hallucinations: in models susceptible to memory injection, the RAG hallucination rate under conflicting memories is markedly higher than that of a memory-free baseline. Inspired by memory signaling mechanisms in the prefrontal cortex, we propose the Memory Decision Layer (MDL), a zero-parameter memory decision controller situated between the retrieval and generation stages. Its core is a three-signal complementary encoder that fuses relevance, reliability, and task risk through QR-based orthogonal subspace projection and a meta-working-memory signal into an interpretable decision representation that quantifies the trustworthiness of retrieved memories. Building on this encoder, MDL explicitly decouples confidence from consistency and introduces risk inversion and explicit abstention. Evaluations on mainstream large language models and multiple open-source datasets show that MDL reduces the hallucination rate under conflicting memories by about 56.04% in general scenarios and approaches zero hallucination in high-risk scenarios. The controller is fully white-box: it relies purely on geometric operations, requires no trained parameters, and adds only about 0.14 ms per decision -- roughly 50x faster than the embedding-retrieval step that precedes it and four to five orders of magnitude faster than an LLM self-evaluation call.

Boosting Deepresearch and LongContext Ability with Self-Generated Deepresearch Rollouts Traces

Zihan Wang , Hao Wang , Boyuan Jiang , Yiqun Zhang , Shi Feng , Xiaocui Yang , Yiwen Ye , Jianghang Lin , Xiaozhong Ji , Jinghao Lin , Kai Wu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses deep-research agents that search and read many web pages, where the context grows so long that the model starts hallucinating or failing to integrate evidence across documents. The core idea, DR-to-Long, takes existing agent rollout trajectories and replaces the short snippets and summaries with the full text of the visited URLs, turning them into long-context question-answering training examples at no extra annotation cost. A second stage, DLD-RL, first trains the agent briefly, converts its rollouts into long-context QA data, trains on that, and then returns to agent training. This is relevant to astronomy because a literature-review or data-analysis agent for JWST would similarly need to combine many papers, catalog pages, and instrument documents without losing track of evidence. I could imagine using this recipe to fine-tune an agent that reads a set of high-redshift galaxy papers and answers synthesis questions with citations. The caveat is that the gains are measured on web deep-research and generic long-context benchmarks, so I should check whether the trajectory format and reward design transfer to scientific PDFs and tabular data.

Reason

Directly relevant to LLM research agents and long-context evidence integration, which maps onto literature review and multi-document reasoning over astronomical papers and survey data.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how trajectories are converted into long-context QA instances and whether the process preserves evidence-to-answer links
  • Look for details on the RL reward and whether it encourages citation-grounded answers or only final-answer accuracy
  • Check whether the long-context benchmarks include multi-document scientific or technical text rather than only web pages
  • Assess whether the method requires full URL contents and how it would handle paywalled or PDF-only astronomy literature
Abstract

Deepresearch (DR) agents interact with real-world web environments through multi-turn search and visit, causing their contexts to grow rapidly over time. We observe that, even after DR Agentic Reinforcement Learning (DR-RL), 61.6% of the model's remaining prediction errors can still be attributed to insufficient long-context understanding, including longcontext hallucination and failures in cross-document evidence integration. It motivates us to further break the bottleneck of DR-RL by strengthening the model's long-context ability. However, effective LongContext training requires more than simply increasing context length. To bridge the data gap, we propose `DR Rollouts to LongContext-QA (DR-to-Long)'. The method repurposes DR-RL trajectories, which naturally contain search histories, visited webpages, evidence snippets, and final-answer supervision. It then replaces the compact snippets and webpage summaries in each trajectory with the full contents of their corresponding URLs, producing substantially longer multi-document contexts while preserving the original evidence relationships. Building on DR-to-Long, we introduce DLD (DR -> LongQA -> DR)-RL. DLD-RL first performs a short DR-RL stage to collect rollout trajectories, which are then converted into LongQA instances at zero annotation cost. The model is subsequently optimized with LongQA-RL to strengthen LongContext ability, followed by full DR-RL to continue improving its DR capability. Experiments show that DLD-RL outperforms standard DR-RL by 7.3% on three Deepresearch benchmarks and improves performance by 13.5% on three long-context benchmarks.

RBS-Attention: Radius-Bounded Sparse Prefill for Long-Context Large Language Models

Chuxu Song , Jiuqi Wei , Zhencan Peng

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that long-context large language model inference is bottlenecked by the prefill stage, where dense self-attention must process the entire prompt before generation begins. The main idea is RBS-Attention, a training-free sparse-prefill method that uses two selection branches: a centroid base branch that captures average relevance and a rescue branch that uses the maximum key-block radius to identify blocks where a highly relevant token might be hidden among irrelevant ones, a failure mode they call mean dilution. The method achieves large speedups on H100 GPUs, including a 5.97x end-to-end time-to-first-token speedup at 128K context, while maintaining accuracy close to dense attention on benchmarks like RULER and LongBench-v2. For astronomy, this matters because LLM agents for literature review, proposal writing, or multi-paper synthesis often need to ingest very long contexts, such as dozens of papers or large catalog descriptions, and faster prefill directly reduces cost and latency. A concrete workflow would be to deploy RBS-Attention in an astronomy literature-review agent that reads many JWST papers at once and answers questions across them. The main caveat is that the method is evaluated on general long-context benchmarks, so its behavior on highly technical astronomy text or tabular catalog data should be tested before relying on it.

Reason

The paper introduces a training-free sparse-attention method for long-context LLM inference, which is directly relevant to building efficient LLM agents for astronomy that need to process long documents, catalogs, or multi-paper literature reviews.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the method is compatible with popular inference frameworks such as vLLM and whether the code is released
  • Look for accuracy results on technical or scientific long-context tasks, not just general benchmarks
  • Assess the memory and latency trade-offs at context lengths relevant to reading many papers or large catalogs
  • Check whether the rescue branch introduces any failure modes on structured data like tables or spectra descriptions
Abstract

Long-context large language model inference is increasingly limited by prefill, where dense self-attention processes the entire prompt before generation begins. Sparse block selection can reduce this cost, but a block centroid may hide a highly relevant token among many irrelevant ones. We call this failure mode mean dilution and propose RBS-Attention, a training-free sparse-prefill method with two complementary selection branches. A centroid base branch captures average relevance, while a rescue branch uses the maximum key-block radius and its prompt-, layer-, and head-dependent distribution to identify blocks at risk of underestimation. Independently thresholding the two branches and combining their masks controls the contribution of rescue blocks while preserving regular block-sparse FlashAttention execution. On H100 GPUs, RBS-Attention achieves 20.65$\times$ standalone prefill-attention speedup, 11.92$\times$ vLLM prefill-attention speedup, and 5.97$\times$ end-to-end time-to-first-token speedup at 128K on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507-FP8. On the dense Qwen3-32B model, it obtains 88.65 overall RULER accuracy versus 89.52 for dense attention; LongBench-v2, InfiniteBench, and Video-MME provide additional quality evaluation. Supporting experiments measure actual retention, compare selectors at matched density, and characterize block-size, threshold, and memory behavior. Together, these results support radius-adaptive dual-branch selection as an effective approach to long-context prefill.

Scaling Discovery through Test-Time Communication

Jongho Park , Vasilis Kontonis , Shivam Garg , Akshay Krishnamurthy , Dimitris Papailiopoulos

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether letting multiple LLM agents communicate during inference (test-time communication) beats running them independently in parallel, which is directly relevant to building research agents. The core idea is a team of k agents with no predefined roles that share findings through a shared directory, and the authors show this team matches the success rate of 4k independent agents, with gains compounding as the team grows. This matters for astronomy because automated discovery pipelines (literature review, data analysis, coding, spectral fitting) could benefit from agents that share intermediate results rather than duplicating effort. A concrete workflow would be a team of agents each exploring a different analysis path on a JWST dataset and sharing promising leads, potentially solving problems no single agent solves. The key caveat is that gains are conditional: with limited compute or no clear progress signal, independent agents can outperform communication, so you must check the compute and feedback requirements before adopting this.

Reason

Multi-agent test-time communication for scaling discovery is directly relevant to LLM research agents and automated scientific discovery, including astronomy workflows where parallel agents could share breakthroughs. The finding that communicating teams solve tasks no single agent can is important for designing research-agent systems.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the compute budget and feedback conditions under which communication helps versus hurts
  • Look for the shared-directory communication protocol details and whether it is reproducible
  • Check whether the research-oriented tasks (polyomino packing, MNIST compression) are representative of real scientific workflows
  • Assess whether the approach could be adapted to astronomy-specific agent tasks like spectral fitting or catalog cross-matching
Abstract

Science advances not in isolation but through collaboration, yet existing agentic systems capture little of this. Whether communicating agents help remains an open question with mixed prior results. We show that test-time communication can substantially outperform independent parallel attempts on challenging tasks, where sharing a breakthrough can push the whole group forward. We first study the effect of scaling multi-agent test-time communication, where agents have no predefined roles and communicate via a shared directory, on ARC-AGI-3, a benchmark requiring novel problem solving. We find that a team of $k$ communicating agents, team@$k$, matches the success rate of $4k$ independent agents, and this advantage grows with $k$, suggesting gains compound with scale. The effect is not merely efficiency: a task that no single agent can solve, a team of agents can solve reliably. Furthermore, these gains transfer to research-oriented tasks, given sufficient compute. On polyomino packing, communicating agents outperform best@$k$ and exceed the prior best-known score. On MNIST classifier compression, communication surpasses the best-known human solution. A team of four agents produced a 1,957-byte classifier submission achieving 99.4% test accuracy, smaller than both the best-known human solution and the best single-agent result. These gains are not unconditional. Independent agents may outperform communication when compute is limited or when a clear measure of progress is absent. However, under sufficient compute and clear feedback, multi-agent communication consistently yields stronger results.

When Better Turns Do Not Make Better Agents: Diagnosing the Gap Between Next-Turn Metrics and Workflow Success

Md Tahmid Rahman Laskar , Xue-Yong Fu , Gundeep Singh , Karol Chang , Kevin Sanders , Shi Zong , Tania Habib , Julien Bouvier Tremblay , Shayna Gardiner , Harsh Saini , Matthias Lee , Elena Khasanova , Quinten McNamara , Shashi Bhushan TN

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether improving an agent's next-action prediction under gold-history evaluation actually translates into better autonomous multi-turn workflow execution. The main finding is that supervised fine-tuning improves next-turn success for all tested models, but these gains do not transfer to autonomous workflow execution, with strict trajectory completion reaching at most about 10 percent success. Tool-specific gains also vary, and none of the fine-tuned models succeeds under holistic workflow evaluation. For astronomy, this is a warning for anyone building LLM agents for literature review, data reduction, or analysis pipelines: good local scores may not mean the agent can complete a real scientific workflow. A concrete workflow would be to evaluate an astronomy research agent not only on next-step accuracy but on end-to-end task completion, such as producing a reproducible analysis from a raw data request. The main caveat is that the study is on customer-support workflows, so the specific numbers may not transfer, but the methodological lesson likely does.

Reason

This paper diagnoses the gap between next-turn metrics and end-to-end workflow success in LLM agents, which is directly relevant to building reliable research agents for astronomy. The finding that local action accuracy does not guarantee workflow success is important for LLM agent evaluation in scientific workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how workflow success is defined and whether it could be adapted to astronomical analysis pipelines
  • Look for the breakdown of text quality, local action correctness, tool execution, and end-to-end completion
  • Assess whether the evaluation includes tool-use errors and recovery behavior
  • Check whether the paper provides recommendations for agent evaluation that could inform astronomy research-agent benchmarks
Abstract

Agent models are frequently evaluated one decision at a time, where the model predicts the next action based on the gold interaction history, which is scored against a reference. We investigate whether improvement under this protocol is predictive of improved autonomous workflow execution. We study pre-SFT and supervised fine-tuned (SFT) Qwen3 models at 4B and 14B parameters and Gemma 3 models at 4B and 12B parameters on multi-turn customer-support workflows. We find that SFT consistently improves text-turn success, and that overall next-turn success increases for every model under gold-history evaluation. However, these improvements do not transfer to autonomous workflow execution. Tool-specific gains also vary across metrics and models. None of the four SFT models succeeds under holistic workflow evaluation, with strict trajectory completion reaching at most 10.4% workflow success. Our results show that next-turn evaluation is not a reliable proxy for workflow success, motivating separate reporting of text quality, local action correctness, tool execution, and end-to-end task completion.
