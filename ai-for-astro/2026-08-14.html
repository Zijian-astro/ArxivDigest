Test-Time Optimization of Query Embeddings with Ranking Aware Reward Maximization

Tianyu Chen , Jiaxing Wu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem of improving dense retrieval performance at test time without updating model weights. The main idea is to distill ranking rewards from a reranker or LLM judge into a lightweight learned vector in the embedding space, optimized purely from scalar scores. This matters for astronomy because it could enhance retrieval of relevant papers or data in RAG systems for literature review or data discovery. A concrete workflow could be using this to improve retrieval of astronomical papers or catalogs in a research agent. A limitation is that it requires access to ranking scores from a judge, which may be costly, and the optimal sharing scope depends on the reward budget.

Reason

The paper proposes a test-time optimization method for dense retrievers using ranking rewards, which is relevant to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and could improve document retrieval for astronomical literature or data. The method is model-agnostic and works with frozen encoders, making it applicable to closed-source models.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the method requires a specific type of reranker or LLM judge, and how the reward budget is defined in practice.
  • Look for experiments on retrieval tasks similar to scientific document retrieval, and whether the method generalizes to unseen domains.
  • Examine the computational overhead of the optimization and whether it is feasible for large-scale astronomical catalogs.
Abstract

Dense retrievers rank documents using vector similarity between a frozen encoder and a precomputed index. While test-time ranking rewards from a reranker or LLM judge can improve results, existing methods discard this signal after a single query. Updating the retriever's weights makes rewards reusable, but this requires parameter access, which is unavailable for closed-source models, and is computationally prohibitive. We propose TTT-Embed (Test-Time Tuning of Embeddings), a framework that distills ranking rewards into a lightweight, learned vector within the output embedding space of a frozen model. This vector is optimized purely from scalar ranking scores assigned to the retriever's own candidate documents, requiring no access to model weights, ground-truth labels, or modifications to index. A single scope parameter controls rewards reuse (global, task, or query), enabling a principled trade-off between reusability and specificity under a fixed reward computation budget. We demonstrate that as the available reward budget scales, the optimal sharing scope shifts dynamically from global-wise to task-wise and finally to query-wise. Evaluated across five embedding models and 15 MTEB retrieval tasks, TTT-Embed improves test-time retrieval by up to +8.36 nDCG@10. Crucially, the learned states generalize effectively to unseen queries (up to +8.57 nDCG@10) and unseen tasks (up to +4.71 nDCG@10). Furthermore, TTT-Embed successfully resolves catastrophic forgetting: by leaving base weights entirely frozen, it recovers degraded general capabilities (up to +8.00 nDCG@10, even surpassing the original base model) while preserving in-domain specialization. These results establish ranking rewards as a reusable test-time state, enabling budget-efficient adaptation for any embedding model, including closed-source APIs.

Reasoning Jury: Multi-Model Consensus for Evaluating Reasoning Traces

Congchao Wang , Diwakar Singh , Qiaozi Gao , Spyros Matsoukas , Yang Liu , Mahdi Namazifar

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem of judging the quality of long reasoning traces in LLMs. The main idea is to use a jury of open-weight models with a moderated consensus mechanism to identify reasoning defects more accurately than single frontier models. This matters for astronomy because it could enhance the reliability of LLM-based reasoning in scientific workflows, such as interpreting JWST data. A concrete workflow could be using Reasoning Jury to evaluate reasoning traces from an LLM agent that analyzes astronomical spectra. A limitation is the cost of running multiple models, though it is lower than frontier models.

Reason

The paper proposes a multi-model consensus system for evaluating reasoning traces, which is relevant to LLM agents for research and automated scientific discovery. It could improve the quality of reasoning in astronomy-related LLM applications, such as data analysis or literature review.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the method can be applied to reasoning traces in scientific domains, and if the defect definitions are general.
  • Look for details on the deliberation mechanism and how consensus is reached.
  • Examine the cost breakdown and whether it is feasible for large-scale use in astronomy.
Abstract

Improving reasoning LLMs requires the ability to judge the quality of long reasoning traces for effective reasoning data curation, strong training signals during reinforcement learning, and an in-depth understanding of reasoning behaviors during model performance evaluation. Additionally, surfacing reasoning mistakes that the model makes would enable improving the model's performance at runtime through providing feedback. Due to the difficulty of this complex task on long reasoning traces, single-model judges (even frontier models) do not do well at identifying reasoning defects. Additionally, leveraging frontier models during online training of reasoning LLMs is generally prohibited due to guardrails in terms of use. In this work, we introduce Reasoning Jury, a system that replaces the single judge with a jury of LLMs and a moderated consensus mechanism, to improve the fidelity of judgments for identifying reasoning defects. In reasoning jury, defects of a reasoning trace and their severity are surfaced through a deliberation where a moderator conducts a discussion amongst the jury where the jurors critique each other's judgments and get to modify their initial votes. The moderator derives a consensus through deliberation amongst jurors or consolidation of judgements. We show that Reasoning Jury with a jury of open-weight models (e.g., gpt-oss-120b) is able to significantly outperform frontier models (opus-4.6, sonnet-4.6, and gemini-3.1-pro) at correctly identifying reasoning defects. Besides accuracy performance improvements, the aggregated cost of the jury (initial verdicts, deliberations, consolidation, etc.) is a fraction (8 to 15%) of the cost of running frontier models in LLM-as-a-judge setup. We also show how these judgements can be leveraged to understand failure modes of reasoning LLMs on benchmarks, which allows much deeper understanding of a model's performance.

@skills: Attention is all you have

Li Yin , Zhi Li , Zhan Shi , Haoran Zhang , Haebin Seong , Zhangyang (Atlas) Wang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem of the long tail of agent skills not being usable due to prompt residency limits. The main idea is a protocol that separates content, persistence, and triggering, allowing skills to be addressed by path without installation. This matters for astronomy because it could enable a library of reusable skills for astronomical data analysis in research agents. A concrete workflow could be using @skills to manage skills for photometric redshift estimation or SED fitting. A limitation is that it requires a hub for discovery, which may need community adoption.

Reason

The paper proposes a protocol for managing agent skills, which is relevant to LLM agents for scientific workflows. It could help organize and reuse skills for astronomical data analysis, such as JWST data processing.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the protocol supports versioning and collaboration for scientific skills.
  • Look for details on how skills are authored and whether they can be domain-specific.
  • Examine the integration with existing agent frameworks and whether it is compatible with astronomy tools.
Abstract

There are 56,804 public agent skills today, and teams write many more privately. The dominant delivery model is installation: once installed, a skill's description remains in the system prompt, competing for fewer than 100 reliable trigger slots. This leaves the long tail with no practical path to use and forces teams' own playbooks to compete for the same scarce space. We observe that installation bundles three separable functions: content, persistence, and automatic triggering. Only the last requires prompt residency. We therefore propose @skills, an open protocol that separates them. A path addresses any skill, subtree, or collection, and reading a skill is sufficient to use it, so nothing is installed or made resident. The operation vendors a copy at the same path into a project's Git-tracked tree for adaptation and ownership. The operation adds one .gitignore-style line, the only element that costs prompt residency. A directory is a menu, making bundles ordinary directories rather than all-or-nothing units. The protocol requires no manifest, lockfile, or registration, and this http URL remains unchanged. @skills is additive, ships as an installable package, and turns any agent that can read files and run commands into a client through a single instruction file. Its open specification is at this https URL and it is implemented in the AdaL CLI at this https URL . Because paths address skills well but cannot find them, the protocol is paired with a free hub at this https URL for corpus-wide search and ranking, repository-free hosting, private and team collections, and one-screen authoring. The hub is optional: gh: and local paths resolve without it, and indexed GitHub skills retain their gh: identities. Install less, use more.

Beyond Outcome Rewards: Step-Level Self-Distilled Policy Optimization for Deep Search Agents

Haoze Wu , Chuqiao Kuang , Tianyi Zhuang , Xiaoguang Li

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of sparse reward in deep search agents, where only a single outcome reward is given for long trajectories. The main idea is Step-Level Self-Distilled Policy Optimization (SSPO), which uses evidence anchors (concise web snippets) as privileged information to provide step-level advantage weights during training, applied only to incorrect trajectories. This decouples what to update from how much to update, improving learning efficiency. For astronomy, this could enhance agents that search through catalogs or literature to identify high-redshift galaxies or anomalous sources, by providing finer-grained feedback on intermediate steps. One could imagine training an agent to navigate JWST data archives and extract relevant spectra, with SSPO improving its search strategy. The caveat is that it requires a source of privileged evidence anchors, which may not be readily available for all astronomical tasks.

Reason

Focuses on improving deep search agents via step-level reinforcement learning, directly relevant to building research agents for astronomy that perform multi-step literature or data searches. The method addresses credit assignment in long trajectories, a key challenge for automated discovery.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether evidence anchors are domain-agnostic and can be constructed from astronomical databases.
  • Look for details on the overhead and stability of the method on longer trajectories.
  • Assess whether the improvement is significant for tasks with noisy or incomplete evidence.
Abstract

Deep search agents operate over trajectories spanning dozens of steps, yet standard reinforcement learning provides only a single outcome reward per trajectory, which is far too sparse for effective credit assignment. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) addresses this by using the model's own logits as dense token-level teachers, but extending it to search agents introduces a fundamental tension: the teacher, having access to privileged information such as the correct answer, produces a distribution that differs systematically from the student's exploration-based reasoning, and naive distillation causes the student to inherit this information asymmetry rather than learn better search strategies. We resolve this tension through two contributions. First, we construct Evidence Anchors, which are concise, step-level evidence snippets extracted from the web, as privileged information that captures key reasoning steps without revealing the entire answer path. Second, we propose Step-Level Self-Distilled Policy Optimization (SSPO), which converts teacher-student disagreement into step-level advantage weights within GRPO, applied exclusively to incorrect trajectories. This design decouples what to update from how much to update: the outcome reward determines the direction of policy change, while the teacher modulates its magnitude at each step. Correct trajectories are left untouched, preserving their diversity. On Qwen3-8B, SSPO consistently outperforms GRPO across BrowseComp, GAIA, and FRAMES, surpassing or matching GRPO trained with twice as many gradient steps while adding only about 5 percent overhead per step from a single additional forward pass.

ARAC: Benchmarking Auto-Research's Alignment and Completeness on End-to-End Researchs

Jiale Cui , Yueyao Yuan , Kaixi Zhong , Xiaogang Xu , Jiafei Wu , Zhe Liu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the challenge of evaluating auto-research systems by measuring how well their research processes align with human research behavior. The main idea is ARAC-Bench, a framework with Academic Cognition Skills rubrics and a three-stage diagnostic protocol (Proposal, Experiment, Synthesis). It finds that even the best systems score only 67.9/100, showing a significant gap. For astronomy, this could help evaluate AI agents that propose and test hypotheses about galaxy formation or AGN evolution. One could imagine using ARAC-Bench to assess an agent designed to analyze JWST spectra and propose follow-up observations. The caveat is that the benchmark is general and may need adaptation to astronomy-specific research workflows.

Reason

Directly addresses benchmarking auto-research systems, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery for astronomy. The framework evaluates alignment and completeness of research processes, not just outcomes, which is crucial for trustworthy AI-driven research.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the rubrics are domain-agnostic or can be customized for astronomy.
  • Look for details on how the three stages are decomposed and scored.
  • Assess whether the framework can provide actionable feedback for improving research agents.
Abstract

The rapid advancement of Auto-Research has surfaced a fundamental evaluation challenge: how can we measure the alignment, logical coherence, and evolutionary completeness of its research trajectory with human research behavior? We propose Auto-Research's Alignment and Completeness, ARAC-Bench: a Researcher-Mimicking Evaluation framework that shifts the objective from matching final answers to reproducing high-quality human research processes. The framework operates through two synergistic components: the Academic Cognition Skills system, which is the first to transforms implicit reviewer expertise into stage-calibrated, quantifiable rubrics; and a three-stage capability diagnostic protocol, which decomposes the research process under strict modular constraints into three traceable, mutually independent dimensions: Proposal, Experiment, and Synthesis. Systematic evaluation of 11 SOTA frameworks yields a best alignment score of only 67.9 of 100, revealing a significant gap in simulating rigorous human methodology. Validation against Ph.D. Candidates rankings shows a strong correlation of 0.8141, confirming that ARAC-Bench reliably reflects the dimensions researchers truly value. ARAC-Bench provides not only a fine-grained diagnostic tool but also a scalable reward signal for training the next generation of autonomous research systems.
