CORTEX: Token-Level Hallucination Detection in RAG via Comparative Internal Representations

Kazuaki Furumai , Shuichiro Haruta , Kazunori Matsumoto , Daisuke Kamisaka

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper proposes CORTEX, a token-level hallucination detection method for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). It compares internal representations of an LLM with and without retrieved documents to identify ungrounded tokens. For astronomy, this could be used to build reliable LLM agents that summarize literature or analyze data while flagging potential hallucinations. The method enables fine-grained localization of hallucinations, which is crucial for scientific accuracy. A limitation is that it requires access to the LLM's internal representations, which may not be available for all models, and it is tested on generic RAG benchmarks, not astronomical texts.

Reason

Token-level hallucination detection in RAG is directly relevant to building reliable LLM agents for literature review and data analysis in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the method can be applied to domain-specific RAG systems (e.g., with astronomical literature or data).
  • Look for the types of hallucinations detected and whether they correspond to common errors in scientific summarization.
  • See if the post-processing smoothing step is robust to the longer spans typical in scientific texts.
  • Check if the method requires specific model architectures or if it is model-agnostic.
Abstract

In this paper, we propose CORTEX, a token-level hallucination detection method for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). In long-form RAG outputs, hallucinations often arise in localized spans rather than throughout an entire response. CORTEX therefore identifies ungrounded content at the token level, enabling fine-grained localization of hallucinations. The key intuition behind CORTEX is that tokens grounded in retrieved documents should be more strongly influenced by those documents than hallucinated tokens. To capture this document-induced effect, CORTEX compares internal representations of a large language model (LLM) under two conditions: with and without the retrieved documents. Instead of relying solely on each token's immediate sensitivity to the retrieved documents, CORTEX also leverages the propagation of document-grounded information through preceding tokens, reducing false positives for tokens whose evidence has already been absorbed into the context. Finally, CORTEX applies post-processing smoothing step that models the tendency of hallucination labels to persist over contiguous spans, reducing local noise and encouraging span-consistent predictions. Experiments on two RAG benchmarks and three LLMs show that CORTEX substantially improves token-level hallucination detection, with each component consistently contributing to performance gains.

One Reflection Is Not Enough: Self-Correcting Autonomous Research via Multi-Hypothesis Failure Attribution

Jie Ma , Binfei Chu , Jie Gao , Jinlu Zhang , Yiwei Ma , Yi Tan , Jiayi Ji , Xiaoshuai Sun , Rongrong Ji

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of brittle failure recovery in autonomous research agents, where current methods rely on single free-form reflections that often lead to trial-and-error or loss of useful context. The core idea is Multi-Hypothesis Failure Attribution (MHFA), which treats failure recovery as a structured causal diagnosis: it generates multiple evidence-grounded explanations for a failure, evaluates their severity, and routes the verified root cause to the correct intervention level (hypothesis, experimental design, or implementation). The method also includes a grounded reporting mechanism that redacts hallucinated numbers. For astronomy, this could be transformative for automated data analysis pipelines: imagine an agent that attempts to fit a spectrum, fails, and systematically diagnoses whether the problem is a bad model, poor data quality, or a bug in the code, then corrects the appropriate component. A concrete workflow might involve using SAGE to autonomously analyze JWST spectra of high-redshift galaxies, where the agent writes code to perform SED fitting, detects failures (e.g., poor chi-squared), and automatically corrects the model or preprocessing steps. However, the paper's benchmark is on general scientific topics (e.g., physics, chemistry) and may not include astronomy-specific tasks, so the agent's performance on astronomical data is unknown. The method requires access to LLMs with coding capabilities and a sandboxed execution environment.

Reason

This paper directly addresses automated scientific discovery with LLM agents, which is a core interest. It proposes a structured failure-recovery mechanism for research agents that could be applied to astronomical data analysis workflows, such as automated SED fitting or spectral analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the benchmark includes any astronomy or astrophysics tasks, or if the method is demonstrated on data similar to astronomical spectra or images.
  • Look for details on how the MHFA mechanism generates and evaluates multiple hypotheses, and whether it requires a predefined set of possible failure modes.
  • Examine the grounded reporting mechanism to see if it can be adapted for astronomical results (e.g., ensuring reported fluxes are within realistic ranges).
  • Check the code and reproducibility: is the system available and can it be run on custom astronomical datasets?
Abstract

Autonomous research agents can now draft hypotheses, write code, run experiments, and produce papers, but they remain brittle when experiments fail. Under the prevailing paradigm, failure recovery is usually delegated to a single free-form reflection: a rich trajectory of metrics, logs, and design choices is compressed into one verbal critique, which often leads either to localized trial-and-error or to hard pivots that discard useful context. We propose SAGE, a Self-correcting, Autonomous, Grounded Experimenter, to tackle this failure-recovery bottleneck. Its core mechanism, Multi-Hypothesis Failure Attribution (MHFA), treats recovery as a structured causal diagnosis. By analyzing dynamic trajectory features, MHFA systematically generates multiple evidence-grounded explanations for a failure, independently evaluates their severity, and deterministically routes the verified root cause to the correct intervention level (hypothesis, experimental design, or implementation). To guarantee scientific honesty, SAGE further employs a grounded reporting mechanism that explicitly constrains drafted results to actual measured values, redacting hallucinated numbers. On a 12-topic, 5-domain benchmark, SAGE increases metrics-bearing outputs from 42% to 92% over a reflection baseline, improves artifact quality from 5.00 to 6.75/10, and blindly outscores AI-Scientist-v2 (52.0 vs. 48.2), with gains concentrated in code development and execution. While fully autonomous scientific writing and generating conference-ready papers remain notoriously difficult open problems for the entire field, SAGE successfully produces significantly more reliable and higher-quality scientific artifacts. Ultimately, by coupling structured recovery with explicit grounding constraints, SAGE significantly outperforms monolithic reflection paradigms, establishing a highly trustworthy foundation for future autonomous research.

TerraDiT-$Ω$: Unified Spatial Control for Satellite Image Synthesis with Any Geospatial Primitive

Brian Wei , Srikumar Sastry , Daniel Cher , Eric Xing , Nathan Jacobs

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces TerraDiT-Ω, a unified spatial control framework for satellite image synthesis that generates imagery from any native geospatial primitive (polygons, polylines, bounding boxes, points). It uses Geometry-Aware Local Attention to inject geometric cues into the diffusion model's attention space. For astronomy, this could be used to generate synthetic astronomical images from catalog data or morphological annotations, aiding in data augmentation for surveys like JWST, Roman, or Euclid. The method supports controllable layouts and could be applied to tasks like land-cover segmentation, object detection, and scene classification, which have direct analogs in astronomy (e.g., source detection, morphology classification). A limitation is that the paper focuses on satellite imagery, and the geospatial primitives may need adaptation to astronomical coordinate systems and source shapes.

Reason

Satellite image synthesis with geospatial primitives is directly relevant to astronomical image generation and data augmentation for surveys.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the method can generate images with point-like sources (stars, galaxies) or extended structures (nebulae, galaxy clusters).
  • Look for the types of geospatial primitives used and whether they can be mapped to astronomical annotations (e.g., source positions, morphological masks).
  • See if the synthetic data augmentation improves downstream performance on tasks analogous to astronomical classification or detection.
  • Check if the code and weights are available and can be adapted for astronomical image generation.
Abstract

Generative models have achieved remarkable progress, yet applying them to satellite imagery remains challenging. Unlike natural imagery, satellite scenes are structured by spatially complex and semantically distinct geometries. Prior work addresses this complexity by adapting natural image frameworks using dense rasters or sparse prompts, trading off annotation cost and fidelity while breaking compatibility with vector primitives commonly used to represent geographic information. We introduce TerraDiT-$\Omega$, a unified spatial control framework that generates satellite imagery directly from any native geospatial primitive. By jointly leveraging precise annotations (polygons, polylines) and coarser ones (bounding boxes, points), the model supports controllable layouts across varying annotation budgets, broadening applicability to design tasks such as urban planning while remaining naturally compatible with end-to-end GeoAI workflows. To effectively leverage these primitives during generation, we propose Geometry-Aware Local Attention, a conditioning mechanism that injects explicit geometric cues into the attention space. Across all conditioning formats, our approach consistently outperforms both dense-control and sparse-control baselines. Furthermore, this flexibility enables controllable synthetic data augmentation using a single generative model, improving downstream performance on land-cover segmentation, object detection, road graph extraction, and scene classification. Code, data, and weights are available at this https URL .

Probabilistic Inversion with Flow Matching

Baldur Paulwitz , Stefan Buske

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper applies Flow Matching, a generative AI technique, to probabilistic inversion in geophysical settings, specifically seismic Full-Waveform inversion. It adapts the mathematical theory of Flow Matching to produce posterior distributions over velocity models given seismic data. The method is demonstrated on a simple 2D velocity model and the OpenFWI dataset. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because many astronomical problems are inverse problems (e.g., reconstructing galaxy properties from spectra, or mapping dark matter from lensing). Flow Matching could provide a principled way to sample from the posterior distribution, enabling uncertainty quantification. The main caveat is that the paper focuses on 2D seismic velocity models, and adapting the method to high-dimensional astronomical data (e.g., 3D maps, spectra) may require significant computational resources and architectural changes.

Reason

Directly applies flow matching to probabilistic inversion in geophysics, a method that could transfer to astronomical inverse problems like source reconstruction or parameter estimation.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the Flow Matching architecture can handle high-dimensional outputs (e.g., images, spectra).
  • Look for discussion of the computational cost of training and sampling.
  • Examine the uncertainty quantification: does the posterior capture realistic uncertainties for astronomical applications?
  • Check if the method requires a forward model that is differentiable, which may be a limitation for some astronomical models.
Abstract

We demonstrate the application of Flow Matching, a technique originating from generative Artificial Intelligence, to probabilistic inversion in geophysical settings, such as seismic Full-Waveform inversion. We adapt the well-established mathematical theory of Flow Matching from generative Artificial Intelligence to the context of probabilistic inversion. We evaluate the approach with two case studies: a simple 2D velocity model to illustrate the general features of the method, and the OpenFWI dataset to show its capabilities for probabilistic inversion of more complex seismic velocity models.

ReGRPO: Reflection-Augmented Policy Optimization for Tool-Using Agents

Binjie Zhang , Mike Zheng Shou

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem that tool-using AI agents often fail silently and cannot recover from errors. It introduces ReGRPO, a method that first collects examples of failures and their corrections, then uses reinforcement learning to jointly optimize the agent's reasoning about errors and its corrective actions. For astronomy, this could enable an agent that runs a photometric redshift pipeline, detects when a fit fails, and automatically tries a different SED template or flags the object for review. A concrete workflow would be an agent that ingests JWST NIRSpec spectra, calls a spectral fitting tool, and if the fit fails, reflects on the cause and retries with different parameters. A key caveat is that the method was tested on general vision-language benchmarks, so its performance on domain-specific scientific tools and data formats needs verification.

Reason

Directly relevant to building LLM agents for scientific workflows, with a focus on tool use and recovery from failures, which is crucial for automated data analysis in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the failure trajectories and reflection triplets are grounded in realistic tool-use scenarios or synthetic tasks.
  • Look for ablation studies showing whether the reflection-cost term is necessary to prevent over-reflection in a scientific context.
  • Examine the tool suite used in experiments to see if it includes any scientific or data-analysis tools relevant to astronomy.
Abstract

Tool-augmented vision-language models (VLMs) can solve multimodal, multi-step tasks by calling external tools, yet they remain fragile in practice. Existing works have two common gaps. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is built mostly on successful trajectories and offers little signal for recovery after tool failures, while sparse trajectory-level RL rewards provide limited guidance on which step failed and how to repair it. We introduce ReGRPO (Reflection-augmented Group Relative Policy Optimization), a framework that learns reflection-guided correction in tool-using agents. ReGRPO starts with a structured reflective data engine: we execute near-miss actions to collect grounded failure observations, then build Reflection-of-Thought triplets (ErrorType, Evidence, FixPlan) paired with corrected actions for warm-start SFT. We then optimize reflection tokens and corrective actions jointly within local trajectories using group-relative advantages, and include a reflection-cost term to reduce unnecessary reflection. Experiments on GTA and GAIA show that, under the same backbone and tool suite, ReGRPO consistently outperforms strong open-source baselines and achieves the best results among the compared open-source controllers. Code and RoT data are available at this https URL .

Calibration, Not Compilation: Detecting and Repairing Misspecified Probabilistic Programs Written by Language Models

Jian Xu , Delu Zeng , John Paisley , Qibin Zhao

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem that LLM-written probabilistic programs can compile and run but be statistically wrong (e.g., wrong likelihood, invalid priors). It proposes using Bayesian workflow diagnostics (posterior predictive checks, simulation-based calibration, sampler diagnostics) as a calibration oracle to detect and repair misspecified models. For astronomy, this is directly applicable to automated generation and validation of SED fitting models, spectral fitting codes, or photometric redshift models where correct statistical specification is critical. A concrete workflow would be using an LLM to write a Bayesian SED fitting model in NumPyro, then running the calibration oracle to check for misspecification before applying to real JWST data. A key caveat is that the reference-free detection achieves only 62-78% AUC, so manual verification of the calibration diagnostics is still needed.

Reason

Directly addresses automated detection and repair of misspecified probabilistic programs, which is highly relevant for astronomical SED fitting, spectral fitting, and photometric redshift estimation where Bayesian models are common.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the specific Bayesian workflow diagnostics used (R-hat, ESS, divergences, posterior predictive checks) and how they are combined into a detection score
  • Check the repair loop implementation: how does the LLM receive calibration feedback and iteratively improve the model?
  • Examine the 14 misspecification types and whether they cover common astronomical modeling pitfalls (e.g., wrong noise model, incorrect prior support for physical parameters)
Abstract

Language models increasingly write probabilistic programs (in NumPyro, Stan, or Pyro), but a program that compiles, runs, and passes every unit test can still be \emph{statistically} wrong -- a Gaussian likelihood for heavy-tailed data, a Poisson for over-dispersed counts, an invalid prior support, or a pathological parameterization. The right verifier is therefore not a test suite but the Bayesian workflow itself: posterior predictive checks, simulation-based calibration, sampler diagnostics ($\hat R$, divergences, ESS), and held-out predictive density. We study this calibration oracle along three axes. \textbf{Detection:} on a benchmark of $14$ misspecification types across $10$ model families ($200$ instances), it flags the bug with AUC $0.97$ ($88\%$ at $2\%$ FPR \emph{when handed the correct reference program, an upper bound}) -- and a fully \emph{reference-free} version that uses no correct program reaches $62$--$78\%$ (the upper figure from a small automated model search), versus $0\%$ for a unit-test oracle. \textbf{Repair:} used as feedback in an LLM repair loop across fifteen models, calibration significantly outperforms unit-test feedback -- which is itself \emph{significantly worse than no feedback at all}, a passing test inducing false confidence that suppresses repair -- and improves over no feedback on strong-but-unsaturated models (GPT-5.1 $33{\to}92\%$, Claude $75{\to}100\%$; paired McNemar, $n{=}228$). \textbf{Reality:} on programs LLMs write from scratch for neutral briefs, $15$--$47\%$ of runnable ones are statistically misspecified (unit tests catch none), and calibration-guided repair significantly beats LLM-as-judge review, a Bayesian-workflow checklist, and data-summary self-debug. Across all three, the lesson is the same: for probabilistic programs, correctness is calibration, not compilation.

An Agentic AI Framework to Accelerate Scientific Discovery in Plant Phenotyping

Renan Souza , Daniel Rosendo , Kelsey Carter , John Lagergren , Frédéric Suter , Shelaine L. Curd , Gerald A. Tuskan , Rafael Ferreira da Silva , David Weston

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether an agentic AI framework can accelerate scientific discovery in high-throughput plant phenotyping, where data acquisition outpaces analysis. It presents an end-to-end system with a conversational Co-Scientist Agent that translates natural-language questions into structured analysis plans, and a Compute Agent that dispatches Vision Transformer segmentation and trait extraction on a supercomputer. The framework turns a days- to weeks-long analysis process into an interactive loop with results in seconds. For astronomy, this is directly relevant: JWST and other surveys generate data faster than scientists can analyze, and an agentic framework could automate tasks like source classification, SED fitting, or literature review. A concrete workflow would be asking a natural-language question like 'Find all little red dots with high EW(Halpha) in this JWST field' and having agents automatically run segmentation, extract spectra, and fit lines. However, the paper focuses on plant phenotyping (leaf segmentation, trait extraction) and uses domain-specific Vision Transformers. One should check if the framework is modular enough to swap in astronomical models (e.g., for galaxy morphology or spectral fitting) and whether the conversational agent can handle astronomical jargon.

Reason

This paper presents an agentic AI framework for scientific discovery in plant phenotyping, which directly parallels the need for automated analysis in astronomy, especially for high-throughput data.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the agentic framework is modular and allows swapping in custom models for astronomical tasks (e.g., source detection, SED fitting).
  • Look for details on how the conversational agent handles domain-specific terminology and complex multi-step workflows.
  • Verify whether the framework supports integration with astronomical databases (e.g., MAST, Vizier) and file formats (FITS).
  • Check if the end-to-end provenance tracking is compatible with reproducibility requirements in astronomy.
Abstract

High-throughput plant phenotyping now generates image derived datasets far faster than scientists can analyze them. At Oak Ridge National Laboratory's Advanced Plant Phenotyping Laboratory (APPL), automated stations image hundreds of plants daily across multiple remote sensing modalities; yet, trait extraction and interpretation remain manual, expert-bound, and strictly post-hoc, making analysis, not acquisition, the binding constraint on discovery. We present an end-to-end agentic AI framework that turns the facility from a data factory into an interactive autonomous, discovery platform, where scientists partner with AI agents to accelerate time to insight. A conversational Co-Scientist Agent translates a scientist's natural-language question into a structured analysis plan, and a headless Compute Agent dispatches Vision Transformer segmentation and trait extraction on the Frontier exascale supercomputer. The two agents run in separate security and resource domains and communicate over a secure, token-authenticated streaming channel, a design that accounts for the federation, data-movement, and provenance realities cloud-native agentic frameworks ignore, ensuring end-to-end provenance is captured for every interaction. The framework turns a days- to weeks-long analysis process into an interactive loop where agents reason over results, recommend next analyses, and respond to follow-up questions in seconds.
