RAGA: Reading-And-Graph-building-Agent for Autonomous Knowledge Graph Construction and Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Chengrui Han , Zesheng Cheng

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper proposes RAGA, an LLM-based autonomous framework for knowledge graph construction and retrieval-augmented generation. It provides an atomic toolset for full KG lifecycle operations and embeds a Read-Search-Verify-Construct cognitive constraint into a ReAct tool loop. A KG-vector synchronization mechanism enables hybrid symbolic-vector retrieval with evidence-anchored verification. For astronomy, this could automate building knowledge graphs from JWST papers and enable RAG systems for literature review. The framework is tested on the QASPER scientific QA dataset. A caveat is that preliminary experiments are on a subset of data, and scaling to full astronomical literature needs validation.

Reason

Directly relevant to retrieval-augmented generation and knowledge graph construction for scientific literature, which can be applied to astronomy research.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the quality of entity disambiguation and cross-chunk semantic relation capture in the KG construction.
  • Look for the evidence-anchored verification mechanism and how it ensures provenance.
  • Examine the hybrid retrieval performance on scientific QA tasks.
Abstract

Existing LLM-driven knowledge graph (KG) construction methods predominantly employ stateless batch processing pipelines, exhibiting structural deficiencies in cross-chunk semantic relation capture, entity disambiguation, and construction process interpretability. These limitations undermine KG quality, retrieval precision, and deployment trust in high-stakes domains. We propose RAGA (Reading And Graph-building Agent), an LLM-based autonomous KG construction and retrieval fusion framework. RAGA provides an atomic toolset supporting full KG lifecycle CRUD operations and embeds a Read-Search-Verify-Construct cognitive constraint into a ReAct tool loop. A KG-vector synchronization mechanism enables hybrid symbolic-vector retrieval, while evidence-anchored verification links every knowledge entry to its source text for auditable provenance. Preliminary experiments on a subset of the QASPER scientific QA dataset indicate that RAGA's fusion retrieval outperforms zero-shot baselines, with KG integration providing measurable gains in both answer and evidence quality. The framework design and experimental baseline serve as a reference for agent-driven autonomous KG construction.

ConflictRAG: Detecting and Resolving Knowledge Conflicts in Retrieval Augmented Generation

Chenyu Wang , Yingmin Liu , Yang Shu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem that RAG systems assume retrieved documents are mutually consistent, which often fails in practice. It proposes ConflictRAG, a framework that detects, classifies, and resolves knowledge conflicts before answer generation, using a two-stage detection (embedding MLP + selective LLM), an Entropy-TOPSIS source credibility assessment, and a Conflict-Aware RAG Score (CARS) for diagnostics. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because astronomical literature and databases often contain conflicting measurements (e.g., different redshifts for the same object, varying flux calibrations). An astronomer could use ConflictRAG to build a literature review agent that identifies and resolves contradictions in published results, or a data analysis agent that cross-checks multiple catalogs. A key caveat is that the framework is evaluated on general benchmarks, not on scientific or astronomical data; the source credibility assessment may need adaptation to scientific contexts (e.g., journal reputation, telescope reliability).

Reason

Directly relevant: conflict-aware RAG framework for detecting and resolving knowledge conflicts, crucial for reliable retrieval-augmented generation in astronomy literature review and data analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the two-stage conflict detection: what is the MLP classifier trained on, and how is the LLM refinement triggered?
  • Check the Entropy-TOPSIS framework: how are source credibility features defined, and could they be adapted to astronomical data sources (e.g., JWST vs. ground-based)?
  • Examine the CARS metric: does it capture conflict-handling capabilities relevant to scientific workflows?
  • Look for any experiments on domain-specific datasets (e.g., scientific literature, databases).
Abstract

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems implicitly assume mutual consistency among retrieved documents -- an assumption that frequently fails in practice. We present ConflictRAG, a conflict-aware RAG framework that detects, classifies, and resolves knowledge conflicts prior to answer generation. The framework introduces three contributions: (1) a two-stage conflict detection module combining a lightweight embedding-based MLP classifier with selective LLM refinement, reducing API costs by 62% while maintaining 90.8% detection accuracy; (2) an Entropy-TOPSIS framework for data-driven source credibility assessment, improving selection accuracy by 7.1% over manual heuristics; and (3) a Conflict-Aware RAG Score (CARS) for diagnostic evaluation of conflict-handling capabilities. Experiments on three benchmarks against six baselines demonstrate 88.7% conflict-detection F1 and consistent 5.3--6.1% correctness gains over the strongest conflict-aware baseline, with the pipeline transferring effectively across backbone LLMs.

Vector RAG vs LLM-Compiled Wiki: A Preregistered Comparison on a Small Multi-Domain Research

Theodore O. Cochran

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper compares Vector RAG and an LLM-compiled markdown wiki for answering questions over a small research corpus (24 papers). The wiki was better at connecting findings across papers, while RAG was better for single-fact lookup. Claim-level citation checking favored the wiki, but a decomposition-based RAG variant recovered most of the wiki's advantage at lower cost. This is directly relevant to building automated literature review tools for astronomy. For example, one could use such a system to synthesize results across JWST papers on high-redshift galaxies. The paper highlights that no single architecture is best on all metrics (organization, citation support, cost). The findings are based on a small corpus (24 papers), so scalability to larger astronomy literature needs testing.

Reason

Directly compares RAG and LLM-compiled wiki for research synthesis, which is relevant to automated literature review and scientific discovery.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the decomposition-based RAG variant's implementation details for potential adaptation.
  • Look for the specific LLM judge prompts used for scoring, to assess bias.
  • See if the corpus size and domain (research papers) are similar to a typical astronomy literature review.
  • Check the cost analysis: token usage for wiki vs. RAG, and whether the upfront build cost is justified.
Abstract

We preregistered a comparison of two ways to help an LLM answer questions over a small research corpus: a single-round Vector RAG system and an LLM-compiled markdown wiki. Both systems answered the same 13 questions over 24 papers using the same answer-generating model, and their answers were scored by blinded LLM judges. The wiki scored much better at connecting findings across papers, but its advantage in answer organization was not strong after judge adjustment. RAG met the preregistered test for single-fact lookup questions. The clean query-side cost result went against the expected wiki advantage: under the tested setup, the wiki used far more query tokens than RAG, so it could not recover any upfront build cost through cheaper queries. Two exploratory analyses changed how we interpret the result. First, claim-level citation checking favored the wiki: its cited pages more often supported the exact claims being made, even though RAG scored better on the overall groundedness rubric. Second, a decomposition-based RAG variant recovered most of the wiki's advantage on cross-paper synthesis at lower LLM-token cost, but it did not recover the wiki advantage in claim-by-claim citation support. The main conclusion is that grounded research synthesis is not a single capability. Systems can differ in how well they organize evidence, how well their citations support each claim, and how much they cost to run. In this study, no architecture was best on all three.

MLReplicate: Benchmarking Autonomous Research Systems for Machine Learning Reproducibility

Sasi Kiran Gaddipati , Diyana Muhammed , Farhana Keya , Gollam Rabby , Sören Auer

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether current autonomous research systems can reliably reproduce machine learning papers. It introduces MLReplicate, a benchmark built from ICML 2025 outstanding papers, and evaluates 6 systems (e.g., AI Scientist, Agent Laboratory) on manuscript generation. Key findings: automated reviews accepted 10/37 submissions, but human reviewers found methodological flaws and hallucinated results in 59% of accepted automated reviews; computational cost did not predict quality. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because similar autonomous agents could be used to reproduce or extend astrophysical analyses, e.g., reproducing JWST data reduction pipelines or SED fitting results. A concrete workflow: use an agent to re-run a published galaxy classification pipeline on new JWST data and generate a reproducible report. Caveat: the benchmark is limited to ML papers, and the systems may not yet handle domain-specific astronomy software (e.g., CASA, astropy) or large astronomical datasets.

Reason

Directly benchmarks autonomous research systems for ML reproducibility, highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether any evaluated system can handle domain-specific scientific software (e.g., astronomy libraries) or only generic ML frameworks.
  • Look for details on how human reviewers detected hallucinated results—this informs how to audit agent outputs in astronomy.
  • Examine the input specification format to see if it could be adapted to astronomy workflows (e.g., specifying a data reduction + analysis pipeline).
Abstract

Autonomous research systems capable of generating complete scientific manuscripts have advanced rapidly, yet robust and realistic evaluation frameworks have failed to keep pace. To bridge this gap, we introduce MLReplicate, an end-to-end benchmark evaluating autonomous research systems on machine learning reproducibility. The benchmark was constructed from ICML 2025 outstanding papers reformulated into standardized input specifications and evaluated across 6 state-of-the-art research systems: AI SCIENTIST-V1, AI SCIENTIST-V2, AGENT LABORATORY, CYCLERESEARCHER, AI RESEARCHER, and TINY SCIENTIST, yielding 45 generated manuscripts, with 3 failed experiments. Outputs are assessed using a dual-protocol approach that combines automated conference-style review and structured expert human evaluation, while tracking computational cost, runtime, and the amount of required human intervention. The automated conference-style review accepted 10 out of 37 valid submissions. An additional 8 submissions were desk-rejected before review for failing to meet the minimum page threshold. In contrast to automated reviews, human reviewers consistently identified methodological flaws, hallucinated experimental results, and reproducibility failures across all systems, and 59% of accepted automated reviews contained fabricated or unsupported claims. We further find that neither token budget nor computational cost predicts output quality: the cheapest system outperforms the most resource-intensive system in human evaluation, despite a 38-fold difference in input tokens. We thus demonstrate that autonomous research workflow design matters more than the scale of compute. MLReplicate exposes a substantial gap between current autonomous research systems and genuine scientific rigor, and establishes a practical, extensible evaluation framework for systematic progress toward trustworthy AI-driven scientific discovery.

In-context learning enables continental-scale subsurface temperature prediction from sparse local observations

Daniel O'Malley , Christopher W. Johnson , Javier E. Santos , Pablo Lara , Sandro Malusà , Bharat Srikishan , John Kath , Arnab Mazumder , Mohamed Mehana , David Coblentz , Nathan DeBardeleben , Earl Lawrence , Hari Viswanathan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether a transformer model can predict subsurface temperature at continental scale from sparse borehole measurements, without retraining for new regions. It introduces In-Context Earth, a transformer that uses sparse local borehole observations as 'in-context examples' to predict temperature-at-depth fields with calibrated uncertainty. The model achieves 4.7 °C MAE in the contiguous US, outperforming physics-informed and other baselines, and adapts to new regions (Alberta, Australia, UK) with only 20 local observations at inference time. For astronomy, this is highly relevant: the same in-context learning approach could be applied to predict photometric redshifts, stellar parameters, or galaxy properties from sparse spectroscopic calibration data, without per-survey retraining. A concrete workflow would be using a few hundred spectroscopic redshifts as in-context examples to predict redshifts for millions of galaxies in a new survey. However, the paper tests on a 1D regression task (temperature vs. depth), not on high-dimensional astronomical data (e.g., multi-band photometry, spectra), and the uncertainty calibration method may need adaptation.

Reason

Directly applies in-context learning with transformers to a geoscience problem (subsurface temperature prediction), demonstrating a method that could transfer to astronomical regression tasks with sparse observations.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for details on how the in-context examples are formatted and fed to the transformer (e.g., as a sequence of (x, y) pairs).
  • Check whether the uncertainty calibration method (Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic) is described and could be applied to astronomical regression.
  • Examine the model architecture: is it a standard causal transformer, and how does it handle variable-length context?
  • Look for any discussion of scaling to higher-dimensional inputs (e.g., multi-band photometry instead of 1D depth).
Abstract

Continental-scale knowledge of subsurface temperature is limited by the cost and sparsity of borehole measurements, but such information is essential for geothermal resource assessment and for understanding heat transport in the shallow crust. The thermal field reflects the interaction between lithology, crustal structure, radiogenic heat production, and advective fluid flow, sometimes producing sharp anomalies that are smoothed by conventional interpolation or difficult to capture with physical models. Here we introduce In-Context Earth, a transformer-based model that uses sparse local borehole observations as geological context to predict continuous temperature-at-depth fields with calibrated uncertainty. In the contiguous United States, the model achieves a mean absolute error of 4.7 °C, outperforming the physics-informed Stanford Thermal Model, a model based on AlphaEarth embeddings, the multimodal Transparent Earth model, and universal kriging, while resolving sharper thermal gradients in geothermal provinces. Its uncertainty estimates are well calibrated, with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic of 2.5%. Without finetuning, the model adapts to Alberta, Australia, and the United Kingdom (UK) using only 20 local observations at inference time, maintaining high accuracy in geologically distinct test regions with a mean absolute error of 2.2 °C in Alberta, 6.2 °C in Australia, and 5.4 °C in the UK. Interpretability analyses show that the model learns internal representations of subsurface properties it never observes during training, including seismic velocities, geochemistry, and crustal structure, and uses these representations in physically consistent ways. More broadly, this work shows that in-context learning can use sparse borehole observations for continental-scale subsurface characterization, without requiring dense measurements or region-specific retraining.

ArtifactLinker: Linking Scientific Artifacts for Automatic State-of-the-Art Discovery

Haofei Yu , Jiaxuan You , Peter Clark , Bodhisattwa Prasad Majumder , Kyle Richardson

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks how to automatically discover the state-of-the-art model for a given dataset by leveraging scientific artifacts on platforms like HuggingFace. The main idea is a two-stage framework: first, use graph neural networks or graph-augmented LLMs to rank unobserved model-dataset links, then use LLM-based coding agents to verify top-ranked links. This matters for astronomy because it could automate the process of finding the best model for a specific astronomical dataset or task, such as source classification or redshift estimation. A concrete workflow would be using ArtifactLinker to automatically identify the best pre-trained model for JWST image segmentation or spectral fitting. A limitation is that the benchmark focuses on HuggingFace artifacts, which may not yet include many astronomy-specific models.

Reason

Directly addresses automatic discovery of state-of-the-art models using graph neural networks and LLM agents, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the benchmark composition: what fraction of artifacts are from scientific domains vs. general computer vision/NLP?
  • Look for the success rate of the LLM-based verification agents on scientific tasks.
  • Examine whether the graph structure captures meaningful relationships between astronomical datasets and models.
Abstract

Scientific artifacts such as models and datasets are foundations for research. With the rapid growth of platforms like HuggingFace, researchers now have access to a large number of artifacts. Yet, a key challenge remains: how can we automatically discover the state-of-the-art (SOTA) model for a given dataset by fully leveraging existing artifacts? We formalize this task as automatic SOTA discovery by modeling HuggingFace as an artifact graph, where nodes are models/datasets and edges represent evaluations. We propose ArtifactLinker, a two-stage framework: (1) ranking promising unobserved model--dataset links using Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) or graph-augmented Large Language Models (LLMs), and (2) verifying top-ranked links via coding experiments with LLM-based agents. We further introduce a benchmark named ArtifactBench with 14,053 artifacts and 51,337 relations to evaluate the performance of both stages. Results show that (1) graph structures between existing artifacts are effective for missing link prediction; (2) end-to-end ranking and verification with ArtifactLinker help discover potential SOTA results and research insights.

1GC-7RC: One Graphic Card -- Seven Research Challenges! How Good Are AI Agents at Doing Your Job?

Robin-Nico Kampa , Fabian Deuser , Anna Bößendörfer , Konrad Habel , Norbert Oswald

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces 1GC-7RC, a benchmark for evaluating AI coding agents on seven ML tasks including language modeling, image classification, semantic segmentation, graph learning, tabular prediction, time-series forecasting, and text classification. Agents must design, implement, and train models from scratch on a single GPU with no pretrained weights or internet access. The benchmark tests implicit ML knowledge, planning, and time-budget management. For astronomy, this could help evaluate agents that automate data analysis pipelines for JWST or survey data. The modular design allows adding new tasks like astronomical source classification or SED fitting. A caveat is that the benchmark currently lacks astronomy-specific tasks, but the framework is extensible.

Reason

Directly evaluates AI coding agents on ML tasks including tabular prediction and time-series, which could transfer to astronomical data analysis and automated scientific discovery.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the benchmark tasks (tabular prediction, time-series) are similar to astronomical regression/classification problems.
  • Look for the modular extension mechanism and whether new tasks can be added easily.
  • Examine agent performance on tabular prediction and time-series tasks specifically.
Abstract

Autonomous AI coding agents are becoming a core tool for ML practitioners in industry and research alike. Despite this growing adoption, no standardized benchmark exists to evaluate their ability to design, implement, and train models from scratch across diverse domains. We introduce **1GC-7RC** (*Single Graphic Card: Seven Research Challenges*), a benchmark comprising seven ML tasks spanning language modeling, image classification, semantic segmentation, graph learning, tabular prediction, time-series forecasting, and text classification. Each task provides a locked data-preparation and evaluation script together with a baseline training script; the agent may only modify the training code, has no access to pretrained weights (with one controlled exception for semantic segmentation), no internet access, and must complete each task within a task-specific wall-clock budget (40-120 minutes) on a single GPU. We evaluate seven coding agents: five proprietary (Claude Code with Sonnet 4.6, Opus 4.6, and Opus 4.7; Codex CLI with GPT 5.5; and OpenCode with Qwen 3.6+) and two open-source (OpenCode with Kimi K2.5, Kimi K2.6). Across 5 runs per agent-task pair, we report substantial performance differences that reveal varying levels of implicit ML knowledge, planning ability, and time-budget management. The benchmark, harness, and all evaluation artifacts are publicly available on GitHub at this https URL to facilitate reproducible comparison of future agents. Because our benchmark design is modular, the benchmark can be extended to new tasks and domains, adapted to different GPU budgets, and used to study multi-agent settings, making it a flexible platform for future research on autonomous research agents.

State-of-the-Art Claims Require State-of-the-Art Evidence

YongKyung Oh

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper identifies a widespread claim-evidence gap in AI benchmarking, showing that SOTA claims often rest on fragile evidence like marginal mean score improvements. The main finding is that in over half of top-model comparisons, at least one assumed property of superiority (e.g., consistency, robustness) does not hold. For astronomy, this is crucial when evaluating models for source classification or photometric redshifts, as benchmark performance may not generalize. One could use this framework to critically assess claims about new models for JWST data analysis. However, the paper is meta-analytical and does not propose new methods. Prerequisites include familiarity with benchmark evaluation practices.

Reason

Critically examines SOTA claims in AI/ML benchmarks, which is directly relevant to evaluating models for astronomical tasks.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the paper provides specific examples of fragile SOTA claims in domains relevant to astronomy.
  • Look for the proposed guidelines for more honest reporting of results.
Abstract

State-of-the-Art (SOTA) claims pervade Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) research. These claims rest on benchmark evaluations, where models are ranked by aggregate scores across tasks. Public benchmarks or leaderboards are the most visible instance, but the same structure appears in paper tables throughout the literature. However, such minimal evidence often cannot support these strong claims. We identify a widespread claim-evidence gap in AI benchmarking. Claiming SOTA carries implicit assumptions beyond mean score superiority, suggesting that a model meaningfully outperforms alternatives across most tasks. However, a marginal improvement in the mean score merely indicates a top average rank rather than true superiority. Analyzing ten cross-domain benchmarks from public leaderboards, we found that in more than half of top-model comparisons, at least one commonly assumed property of superiority does not hold. These properties include meaningful effect size, consistency across tasks, or robustness to dataset removal. Instead, aggregate gains are frequently driven by outlier datasets. This fragility persists even in benchmarks with many tasks. We argue that claim language should reflect the strength of the underlying evidence. This requires no additional experiments, only honest reporting of what results actually show, enabling more precise and interpretable comparisons across models.
