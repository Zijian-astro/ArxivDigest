Self-Supervised Representation Learning: From Spectral Foundation Models to Auroral Emission Spectra

Matthieu Le Lain , Gaël Cessateur , Sébastien Lefèvre

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem that auroral spectrographs record hundreds of thousands of emission spectra but only a few hundred can be expert-labeled, so most data go unused. The core idea is to pretrain a 1D Vision Transformer with a masked autoencoder, a self-supervised method that hides parts of the input spectrum and trains the model to reconstruct them, forcing it to learn useful internal representations without labels. Without any labels, the learned representation recovers emission-line intensity ratios that physicists use to diagnose precipitating particles, and with a simple linear probe it matches expert-designed features; fine-tuned, it beats the previous supervised classifier. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because JWST and ground-based spectrographs face the same label scarcity, and the paper explicitly tests whether astronomical spectral foundation models (SpectraFM, SpecFormer) transfer, finding that spectral-window overlap matters. A concrete workflow would be pretraining a masked-autoencoder ViT on unlabeled JWST NIRSpec or MIRI spectra and then fine-tuning for redshift estimation, AGN classification, or line-ratio diagnostics. The caveat is that auroral spectra are optical and relatively low-resolution compared to JWST, so you should check whether the architecture and pretraining scale up to noisier, higher-dimensional astronomical spectra.

Reason

This paper is directly about self-supervised spectral representation learning, pretraining a 1D ViT with a masked autoencoder on unlabeled spectra, and even tests transfer from astronomical spectral foundation models. It strongly matches your interests in spectral fitting, foundation models, and JWST spectroscopy analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the masked-autoencoder architecture details and whether they scale to JWST spectral resolution and noise levels
  • Look for the transfer results from astronomical spectral foundation models and what spectral-window mismatch implies
  • Assess the linear-probe and fine-tuning protocols for label efficiency
  • See whether attribution methods identify physically meaningful spectral features
Abstract

Auroral spectrographs such as the Auroral Spectrograph In Skibotn (ASIS) record hundreds of thousands of emission spectra, but only a few hundred can be labelled by an expert. To exploit the rest, we pretrain a 1D Vision Transformer with a masked autoencoder on 223,000 unlabelled spectra. Without labels, its representation recovers the emission-line intensity ratios that physicists use to diagnose the precipitating particles (R^2 0.91 vs. 0.77 for an untrained control) and, under one linear probe, classifies as well as 13 features designed by experts. Fine-tuned, the model outperforms the previous supervised auroral classifier on its own benchmark (macro-AP 88.5 vs. 77.8), reaches 0.870 mAP, and exceeds the same architecture trained from scratch by +0.159 with 10% of the labels; attribution shows that it uses both N2+ bands. Could an existing pretrained model replace it? Two astronomical spectral foundation models and a time-series model transfer according to their spectral window: SpectraFM, trained in the infrared, falls below the untrained control, whereas SpecFormer, trained in the optical, approaches in-domain pretraining without reaching it.

AutoResearch at Production Scale: Failure Modes and a Multi-Agent Framework

Aparajith Chandran , Juwon Kim , Saurav Jha , Pablo Castells , Florian Hottier

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how to make Karpathy's AutoResearch paradigm—an LLM that repeatedly edits a training script and keeps changes that improve a held-out metric—actually work when each iteration costs hours of multi-GPU compute and campaigns run for weeks. The main idea is a three-principle scaffolding called prevent, persist, redirect, which maps five observed failure modes (infrastructure fragility, agent memory decay, search-direction stagnation, iteration-cost asymmetry, and metric fixation) to structural remedies rather than just better prompts. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because automated discovery agents for JWST or Rubin will face the same long-horizon, expensive-iteration regime, and the failure taxonomy is a useful checklist for designing robust research agents. A concrete workflow would be to wrap an LLM agent around an SED-fitting or photometric-redshift pipeline, letting it propose and test configurations while the scaffolding prevents runaway or stagnating searches. The main caveat is that the study is on a book recommendation pipeline, so the specific remedies may need adaptation; also, the reported gains are against hand-tuned baselines, not against strong Bayesian optimization or human experts. Before investing time, check whether the scaffolding is described in enough detail to reimplement, and whether the failure modes are diagnosed with quantitative evidence rather than anecdotes.

Reason

Directly addresses LLM-driven automated research loops at production scale, including failure modes and scaffolding that transfer to astronomy research agents and automated scientific discovery workflows. The multi-agent framework and long-horizon campaign management are relevant to building reliable LLM agents for JWST/survey analysis pipelines.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the concrete definition and implementation of the prevent/persist/redirect scaffolding, and whether it is general enough to port to a non-recommendation domain.
  • Check how each failure mode is detected and quantified, and whether the diagnostics rely on metrics that would exist in an astronomy pipeline.
  • Look for the cost accounting: how many GPU-hours per experiment, how the agent decides to retain or discard a modification, and how the held-out metric is protected from overfitting.
  • Check whether the paper discusses transfer to domains with different data modalities or evaluation criteria, and whether any code or configuration is released.
Abstract

Optimizing embedding systems for production recommendation pipelines demands systematic exploration that consumes disproportionate engineering effort at scale. We apply Andrej Karpathy's AutoResearch paradigm -- a large language model that iteratively edits a training script and retains modifications that improve a held-out scalar metric -- to automate this exploration. We report on twelve weeks of running this paradigm at production scale, where iterations consume hours of multi-GPU compute, evaluation involves competing criteria, and campaigns span weeks across many training jobs. Across two independently developed representation-learning systems for a book recommendation pipeline, we ran 220+ experiments and observed five recurring failure modes absent from the original setting: infrastructure fragility, agent memory decay, search-direction stagnation, iteration-cost asymmetry, and metric fixation. We contribute a three-principle scaffolding design -- prevent, persist, redirect -- that maps each failure mode to a structural remedy and whose instantiation scales with iteration cost. The framework produced a 1.82x Recall@6 lift and a 2.1x coherence lift over hand-tuned baselines, and the agent autonomously designed a text-only fallback that expanded catalog coverage by 5.8x. The two systems span nearly three orders of magnitude in per-iteration cost yet exhibit the same failure modes, suggesting these are structural properties of production-scale autonomous research rather than artifacts of either application.

Epstein Files Engine: Agentic Search for Investigative Journalism

Duy K. Nguyen , Teresa Mondría Terol , Dylan Freedman , Zach Seward

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper describes an AI agent built by The New York Times to investigate a large release of mixed-media documents, translating reporter questions into SQL queries over document, archive, and headline corpora. The agent uses an LLM to plan queries and returns citation-rich answers that a reporter can verify, and more than 100 journalists used it, contributing to at least 20 published stories. A key technical piece is Diff, a text-and-visual duplicate matching method that amplifies novelty signals so the system surfaces genuinely new information rather than repeated content. For astronomy, this maps almost directly onto literature and archive triage: an agent that queries ADS, arXiv, and observatory logs, deduplicates near-identical results, and returns cited answers for a researcher. A concrete workflow could be an agent that searches a large JWST or HST document set, identifies novel claims, and produces a verifiable summary with links to source pages. The caveat is that the paper is a deployment report, not a controlled benchmark, and the duplicate-matching method is tuned to news documents and PDFs, so its behavior on scientific figures and spectra is untested.

Reason

This is a deployed LLM agent for investigative journalism over a large mixed-media corpus, with citation-rich answers and duplicate detection; it is a strong template for LLM agents doing literature review and document triage in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the query-planning and citation-verification loop, and whether it can be adapted to ADS or arXiv APIs
  • Check the Diff duplicate-matching method and whether it handles figures, tables, and spectra rather than just text and images
  • See how reporters interacted with the agent and what failure modes were observed in practice
  • Check whether the system architecture is described in enough detail to reimplement for a scientific corpus
Abstract

On Jan. 30, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice released a mixed-media collection concerning Jeffrey Epstein, including about three million pages of PDFs. We describe the Epstein Files Engine, an A.I. agent The New York Times deployed to investigate the files. The Engine translated reporter questions into Google BigQuery SQL queries across three corpora: Epstein-related releases, the Times's archive and external, Epstein-related news headlines. It used an LLM to plan queries and returned citation-rich answers a reporter could verify and trust. More than 100 journalists used the Engine, and it contributed to at least 20 published stories. We report how reporters queried it and describe Diff, our text-and-visual duplicate matching method that amplified novelty signals and allowed the Engine to surface genuinely new information. We argue that newsroom agents serve newsrooms best not as autonomous writers, but as interfaces to source material and institutional knowledge.

Structure-Guided Masked Autoencoders for Ultra-High Resolution Scientific Image Understanding

Enzhi Zhang , Du Wu , Rui Zhong , Cong Ma , Isaac Lyngaas , Amir Koushyar Ziabari , Xiao Wang , Peng Chen , Tao Luo , Toshio Endo , Fumiyoshi Shoji , Kento Sato , Kentaro Uesugi , Takayuki Nonoyama , Ryuji Kiyama , Masahiro Yoshida , Masaru Tezuka , Tetsuya Ishikawa , Satoshi Matsuoka , Masaharu Munetomo , Mohamed Wahib

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tries to make masked autoencoders practical for gigapixel scientific images, where random masking and uniform tokenization are poorly matched to structured, multi-scale morphology. It proposes SGMA, which combines a content-adaptive quadtree tokenizer that compresses gigapixel images into a fixed-length sequence with a structure-conditioned masking process that biases reconstruction toward spatially informative regions. A Damped Accumulation mechanism aggregates signal-dependent responses across the tree into a structure canvas used to guide masking, keeping the pretraining task compatible with standard ViT encoders. Across electron microscopy, whole-slide optical microscopy, and X-ray CT datasets, SGMA outperforms MAE baselines, achieving 95.68 percent Dice on an 8K x 8K x 28K dataset and 83.21 percent Dice on a 32K squared dataset, with up to 24.8 times inference speedup. For astronomy, this is directly relevant to JWST mosaics, Roman and Euclid survey images, and any gigapixel astronomical image where morphology is hierarchical and spatially structured. A concrete workflow would be pretraining SGMA on unlabeled JWST or HST mosaics, then fine-tuning for source detection, morphology classification, or anomaly detection. The main caveat is that the paper evaluates on microscopy and CT data, so you should check whether the quadtree tokenizer and masking strategy transfer to astronomical backgrounds, point sources, and noise properties.

Reason

This paper is about structure-guided masked autoencoders for ultra-high-resolution scientific images, including gigapixel microscopy and X-ray CT. It is highly relevant to JWST and survey image analysis because it addresses self-supervised pretraining on large, structured scientific images with multi-scale morphology.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the quadtree tokenizer can handle astronomical point sources, extended galaxies, and diffuse emission
  • Look for the structure-conditioned masking details and whether they rely on domain-specific signal statistics that may differ in astronomy
  • Assess whether the method has been tested on images with high dynamic range or sparse sources like JWST deep fields
  • Check whether the paper provides code and pretrained models that could be adapted to astronomical datasets
Abstract

Self-supervised pre-training with Vision Transformers, including Masked Autoencoders (MAE), is difficult to apply to gigapixel scientific images. Random masking is poorly matched to the structured, multi-scale morphology of scientific data, while uniform tokenization produces prohibitively long sequences that make $O(N^2)$ attention impractical. We propose SGMA, a structure-guided masked autoencoding framework for ultra-high-resolution scientific images. SGMA couples two components: a content-adaptive quadtree tokenizer that compresses gigapixel images into a fixed-length sequence, and a structure-conditioned masking process that biases reconstruction toward spatially informative regions. To stabilize this process across scales, we introduce Damped Accumulation (DA), which aggregates signal-dependent responses across the tree into a structure canvas used to guide masking. The resulting pre-training task preserves fine microstructure while remaining compatible with standard ViT encoders and MAE-style reconstruction. Across electron microscopy, whole-slide optical microscopy, and X-ray CT datasets, SGMA consistently outperforms MAE baselines. It achieves 95.68% Dice on the 8K x 8K x 28K SpringXCT dataset, improving over the same-architecture MAE baseline by +13.00 points, and 83.21% Dice on the 32K^2 WSI PAIP dataset, improving by +16.84 points, while providing up to a 24.8x inference speedup.

Learning What to Skip: Counterfactual Credit Assignment for Efficient Multi-Agent LLM Workflows

Jinfeng Xu , Zheyu Chen , Ziyue Peng , Zheng Lin , Shuo Yang , Jinze Li , Zheng Xing , Mengran Li , Victor C. M. Leung

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that multi-agent LLM workflows often execute every planning, execution, verification, and summarization step even when some steps are redundant or harmful. Its main idea is Learning What to Skip, which treats component omission as counterfactual credit assignment: full-workflow logs show the reward of the executed trajectory, while controlled skip interventions reveal what happens if a future step is omitted. The method learns action-specific safety models from these interventions and combines calibration with domain-native guards to decide when to skip. This matters for astronomy because an LLM research agent for JWST or survey analysis could waste tokens and time running redundant checks, or could overwrite a correct intermediate result. A concrete workflow would be an agent that plans a spectral-fitting or source-classification pipeline, executes steps, verifies outputs, and skips a verification or summarization step when the counterfactual safety model says it is safe. The main caveat is that the paper evaluates on math reasoning, multiple-choice QA, and code generation, so I should check whether the skip logic depends on task-specific guards that would need redesign for astronomical data.

Reason

This paper is directly relevant to my interest in LLM agents for scientific workflows, because it studies how to skip unnecessary components in multi-agent LLM pipelines while preserving accuracy. The counterfactual credit-assignment idea could transfer to astronomy research agents that plan, execute, verify, and summarize data-analysis tasks.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the exact definition of counterfactual skip interventions and whether they require repeated full-workflow runs
  • Check whether the safety models are trained per task or can generalize across domains
  • Look for evidence on shared-error cases and whether agreement among agents is explicitly shown to be insufficient
  • Assess whether the method could be integrated into an astronomy LLM agent with tools for SED fitting, spectral fitting, or catalog querying
Abstract

Multi-agent LLM workflows use planning, execution, verification, and summarization to improve task performance, yet the value of each component depends on the state already produced. Executing every component can waste computation or overwrite a correct intermediate answer. We formulate component omission as counterfactual credit assignment: full-workflow logs reveal the executed trajectory's reward, while controlled skip interventions reveal the consequences of omitting a future step. We introduce Learning What to Skip (LW2S), which learns action-specific safety models from these interventions and combines held-out calibration with domain-native guards to select skips. When an early skip is rejected, the controller can continue execution and reconsider a later component. Across mathematical reasoning, multiple-choice QA, and code generation with two instruction-model families, LW2S reduces recorded token cost while matching or improving aggregate full-workflow accuracy in the evaluated settings. Scale-up and second-topology experiments further examine component redundancy, while shared-error cases reveal why agreement alone is insufficient for skip selection. These findings connect efficient workflow execution to learning the conditional utility of individual components.

ALF: An Active Learning Framework for Scientific Discovery

Shikha Surana , Alex Hawkins-Hooker , Olivia Gallup , Christoph Brunken , Jules Tilly , Paul Duckworth

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that machine learning for science is usually limited by data, and producing high-quality labeled data under budget constraints is expensive. Active learning (AL) is the strategy of letting the model choose which examples to label next, which helps when labels require costly experiments, measurements, or simulations. The authors present ALF, a modular framework with five components that runs the full data-acquisition loop, supporting both offline experimentation against an existing dataset and online deployment against an oracle. For astronomy, this is directly applicable: JWST spectra, follow-up spectroscopy, and expert visual classifications are all expensive labels, so an AL loop could prioritize which targets to observe or label. A concrete workflow would be using ALF to select which candidate little red dots or high-redshift galaxy candidates get scarce spectroscopic follow-up. The caveat to check is whether the framework is general enough to plug into astronomical data loaders and whether the online oracle interface can wrap a telescope or simulation.

Reason

Active learning for scientific discovery directly matches your interest in automated scientific discovery and efficient data acquisition, which is highly relevant for expensive JWST spectroscopy and survey follow-up. The modular offline/online framework could be adapted to astronomical observing or labeling campaigns.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the five modular components have clean APIs for custom astronomical datasets and oracles
  • Look for experiments on real scientific domains versus only standard AL benchmarks
  • Assess how it handles batch acquisition and budget constraints relevant to telescope time
  • See if uncertainty estimation and acquisition functions are customizable
Abstract

Machine learning for scientific discovery is almost systematically data bound. Producing relevant high quality data, under budget constraints, is amongst the most promising ways to advance the field. Active learning (AL) offers promise wherever labelling requires expensive experiment, measurement, or simulation. Most existing tools cover only part of the data acquisition loop, and typically focus on either offline benchmarking or online deployment, but not both. We present ALF, a modular AL Framework that runs the full data acquisition loop via five modular components. One clear API for both settings: offline, against an existing dataset for controlled and reproducible experimentation; and online, against an oracle for acquiring new candidates in real-world deployments. ALF is open-source and available at this https URL .
