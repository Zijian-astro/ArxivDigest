HG-RAG: Hierarchy-Guided Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Structured Knowledge Graphs

Pranav Yadav

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of retrieving structured context for LLMs when queries require hierarchical or relational reasoning. HG-RAG performs graph traversal over a hierarchical knowledge graph, expanding context upward, laterally, and downward from a named entity anchor. For astronomy, this could enable querying hierarchical ontologies (e.g., galaxy types, spectral classes) or relational databases (e.g., multi-wavelength catalogs). An astronomer could use it to build a RAG system that retrieves relevant papers, object properties, or observational constraints from a structured knowledge base. The method is evaluated on small graphs (18-800 nodes), so scalability to large astronomical catalogs needs verification.

Reason

Directly addresses retrieval-augmented generation with hierarchical knowledge graphs, which is relevant to structured astronomical knowledge (e.g., catalogs, ontologies).

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the graph traversal method scales to graphs with millions of nodes typical in astronomy.
  • Look for evaluation on real-world hierarchical datasets, not just synthetic ones.
  • Examine the retrieval pipeline's ability to handle ambiguous entity names (e.g., multiple objects with same name).
Abstract

Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) has proven to be a widely successful process at improving the quality of outputs from a Large Language Model (LLM) for wider context. However, RAG systems typically retrieve context from flat document stores, which struggles when queries require hierarchical or relational reasoning across structured knowledge. I present HG-RAG (Hierarchy-Guided RAG), a framework that performs graph-traversal over a hierarchical knowledge graph to deliver structured context to a language model. My retrieval pipeline resolves a named entity anchor from the query, then expands context upward through parent nodes, laterally through relational neighbors, and downward through child nodes when needed. I evaluate HG-RAG against a dense retrieval baseline across three world scales (18-800 nodes) with four query types: local fact, hierarchical, neighborhood, and multi-hop. Results show HG-RAG consistently outperforms the flat baseline on hierarchical, relational, and multi-hop reasoning tasks, while reducing hallucination and maintaining locality coherence.

PReM: Learning What to Preserve and When to Refresh for Context Compression

Bohan Yu , Lei Shen , Chenxi Zhou , Chen Han , Junlin Liu , Wenbo Su , Yu Cheng , Bo Zheng

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of efficiently compressing long context (e.g., 32K tokens) for LLMs without losing useful information for later reasoning steps. The core idea is a framework called PReM that maintains the long context as internal layer-wise key-value memory, learning what to preserve and when to refresh using a special memory token. For astronomy, this could help process lengthy survey papers, multi-object spectra catalogs, or JWST observation logs in a single LLM context window. A concrete workflow might be feeding a stack of 50+ astronomical papers or a large source catalog into an LLM agent for literature review or data extraction. A caveat is that the method is demonstrated on general NLP tasks, not scientific documents, so domain-specific testing would be needed.

Reason

Directly addresses context compression for long-context LLMs, which is relevant for processing large astronomical documents or survey data.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the compression method preserves numerical precision for tabular data (e.g., fluxes, redshifts).
  • Look for ablation studies on the refresh token's behavior with domain-specific text.
  • Evaluate if the method can handle mixed text and tables common in astronomical literature.
Abstract

Efficient long-context inference is not only about reducing memory cost, but also about keeping useful contextual evidence accessible as generation proceeds. However, existing compression-oriented approaches, such as key-value (KV) cache compression and context compression, often either make an early decision about which contextual information to keep or rely on an external compressor. Such designs make it difficult to adapt the compressed context to the evidence needed by later reasoning steps. This paper introduces PReM (Preserve and Refresh Memory), a context-compression framework that maintains the long context as the model's internal layer-wise KV memory and learns what to preserve and when to refresh it. Specifically, PReM uses a dedicated memory layer to make memory-selection decisions, and a special memory token <m> to trigger refreshes during generation. To train this behavior, PReM introduces Phase-Separated Refresh Training, aligning memory selection with memory-conditioned generation while preserving continuity across refreshes. Experiments with 32K-token contexts show that PReM outperforms strong baselines under both 16x and 32x compression, while maintaining a favorable balance between answer quality and inference efficiency.

MARS: Multi-hop Adaptive Retrieval and SPARQL Generation for KGQA

Nikit Srivastava , Daniel Vollmers , René Speck , Nikolaos Karalis , Hamada M. Zahera , Axel-Cyrille Ngonga Ngomo

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of hallucination in LLMs when answering knowledge-intensive questions by combining them with knowledge graphs (KGs). It proposes MARS, a scalable KGQA approach that performs structured, iterative retrieval from a KG to generate SPARQL queries, without requiring model fine-tuning. For astronomy, this could enable querying astronomical knowledge bases (e.g., SIMBAD, NED, or a custom KG built from JWST catalogs) using natural language, allowing researchers to ask complex multi-hop questions like 'find all high-redshift galaxies with detected AGN in the GOODS field that have JWST spectroscopy.' A concrete workflow would be: (1) build a KG from astronomical catalogs and literature, (2) use MARS to allow an LLM agent to answer user queries by traversing the KG and generating SPARQL, (3) integrate this into a research assistant tool. A limitation is that MARS requires a pre-existing KG with structured entities and relations, which may need significant upfront effort to construct from astronomical data.

Reason

This paper directly addresses combining LLMs with knowledge graphs for question answering, which is highly relevant to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and scientific knowledge base querying for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the method supports dynamic or user-defined KGs, or if it requires a fixed schema
  • Look for evaluation on domain-specific KGs (e.g., scientific or biomedical) to assess transferability to astronomy
  • Examine the computational cost of the iterative retrieval process for large KGs typical in astronomy
  • Check if the SPARQL generation is robust to complex astronomical queries (e.g., spatial joins, cone searches)
Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong reasoning performance, but their tendency to hallucinate limits their reliability in knowledge-intensive tasks requiring up-to-date and grounded information. Combining knowledge graphs (KGs) with LLMs facilitates the use of explicit symbolic knowledge that can be continuously updated without costly fine-tuning, while benefiting from rapidly advancing LLM reasoning. We propose MARS, a scalable knowledge graph question answering (KGQA) approach that requires no model fine-tuning. Rather than relying on open-ended agentic exploration, MARS performs a structured retrieval procedure that links question entities to the KG and iteratively retrieves relevant next-hop information. At each step, MARS decides whether to continue graph traversal or to generate the final SPARQL query, allowing the model to adapt the retrieval depth to the question while keeping the overall pipeline more predictable than fully agentic approaches. We evaluate MARS on three established KGQA benchmarks across several LLMs and settings, including multilingual evaluation, and provide insights through ablation studies and error analysis. Our approach achieves competitive performance relative to state-of-the-art methods while remaining efficient and scalable. The evaluation results, code and resources are publicly available: this https URL .

SEED: Self-Evolving On-Policy Distillation for Agentic Reinforcement Learning

Jinyang Wu , Shuo Yang , Zhengxi Lu , Fan Zhang , Yuhao Shen , Lang Feng , Haoran Luo , Zheng Lian , Shuai Zhang , Zhengqi Wen , Jianhua Tao

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper proposes SEED, a framework that converts completed agent trajectories into hindsight skills and distills them back into the policy model during reinforcement learning. This allows the agent to learn from its own successes and failures, improving sample efficiency and generalization. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because it could be used to train LLM agents that perform automated data analysis, literature review, or code generation for JWST data analysis. The agent could learn reusable workflows for SED fitting, spectral classification, or anomaly detection. A key limitation is that the method is demonstrated on text-based and vision-based agentic tasks, not scientific data, and would require adaptation to astronomical workflows. The computational cost of on-policy RL with large models may also be a barrier.

Reason

Self-evolving on-policy distillation for agentic RL is highly relevant to building LLM agents for scientific workflows, including automated data analysis and literature review in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the specific mechanism of hindsight skill extraction and whether it could be applied to astronomical analysis workflows (e.g., extracting reusable analysis steps).
  • Check the sample efficiency gains reported and assess whether they would translate to the smaller datasets typical in astronomy.
  • Examine the generalization results to unseen scenarios to understand how robust the learned skills are.
  • Look for any discussion of the computational requirements (GPU hours, model size) to assess feasibility for astronomy groups.
Abstract

Large language models are increasingly trained as interactive agents for long-horizon tasks involving multi-turn interaction, tool use, and environment feedback. Outcome-based reinforcement learning (RL) provides a practical optimization paradigm, but its sparse trajectory-level rewards offer limited guidance on intermediate decisions, leaving a supervision gap between episode-level outcomes and token-level policy learning. We propose SEED (SElf-Evolving On-Policy Distillation), a self-evolving framework that converts completed on-policy trajectories into training-time hindsight skills and distills their behavioral effect back into the policy model. SEED first fine-tunes the policy to analyze completed trajectories and generate natural-language skills that capture reusable workflows, decisive observations, or failure-avoidance rules. During RL, the current policy both collects trajectories and serves as the analyzer that extracts hindsight skills from them. Policy updates therefore improve subsequent decision making and skill analysis together, allowing hindsight supervision to evolve with the policy. SEED then re-scores the sampled actions under ordinary and skill-augmented contexts, converting the skill-induced probability shift into a dense token-level on-policy distillation signal. This signal is jointly optimized with outcome-based RL, keeping the auxiliary supervision aligned with the current trajectory distribution. Extensive experiments on text-based and vision-based agentic tasks show that SEED consistently improves performance and sample efficiency, exhibiting robust generalization to unseen scenarios. Our code is available at this https URL .

BrainPilot: Automating Brain Discovery with Agentic Research

Haoxuan Li , Tianci Gao , Jianhe Li , Yang Fan , Runze Shi , Weiran Wang , Tianxiang Zhao , Zezhao Wu , Xiaoyang Jiang , Qihui Zhang , Jia Li , Xiao Xiao , Kai Du , Xiaoxuan Jia , Chao Xie , Lu Mi

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper presents BrainPilot, a multi-agent AI system designed to accelerate brain science research by coordinating specialist agents for literature review, data analysis, and result interpretation. The system uses a principal investigator agent to delegate tasks, a knowledge base of 7,233 indexed items, and a skill library of 72 methodology units. All steps are recorded in a Graph of Trace for auditability, and an Auditor agent checks for fabricated claims. For astronomy, this framework could be adapted to manage complex workflows like JWST data reduction, spectral fitting, or multi-wavelength catalog cross-matching. An astronomer could imagine using BrainPilot to automate a literature search on high-redshift galaxies, then run SED fitting code, and compile results with traceable provenance. A limitation is that the current knowledge base and skills are specific to brain science, requiring significant curation for astronomy. The system's performance depends on the underlying LLM and may require fine-tuning on astronomical literature.

Reason

BrainPilot is a multi-agent system for scientific research that integrates literature review, data analysis, and result verification. This directly aligns with interests in LLM agents for scientific workflows, and the open-source, auditable design is valuable for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for details on how the Graph of Trace records tool use and evidence, which is crucial for reproducibility.
  • Check if the system can be extended with custom tools (e.g., for SED fitting or spectral analysis) without modifying the core architecture.
  • Examine the evaluation metrics: are they focused on task completion, accuracy, or scientific insight?
Abstract

Understanding the brain increasingly depends on integrating evidence across scales, modalities, and disciplines. Addressing a single research question therefore requires a coordinated sequence of operations, from surveying prior work to executing analyses and interpreting results in light of domain knowledge. AI agents promise to accelerate this process, but current agents lack domain expertise in brain science, may fabricate claims, drift during multi-step reasoning, and offer few defined points for expert intervention. These failures are especially costly in brain science, where conclusions feed into downstream scientific claims and depend on laboratory-specific expertise and careful human judgment. We present \textbf{BrainPilot} a \textbf{fully open-source} multi-agent system that accelerates brain science research with traceable logs and agent-verified results. A principal investigator (PI) agent coordinates specialist agents grounded in curated domain knowledge: a unified brain science knowledge base containing 7{,}233 indexed items and a skill library of 72 reusable methodology units across seven research domains. Every major step is recorded in the Graph of Trace, an auditable record that links subgoals, tool use, evidence, and claims and allows researchers to follow and inspect the workflow. An Auditor agent further integrates fabrication checking into the workflow. For evaluation, we run three brain science tasks from Agents' Last Exam, introduce our own benchmark, \textbf{BrainPilotBench-v0}, and present additional end-to-end case studies. Across these evaluations, BrainPilot with an open-source backbone model attains performance comparable to state-of-the-art agent framework with less costs.

AutoSynthesis: An agentic system for automated meta-analysis

Moein Taherinezhad , Sebastian Maier , Gerardo Vitagliano , Francesco Pierri , Stefan Feuerriegel

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces AutoSynthesis, an end-to-end multi-agent system that automates the entire meta-analysis pipeline from a natural-language research question. The system uses multiple LLM-based agents to formulate search strategies, retrieve and screen scientific literature, extract quantitative statistics, compute effect sizes, and perform random-effects meta-analysis, outputting a PRISMA-compliant report. For astronomy, this could automate the synthesis of results across multiple papers on, e.g., high-redshift galaxy properties or AGN demographics, saving significant manual effort. A concrete workflow would be to input a question like 'What is the typical stellar mass of little red dots at z>6?' and let the system aggregate published measurements. A key caveat is that the system was demonstrated on only 28 studies; scaling to the full astronomical literature and handling heterogeneous data formats (e.g., SED-fit results vs. direct measurements) may require additional engineering.

Reason

This paper directly addresses automated scientific discovery by introducing a multi-agent system for meta-analysis, which is a core interest. The system retrieves literature, screens studies, extracts statistics, and performs analysis, aligning with interests in LLM agents for literature review and scientific workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the system can handle heterogeneous data types common in astronomy (e.g., flux densities, redshifts, masses with asymmetric error bars)
  • Look for evaluation metrics comparing the system's pooled effect sizes to manual meta-analyses, especially for datasets with high scatter or publication bias
  • Examine the system's screening criteria and whether it can incorporate domain-specific quality flags (e.g., spectroscopic vs. photometric redshifts)
Abstract

Evidence synthesis is crucial for turning primary research into reliable knowledge for science, medicine, education, and policy. Yet, quantitative evidence synthesis remains largely manual and difficult to scale. Here, we introduce AutoSynthesis, an end-to-end multi-agent system for automated meta-analysis. Given a research question in natural language, AutoSynthesis formulates a search strategy, retrieves scientific literature, screens candidate studies, assesses full-text eligibility, extracts quantitative statistics, computes standardized effect sizes, and finally performs random-effects meta-analysis. AutoSynthesis further supports heterogeneity analysis to examine how effect sizes vary across moderators, as well as risk-of-bias assessment. As output, AutoSynthesis produces a transparent report aligned with PRISMA guidelines. In our application, AutoSynthesis screened over 28 studies and extracted more than 20 quantitative claims. The pooled effect estimates produced by AutoSynthesis are similar to Hedges' $g$ of expert-conducted meta-analyses, indicating close agreement with manual evidence synthesis. Together, these results show that AutoSynthesis can make quantitative evidence synthesis more scalable, thereby supporting evidence-based decision-making across disciplines.
