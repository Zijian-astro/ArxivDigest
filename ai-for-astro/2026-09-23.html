Ovis-Embedding: Pushing the Frontiers of Universal Omni-Modal Embeddings

Ovis-Embedding Team

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper introduces Ovis-Embedding, a family of omni-modal embedding models that encode text, images, video, and audio into a common representation space instead of using separate modality-specific towers. The core idea is to start from a pretrained omni-modal backbone and adapt it with contrastive training, using low-rank initialization, a large and diverse multimodal corpus, homogeneous-source sampling for better in-batch negatives, focal loss for hard examples, and embedding distillation from expert models. At inference, low-rank feature decomposition allows compact embeddings with flexible dimensionality. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because it offers a recipe for building a shared embedding space across JWST images, spectra, catalog tables, and literature text, enabling any-to-any retrieval such as finding spectra similar to an image or papers similar to a data product. A concrete workflow could be fine-tuning an omni-modal embedding model on astronomical image-spectrum-catalog triplets for cross-modal search and anomaly detection. The main caveat is that the paper evaluates on general multimodal benchmarks, not scientific data, so you would need to test whether the contrastive training and distillation transfer to astronomical domains with different statistics and noise.

Reason

This is a state-of-the-art omni-modal embedding model combining text, image, video, and audio in a shared representation space, which is highly relevant to multimodal retrieval and cross-modal alignment for astronomical images, spectra, catalogs, and literature.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the training corpus or benchmark includes any scientific, remote sensing, or medical imaging data that hints at transferability.
  • Look for details on how homogeneous-source sampling handles highly imbalanced modality pairings.
  • Assess whether the embedding distillation method can incorporate domain experts such as spectral fitting models.
  • Check whether the released embeddings or code support fine-tuning on new modalities or scientific datasets.
Abstract

In this report, we introduce \textbf{Ovis-Embedding}, a state-of-the-art omni-modal embedding family built on native integration of text, image, video, and audio. Instead of assembling separate modality towers, Ovis-Embedding uses a shared multimodal backbone to encode different modalities in a common representation space. Specifically, we make \textbf{three key advances}: (1) \textbf{native omni-modal initialization}: we adopt a pretrained Qwen-omni model as the embedding backbone and adapt it through contrastive training with low-rank initialization; (2) \textbf{data-centric omni-modal training}: we construct a broad, high-quality corpus spanning text, images, video, audio, and interleaved multimodal data. To improve data efficiency, we introduce homogeneous-source sampling to form task-consistent batches with informative in-batch negatives; and (3) \textbf{embedding-specific training and inference optimization}: we use focal loss to emphasize hard examples and similarity-based Embedding Distillation to transfer fine-grained similarity structure from complementary experts. At inference time, low-rank feature decomposition enables compact embeddings with flexible dimensionality and minimal performance loss. Empirical evaluations show that the \textbf{Ovis-Embedding} family achieves state-of-the-art performance on \textbf{MMEB-v3}, \textbf{MMEB-v2}, \textbf{MVEB}, \textbf{MAEB}, and \textbf{RTEB}, demonstrating its effectiveness across text, image, video, and audio modalities. These results highlight the potential of unified omni-modal training to overcome modality fragmentation and advance universal embedding models for any-to-any retrieval.

Recursive self-improvement of AI research agents

Dhruv Srikanth , Bingchen Zhao , Dixing Xu , Yuxiang Wu , Zhengyao Jiang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether an AI research agent can improve its own research efficiency by editing its own code and keeping changes that perform better on hidden evaluations. AIDE^2 implements this recursive self-improvement loop: it proposes code changes, benchmarks modified versions of itself on AI R&D tasks, and retains the best-performing variants. In an autonomous 8-day run it discovered seven successive improvements, including new search policies and memory mechanisms, and these gains transferred to held-out benchmarks in ML engineering, heuristic algorithm engineering, and physics-based weather forecasting. For astronomy, this matters because it suggests a path toward LLM agents that can iteratively improve their own literature-review, data-analysis, or pipeline-building workflows without constant human redesign. A concrete workflow could be applying the same loop to an agent that writes and refines spectral-fitting or source-classification code, using held-out astronomical tasks as the hidden evaluation. The main caveat is that recursive self-improvement raises safety, reproducibility, and reward-hacking concerns, and the paper's gains are on AI R&D benchmarks rather than astronomical data.

Reason

This is directly about recursive self-improvement of AI research agents, which aligns strongly with research agents, automated scientific discovery, and LLM workflows for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the hidden evaluations are constructed and whether they could be replaced with astronomical tasks
  • Look for details on memory mechanisms and context management that could help long literature-review or data-analysis agents
  • Assess the reward-hacking analysis and whether the safeguards would transfer to scientific workflows
  • Check whether the discovered improvements are architecture-specific or generalizable to other agent frameworks
Abstract

AI agents are beginning to automate research and development across the AI stack, from improving training efficiency to optimizing inference. A natural next step is to improve the research efficiency of the agents themselves. When an AI research agent's own code is the object of optimization, each accepted rewrite becomes the agent that the next round edits. We refer to this loop as recursive self-improvement. Its significance lies in a long-standing trend, in which increased cumulative spending on R&D yields diminishing returns. Sustained self-improvement offers a way to counter this trend. We present AIDE^2, a system that implements this loop for a frontier AI research agent. It proposes changes to its own code, benchmarks modified versions of itself on a suite of AI R&D tasks, and keeps the changes that perform best on hidden evaluations. In an autonomous 8-day run, AIDE^2 discovered seven successive improvements, ranging from a new search policy to memory mechanisms that compress and manage the agent's growing context. These gains generalize to four held-out benchmarks spanning machine learning engineering, heuristic algorithm engineering, and physics-based weather forecasting, the last of which is out of distribution from the selection tasks. On all four, the strongest discovered agent matches or exceeds a human-engineered production research agent that ranks among the strongest on FML-Bench. On a separate held-out task family, the discovered agents also exhibit reduced reward hacking, a property the loop never explicitly optimized for: the rate falls from 55% to 32% during the run, 7 percentage points below the human-engineered agent. Together, these results show that an AI research agent can improve its own research efficiency through recursive self-improvement, and that these gains transfer to tasks and domains the loop never encountered.

AIBuildAI-2.5: Efficient Autonomous AI Model Development Through LLM-Guided Tree Search

Peijia Qin , Ruiyi Zhang , Qi Cao , Han Guo , Li Zhang , Pengtao Xie

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how to make autonomous agents that build AI models more efficient, since current tree-search agents waste budget on noisy candidate evaluations, poor hardware scheduling, and expensive single-model inference. The authors introduce AIBuildAI-2.5, which uses an LLM judge to score candidate programs on expected improvement, grounding, and feasibility, a selector to choose which node to explore, a resource-aware scheduler for training jobs, and a router that assigns cheaper models to easier subtasks. The main AI/ML idea is LLM-guided tree search, where language models both propose and evaluate candidate code, combined with cost-aware orchestration to make the search practical under real compute budgets. For astronomy, this is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery, where an agent could search over model architectures, feature sets, or analysis pipelines for JWST data and allocate GPU resources efficiently. A concrete workflow would be to use a similar agent to search over photometric-redshift or spectral-fitting pipelines, with the judge scoring candidates on expected performance and feasibility. The caveat is that the system is evaluated on ML benchmarks and autonomous research tasks, so its ability to handle domain-specific astronomical constraints and data formats is untested.

Reason

This paper presents an LLM-guided tree search system for autonomous AI model development, which is directly relevant to research agents and automated scientific discovery methods. The efficiency improvements in search, scheduling, and model routing could transfer to autonomous experimentation and model-building workflows in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the LLM-guided tree search and judge scoring are described in enough detail to adapt to astronomical model search
  • Look for the scheduler and router design and whether they assume specific hardware or model APIs
  • Check the MLE-Bench and AIRS-Bench task descriptions and whether any resemble scientific data analysis
  • Look for the limitations section and whether the authors discuss domain transfer or data contamination
Abstract

Autonomous agents that automatically build artificial intelligence (AI) models could broaden access to AI across science and engineering. A popular line of such agents frames model building as a code search problem and solves it by tree search, in which each node is a candidate program and the tree grows by generating a child program from a parent, and these agents now approach the capability of experienced AI engineers on realistic benchmarks. However, these agents have three weaknesses in efficiency that have not been fully addressed. First, only a small number of candidates can be executed within a realistic budget, so search rules that rank nodes by executed rewards, such as Monte Carlo-style tree search, rely on few and noisy scores and select the next node to explore less effectively. Second, no resource-aware strategy is used to schedule training jobs, which can lower hardware utilization and training efficiency. Third, every agent call is served by a single powerful model, which inflates inference cost. Here we introduce AIBuildAI-2.5, an agentic system that carries out the tree search with LLM agents and addresses each of the three issues. AIBuildAI-2.5 proposes a novel LLM-guided tree search, in which a judge scores each candidate on its expected improvement, grounding, and feasibility, and a selector ranks the pool of candidates from these scores and the state of the search. In addition, AIBuildAI-2.5 comprises a scheduler that launches training jobs with the current hardware resource status taken into account and a router that assigns lower-cost LLMs to less demanding tasks while reserving the most capable LLM for the most challenging sub-tasks in the AI model building workflow. AIBuildAI-2.5 ranks first on MLE-Bench with a medal rate of 73.3%, and outperforms a strong baseline on six autonomous AI research tasks from AIRS-Bench.

The AI Neuroscientist: An Interactive Agentic Interface for Neuroimaging Analysis

Aakash Patel , Panos Ketonis , Shreya Saxena , Smita Krishnaswamy , David van Dijk

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the barrier that specialized coding and statistics create for researchers analyzing neuroimaging data. The core idea is an LLM agent coupled to a domain toolset that performs quality control, modeling, and visualization, letting users specify analyses in natural language instead of scripts. This is directly relevant to astronomy because JWST and survey work face the same accessibility and reproducibility problems, and an analogous agent could wrap spectral fitting, photometric redshift, or image reduction tools. A concrete workflow would be to build a similar agent over astronomical toolsets for interactive exploration of high-redshift galaxy or AGN data. The caveat is that the demonstration is small-scale fNIRS data with a custom benchmark, so generalization to large, heterogeneous astronomical datasets is unproven.

Reason

It presents an LLM agent interface for interactive scientific data analysis with quality control, modeling, and visualization, closely matching my interest in research agents for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the agent is grounded to the neuroimaging toolset and whether the pattern is domain-agnostic
  • Look at the custom benchmarking suite and how the agent is compared to general-purpose code-sandbox agents
  • Assess how quality control and visualization steps are exposed to natural-language queries
  • Check the stated limitations and plans for extending to other modalities
Abstract

Analyzing neuroimaging data requires specialized coding and statistical expertise, which limits accessibility for researchers without computational backgrounds. We present the AI Neuroscientist, a language agent for interactive data exploration. The system integrates a large language model (LLM) with a neuroimaging toolset to perform quality control, modeling, and visualization. This allows researchers to query data quality and specify analysis parameters directly in natural language, providing a transparent and interactive alternative to conventional scripted pipelines for small-scale data exploration. We demonstrate these capabilities using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) data, and evaluate the agent on a custom fNIRS benchmarking suite against general-purpose LLM agents with code sandboxes. Future extensions will generalize the architecture to additional modalities, including functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data, and expand the benchmarking suite to additional fNIRS tasks.

Beyond Natural Language: An Agent-Native Language for Autonomous Science

Yifeng He , Jiachen Liu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper argues that as AI agents take on scientific inquiry, natural-language prose is too ambiguous and untracked for machines to audit, so it introduces Lara, a machine-checkable language and protocol for research claims. In Lara, authors explicitly declare claims, supporting evidence, assumptions, and known objections, and a deterministic checker assigns each claim a status such as justified, defeated, contested, or gap, where gap marks incomplete support. The system also lets declared bridges connect arguments across papers into an auditable network and mechanizes the semantic guarantees in Lean 4, with a sorry-free public metatheory. For astronomy, this is relevant to building research agents that track which claims in a JWST or high-redshift paper are supported, contested, or dependent on assumptions, and to literature-review agents that need auditable reasoning. A concrete workflow would be encoding a set of galaxy-evolution claims and their evidence into Lara and letting an agent flag gaps or defeated claims. The caveat is that encoding arguments is labor-intensive and the paper is a formal-methods contribution, so practical adoption depends on tooling and community uptake.

Reason

A machine-checkable language for research claims and autonomous science directly targets automated scientific discovery and auditable research agents, which is central to the stated interests.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the syntax and semantics of claim, evidence, assumption, and objection declarations and how the checker assigns statuses
  • Look for the case studies, especially the empirical review and the withdrawn-axiom example, to see how gaps and defeat propagate
  • Check the Lean 4 mechanization claims and what remains on paper rather than mechanized
  • Assess whether Lara could encode claims from an astronomy paper and integrate with an LLM literature agent
Abstract

As autonomous AI agents take on every stage of scientific inquiry, research output is expanding far beyond human review capacity. Yet scientific communication still relies on natural-language prose: an informal medium prone to ambiguity, hidden assumptions, and untracked limitations that machines cannot reliably audit. We introduce Lara, a machine-checkable language and protocol for checking and revising support for research claims. By turning research arguments into executable artifacts, Lara provides an epistemic kernel for autonomous science: it enables automated validation pipelines for research agents, lets declared bridges connect arguments across papers into an auditable network, and allows both humans and machines to recheck the standing of an encoded claim in milliseconds. In a Lara program, authors explicitly declare their claims, supporting evidence and assumptions, and known objections or limitations. A lightweight, deterministic checker adjudicates these interactions, assigning each claim a reproducible status: "justified", "defeated", "contested", or "gap", which marks a claim whose support is incomplete and locates the unanswered question. Case studies cover empirical review, a philosophical debate without measurements, and the loss of support when an assumed axiom is withdrawn. We establish the metatheory of claim checking and cross-context argument transport, and mechanize the semantic guarantees in Lean 4 (roughly 117,000 lines), leaving three arguments on paper. The audited public metatheory is "sorry"-free and uses only Lean's three standard axioms; some executable examples additionally trust native evaluation.

ArticleMiner: Ontology-Guided Knowledge Graph Construction from Scientific Publications

Md Abrar Jahin , Craig A. Knoblock , Jay Pujara

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that scientific facts in papers are often buried in tables and supplementary files, where numbers only make sense with their headers, units, methods, and field conventions. The proposed framework, ArticleMiner, combines multiple parsers and a language model with a small human-authored task module per domain that defines canonical names, surface forms, derivation rules, validity constraints, and RDF bindings. This matters for astronomy because extracting structured facts from the literature, such as measurements, uncertainties, and methods, is a core need for building knowledge bases and for LLM agents that reason over published results. A concrete workflow would be writing an astronomy task module and running ArticleMiner over JWST papers to build a queryable graph of galaxy properties and measurements. The caveat is that the evaluation covers four non-astronomy domains and the abstract notes uncertainty on smaller benchmarks, so the effort to author an astronomy module and its accuracy are unproven.

Reason

Builds an ontology-guided knowledge graph from scientific publications, including tables and supplementary files, directly relevant to LLM agents for literature review and structured extraction in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how much human effort the task module requires and whether it is feasible for astronomy
  • Look for the evaluation metrics on table and supplementary extraction, not just final graph accuracy
  • Check whether the framework handles uncertainty, units, and upper limits common in astronomy
  • Assess whether the code and ontology are released and extensible to new domains
Abstract

Scientific papers keep much of their quantitative content in tables and supplementary files, where a number means something only through its header, caption, unit, analytical method, and the conventions of its field. Recovering the rows and columns of a table is therefore not the same as recovering the scientific fact it reports. Most semantic table-interpretation methods assume that a clean table is already available and subsequently map its cells or columns to ontology terms, whereas most publication-level extraction systems are designed for a single domain. We study a middle path: a shared process that reads a paper and its supplementary files, gathers evidence from several parsers and a language model, and reconciles that evidence, while a bounded human-authored task module for each task supplies the domain meaning. The module lists the canonical names the graph may use, the surface forms that map to them, a small set of derivation rules and validity constraints, an identity key, and the bindings used to write RDF. It defines what a task is allowed to emit; it does not try to list every convention of a field. We build four such modules (for drug-discovery chemistry, materials science, machine learning, and mineral geochemistry) in the ArticleMiner framework, and evaluate them on 163 papers, including a new geochemistry benchmark with expert-curated ground truth. In comparisons against a same-LLM few-shot baseline, the point estimates favor ArticleMiner on all four tasks, with uncertainty on the two smaller benchmarks. The geochemistry comparison also includes access to supplementary files, so its improvement cannot be attributed to domain guidance alone.

Qwen3.8-Omni: Towards Native Omni-Modal Agents

Qwen Team

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses how to build a single model that can perceive and reason across text, images, audio, and video while also acting as an autonomous agent over long tasks. Its main idea is a natively multimodal mixture-of-experts model (a design where only part of the network activates per input, saving compute) trained so that agentic skills learned in text transfer to audio and video, with a one-million-token context window for long reasoning and planning. For astronomy, this matters because JWST and multi-wavelength work increasingly requires combining images, spectra, tables, and literature in one reasoning loop, which is exactly the multimodal, long-context setting this model targets. A concrete workflow to imagine is using it as an agent that reads a folder of JWST cutouts and spectra, calls retrieval over a paper corpus, writes analysis code, and reports candidate little red dots or AGN. The caveat is that the abstract reports only generic multimodal and productivity benchmarks, with no astronomy evaluation, so I would need to verify whether it can handle FITS-level numerical data, precise photometry, and scientific uncertainty rather than just natural images and video.

Reason

A natively multimodal agentic foundation model with million-token context and open plugin/harness frameworks is directly relevant to building LLM/LMM agents for JWST imaging, spectra, catalogs, and literature workflows. The long-horizon agentic and multimodal reasoning capabilities could transfer to astronomical data analysis pipelines.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether any evaluation involves scientific, tabular, or FITS-like numerical data rather than only natural images, audio, and video
  • Look for details on tool-use and code-execution ability, since astronomical workflows depend on running analysis code
  • Check the context-window and memory-management claims for whether long multi-file astronomical sessions are realistically supported
  • Look for the plugin/harness documentation to see how easily custom astronomy tools and data loaders can be integrated
Abstract

We introduce Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash, a natively multimodal agentic model for real-world multimodal productivity. Compared with previous omni models, which primarily emphasized perception and interaction, Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash substantially improves multimodal understanding and reasoning, as well as performance on long-horizon agentic tasks. These capabilities are supported by a native multimodal co-training strategy that preserves strong text-domain capabilities while facilitating the transfer of agentic capabilities from text to audio and video tasks. The model inherits the sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture of Qwen3.8-Next and extends the context window to one million tokens, supporting long-context multimodal reasoning and long-horizon planning. These advances enable integration into production workflows as a primary agent or a specialized sub-agent, supporting video editing, long-form audio and video translation, music-conditioned music video or movie generation, and video-based note or omni-skill creation. To address the lack of native audio and video support in existing agent harnesses, we release Qwen-MM-Plugins, a lightweight open-source plugin framework for multimodal productivity. We further frame real-time multimodal interaction as a system-level challenge requiring orchestration of context and memory management, tool use, and sub-agent delegation. Accordingly, we release Qwen-Live-Harness, an open-source framework for building responsive, real-time multimodal agents based on Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash. Extensive evaluations demonstrate that Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash achieves strong performance across multimodal understanding, reasoning, long-horizon agentic execution, and video productivity tasks. These results and the accompanying open-source tools support Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash as a practical foundation for deploying natively multimodal agents in research and production.

How Strongly Should Task State Influence an LLM Agent?

Chenyu Zhang , Wonbin Kweon , Jiawei Han

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how strongly an LLM agent's task state, meaning which steps are done, blocked, cancelled, or open, should be injected into the agent's decision process. The authors fix the task rules, model, and episodes, then vary the state representation from a raw transcript to an exact checklist, to per-turn directives from a compiled state machine, to an enforcement gate that refuses state-violating actions, scoring every episode by exact payload matching against dynamic ground truth. Across three models, two reasoning regimes, and two domains, they find that merely displaying accurate state is unreliable, that an unverified ledger the agent writes itself beats an accurate checklist it is shown, that directives help in proportion to the model's obedience, and that enforcement needs no obedience but is bounded by the correctness of its state and matcher. For astronomy, this is directly relevant to designing LLM agents that carry out multi-step workflows such as data reduction, catalog cross-matching, or literature review, where tracking which steps are complete is essential. A concrete workflow could be compiling a state machine from an observing or analysis brief and using an enforcement gate to prevent the agent from repeating or skipping steps. The main caveat is that enforcement only pays when failures are state-decidable and frequent, and it can hurt when the gate's judgment is wrong, so you should check whether astronomical workflows have clearly decidable state transitions.

Reason

This paper directly studies how task state should influence LLM agents in long-horizon work, which is central to building reliable research agents for multi-step astronomical analysis and literature workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the exact definitions of the four state-injection rungs and how they are implemented
  • Look for the conditions under which enforcement helps versus hurts, especially the state-decidability criterion
  • Check whether the domains and tasks resemble long-horizon scientific workflows
  • Look for the matcher design and how errors in mapping requests to steps affect performance
Abstract

Long-horizon assigned work requires an LLM agent to track the state of a task: which steps are done, blocked, cancelled, or open to repetition. Agent systems either keep this state as text in the prompt and rely on the model to read that text, or move the state into a module that enforces it, and each system is evaluated as a whole, so no one knows how much reliability comes from the state being shown, told, or enforced. We fix the task rules, the model, and paired episodes and vary how strongly task state reaches the agent: a raw transcript, an exact checklist, per-turn directives from a state machine compiled from the brief and advanced only by execution receipts, or an enforcement gate on that machine that refuses state-violating actions; every episode is scored by exact payload matching against dynamic ground truth. Across three models, two reasoning regimes, and two domains, four findings hold without per-turn reasoning: displaying accurate state is unreliable, an unverified ledger the agent writes itself beats an accurate checklist it is shown, directives help in proportion to the model's obedience, and enforcement needs no obedience but is bounded by the correctness of its state and by the matcher that maps requests to steps; per-turn reasoning at a 235B agent compresses these separations without repairing the text rungs. The same gate, compiled from $\tau^2$-bench's airline policy, raises a 235B agent's pass$^1$ from 0.39 to 0.54 and changes nothing for a 35B agent that rarely violates the policy; on PM-Bench, where acting turns on recognizing a cue rather than on state, showing the record is the best rung--matching or beating both gates and reversing the ledger-over-checklist finding--and enforcing the matcher's judgement drops a 35B agent below its raw transcript. Enforcement pays when failures are state-decidable and frequent, and hurts when the gate's judgement is wrong.
