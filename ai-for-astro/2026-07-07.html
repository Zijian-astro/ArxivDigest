VideoSearcher: Empowering Video Deep Research with Multi-Tool Agentic Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning

Zhenkun Gao , Yicheng Bao , Jinlong Peng , Xueheng Li , Theo Huang , Bangwei Liu , Kunquan Li , Zhenye Gan , Tao Hu , Chengjun Xie , Mingqian Yang , Xuanhua He , Zhizhong Zhang , Xin Tan , Chengjie Wang , Yuan Xie

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of open-world video evidence exploration, moving beyond closed-context perception. It proposes VideoSearcher, a closed-loop agentic framework that combines temporal localization, spatial focusing, and multimodal search using reinforcement learning. For astronomy, this is highly relevant for building an agent that can search through large video or image datasets (e.g., JWST data archives), locate specific sources, and retrieve relevant information from catalogs and literature. A concrete workflow would be an agent that takes a query like 'Find all little red dots in this JWST field and retrieve their photometric redshifts from the literature.' A key caveat is that the benchmark is focused on video, and adaptation to astronomical images (e.g., multi-band FITS files) may require significant engineering.

Reason

Directly addresses video deep research with multi-tool agentic reasoning, which is highly relevant for building research agents for astronomical data analysis and literature review.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the 'multi-tool reasoning' can be extended to astronomical tools (e.g., SExtractor, photometric redshift codes, SED fitting).
  • Look for the 'BiSPO' algorithm details to see if it can handle rewards from scientific accuracy (e.g., correct source classification).
  • Examine the 'VideoSearch-QA' benchmark to understand the types of questions and whether they are analogous to astronomical queries.
Abstract

Video understanding is moving beyond closed-context perception toward open-world evidence exploration, a paradigm formalized as Video Deep Research (VDR). However, existing multimodal search agents primarily target static images, and the current VDR benchmark relies on text-centric retrieval that discards crucial visual information. To address these limitations, we propose VideoSearcher, a closed-loop agentic framework that empowers Vision-Language Models with multi-tool reasoning for VDR. VideoSearcher unifies temporal localization, spatial focusing, and multimodal search within a single reasoning trajectory, enabling agents to progressively ground visual clues, retrieve relevant evidence, and synthesize answers. To optimize knowledge-intensive reasoning trajectories, we propose Bi-branch Sequence Policy Optimization (BiSPO), a reinforcement learning algorithm that decouples tool-invocation optimization from answer-accuracy optimization. This design provides stable learning signals for both evidence-grounded reasoning and purposeful tool use. Furthermore, we construct VideoSearch-QA, the first benchmark designed to evaluate open-world video information grounding and multimodal search-based reasoning. Extensive experiments demonstrate that VideoSearcher significantly outperforms prior open-source agentic baselines across various search-oriented and multimodal understanding benchmarks.

Dictionaries, Not Darwin: Set-Level Selection Beats LLM Evolution in Scientific Equation Discovery

Pan Li

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether LLM-based evolutionary loops (generate, select, repeat) actually improve scientific equation discovery over independent sampling. It finds that under matched LLM budgets, parent-conditioned evolution is indistinguishable from fresh sampling, and proposes PTB-Search: a one-generation method that samples LLM proposals once, extracts reusable terms into a dictionary, and performs set-level sparse selection. For astronomy, this could be used to discover physical laws or empirical relations from observational data (e.g., galaxy scaling relations, star formation laws). The method achieves 73.2% accuracy on LLM-SRBench with 1/10th the LLM calls of baselines. A key caveat is that equation discovery requires a well-defined search space of candidate terms, and the method's performance depends on the quality of initial LLM proposals.

Reason

Directly addresses using LLMs for scientific equation discovery, with a critical audit of evolutionary approaches and a proposed alternative that could transfer to astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the set-level selection algorithm details: how are term dictionaries constructed and how does sparse selection work?
  • Check if the method has been applied to any physics or astronomy datasets, or only to synthetic benchmarks.
  • Examine the failure modes: what types of equations or data regimes cause the method to struggle?
Abstract

Large language models are increasingly used as evolutionary engines for scientific discovery: generate candidates, select winners, feed them back as parents, and repeat. We audit whether this loop actually compounds discovery in scientific equation discovery, a setting where finite samples make structure underdetermined and interpolation easy. Under matched LLM-call budgets, parent-conditioned evolution is indistinguishable from fresh independent sampling: median OOD NMSE is 0.045 vs. 0.049, instructed multi-parent crossover is worse, final success is predicted by initial proposal quality, and multiple iteration schemes fail to add solved problems. Operationally, the loop reduces to what it produces: a dictionary of candidate terms. We turn that diagnosis into PTB-Search, a one-generation method for componentized scientific discovery. PTB-Search samples independent LLM proposals once, extracts reusable terms into a per-problem dictionary, and performs train-only set-level sparse selection with least-squares coefficients. Its central principle is that underdetermined data identifies the joint behavior of term sets, not reliable per-term credit. On identical dictionaries and zero additional LLM calls, set-level selectors solve 165--169 of 717 cells, while single-term reductions solve only 74--78. On the official 239-problem LLM-SRBench split, PTB-Search reaches 73.2% Acc0.1 with Llama-3.1-8B and 77.0% with a single-seed DeepSeek-V4 anchor, versus 49.2% for the best reported baseline, using one tenth of the standardized call budget. A program-domain stress test gives a scoped boundary: generation count remains unreliable, while retained external state can help in harder non-linear spaces. Across these results, LLMs are best understood as material suppliers; discovery is carried by external set-level selection over reusable components.

From Tensor Buffer to Distributed Memory Hierarchy: A Survey of KV Cache Management for LLM Serving

Jie Li , Tongyang Wang , Yong Chen

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper surveys more than thirty KV-management systems for LLM serving, classifying them using four axes: locality, lifetime, ownership, and substrate. It identifies five architectural archetypes and seven missing KV-specific measurements linked to open problems. For astronomy, understanding KV cache management is crucial for deploying LLM agents for literature review, data analysis, and scientific workflows at scale, especially when processing large astronomical catalogs or multi-wavelength datasets. A concrete workflow could involve using a local-paged or shared-store KV cache to enable efficient retrieval-augmented generation over astronomical literature or database queries. A key caveat is that the survey focuses on general LLM serving, not astronomy-specific optimizations, so the transferability of specific techniques to astronomical workloads needs verification.

  • Look for any mention of memory-efficient serving that could enable running large LLMs on limited hardware for astronomical research
  • Check if the survey covers open-source frameworks that could be adapted for astronomy-specific LLM workflows
Abstract

The key-value (KV) cache has become a first-order memory object in LLM serving rather than a temporary per-request tensor. This survey classifies more than thirty KV-management systems and frameworks using four axes: locality, lifetime, ownership, and substrate. The axes reveal five architectural archetypes -- local-paged, disaggregated-pipeline, shared-store, memory-pool, and hybrid-tier. Once workload and hardware are fixed, ownership accounts for much of the remaining design variance among distributed systems. The survey also audits current evaluations and identifies seven missing KV-specific measurements, linking them to open problems in fault tolerance, isolation, tiered eviction, speculative decoding, MoE serving, and shared-cache semantics.

Automated Data Readiness for Scientific AI

Sean R. Wilkinson , Valentine G. Anantharaj , Jong Youl Choi , Ketan Maheshwari , Marshall McDonnell , Massimiliano Lupo Pasini , Polina Shpilker , Renan Souza , Patrick Widener , Sarp Oral , Wesley Brewer

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks how to automate the transformation of raw scientific datasets into AI-ready training data. It presents REDI, a five-stage pipeline (ingest, preprocess, transform, structure, output) with provenance tracking and agent-native deployment, plus SetGo for FAIR compliance. For astronomy, this could automate the preparation of JWST, Rubin, or ALMA data for machine learning workflows. One could imagine using REDI to create standardized training datasets from raw FITS files, catalogs, and spectra. The framework scales to 100 nodes on Frontier, but requires integration with existing astronomy data formats and pipelines.

Reason

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if REDI supports FITS, HDF5, or other astronomy-specific data formats
  • Look for examples of how provenance tracking works for reproducibility
  • See if the agent-native deployment allows integration with astronomy workflow systems like Firefly or Jupyter
  • Evaluate the parallel scaling performance on realistic astronomy dataset sizes
Abstract

Leadership computing facilities steward large-scale scientific datasets that routinely require substantial transformation before serving as AI training data. However, no existing framework fully unifies automated transformation, readiness assessment, provenance tracking, and agent-native deployment. We present REDI, an open-source framework that addresses this gap through a unified five-stage pipeline (ingest, preprocess, transform, structure, and output) with per-stage instrumentation for reproducibility and deployment as an agent-callable skill; companion tool SetGo automates FAIR compliance and catalog publication. Evaluated across climate, proteomics, materials science, and nuclear fusion, REDI transforms all datasets from raw to AI-ready, with outputs validated against domain-expert references, and preliminary results show near-ideal parallel scaling to 100 nodes on Frontier for the climate case. Provenance-instrumented profiling reveals file I/O as the dominant pipeline cost, with format selection a first-order optimization lever. These results establish REDI as a cross-domain platform providing automated data readiness for scientific AI, transforming data preparation bottlenecks into reproducible, reusable community assets.

Learning Taxonomic Trees with Hierarchical Representation Regularization for Large Multimodal Models

Hulingxiao He , Zhi Tan , Yuxin Peng

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem that large multimodal models (LMMs) lack taxonomic knowledge, leading to poor hierarchical visual recognition (e.g., confusing a spiral galaxy with an elliptical). It proposes Hierarchical Representation Regularization (HiR^2), a plug-and-play regularizer that enforces taxonomic consistency by using hyperbolic entailment cones to represent class hierarchies. For astronomy, this could be directly applied to improve LMM-based classification of galaxies into a hierarchy (e.g., star-forming vs. quiescent, then spiral vs. elliptical, then barred vs. unbarred). A concrete workflow would be fine-tuning an LMM on a dataset like Galaxy Zoo with HiR^2 to ensure the model respects the known taxonomy. A key caveat is that the method requires a predefined taxonomy, which may not exist for all astronomical classification tasks (e.g., anomaly detection).

Reason

Directly addresses improving hierarchical visual recognition in LMMs, which is highly relevant for astronomical classification (e.g., galaxy morphology hierarchies, spectral type taxonomies).

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the 'semantic-aware visual tree construction' can be adapted to build a taxonomy from astronomical catalogs (e.g., using photometric redshifts or SED types).
  • Examine the computational overhead of the regularizer when applied to large LMMs.
Abstract

Taxonomies provide key information about the semantic relationships between concepts and the inherent organization of vision and language. Despite their impressive capabilities, large multimodal models (LMMs) often lack taxonomic knowledge, leading to low hierarchical visual recognition (HVR) consistency. These models typically only rely on language modeling objectives during fine-tuning and lack explicit taxonomy-aware regularization. To address this, we propose Hierarchical Representation Regularization ($HiR^2$), a simple plug-and-play regularizer that improves hierarchical consistency in LMMs. Specifically, we introduce a semantic-aware visual tree construction framework that extracts coarse-to-fine visual features from intermediate LLM layers guided by textual cues. The regularizer combines two complementary objectives: a taxonomic entailment loss that enforces hierarchy via hyperbolic entailment cones in the Lorentz model, and a discriminative dispersive loss that promotes angular separation of semantically similar embeddings on the unit sphere without disturbing the radial hierarchical structure. Extensive experiments demonstrate that $HiR^2$ effectively captures taxonomic structures across diverse LMMs and fine-tuning methods. Code is available at this https URL .

CoACT: Action-Preserving Observation Compression for Coding Agents

Haorui Chen , Yuancheng Zhu , Yitong Zhang , Jia Li

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of high inference cost in LLM-based coding agents due to accumulating observations from the environment. It proposes CoACT, a method that compresses observations while preserving the agent's next action, using a teacher model to generate compressed candidates and a reward to select those that don't change the agent's behavior. For astronomy, this is crucial for building cost-effective research agents that interact with databases, APIs, or simulation tools over many steps. A concrete workflow would be an agent that queries the NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database (NED), retrieves spectra, and runs fitting codes, where CoACT could compress the returned data to save tokens. A key caveat is that the method is designed for coding agents and may need adaptation for agents that process scientific data (e.g., images, spectra) rather than text.

Reason

Directly relevant to building efficient LLM agents for coding and scientific workflows, which is a key interest for automated scientific discovery in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the 'next-action preservation' criterion works for agents that perform non-coding actions (e.g., running a simulation, querying a database).
  • Look for analysis of compression quality on different types of observations (e.g., error messages, data tables, plots).
  • Examine whether the lightweight compressor can handle scientific data formats (e.g., FITS headers, ASCII tables).
Abstract

LLM-based coding agents solve software-engineering tasks through iterative interactions with development environments, where returned observations accumulate in the context and become a major source of inference cost. Observation compression reduces this cost by shortening observations before they are appended to the context. However, existing methods still exhibit an unsatisfactory efficiency-effectiveness trade-off, as they do not explicitly model how compression affects the agent's subsequent behavior. This paper proposes CoACT, an action-preserving observation compression method for coding agents. CoACT is built on next-action preservation (NAP), which requires a compressed observation to induce the same next action as the raw observation. By checking the agent's immediate next action, NAP provides a practical signal for whether a compression preserves the information needed for continued task solving. During training, a teacher model first generates multiple compressed candidates of each observation. CoACT then uses an action-preservation reward based on NAP to filter out candidates that would change the agent's next action, and uses a length-reduction reward to choose compact candidates as supervision for a lightweight compressor. Experiments on SWE-bench Verified with three agentic models show that CoACT reduces average total token consumption by 33.0% while maintaining task-solving effectiveness close to the uncompressed agent.

Out-of-distribution Neural Inference in Dynamical Ising Models

Yuan-Bin Zhu , Shuang Qiao , Shi-Ju Ran

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether neural networks can learn transferable physical rules for reconstructing interaction graphs from dynamical observations, using a kinetic Ising model as a controlled testbed. The main finding is that data-driven training produces architecture-dependent statistical priors rather than true physical understanding: Transformer models preserve training link density, while convolutional models collapse toward sparse predictions by exploiting the majority no-link class. This matters for astronomy because it reveals a concrete failure mode where high in-distribution accuracy and apparent out-of-distribution robustness do not imply learned physical rules—a critical caution for applying AI to JWST data where training and target distributions differ. An astronomer could use this insight to design better validation strategies for neural network-based inference of galaxy properties, SED fitting, or source classification from survey data. The caveat is that the study uses a simple Ising model, and the failure modes may manifest differently in more complex astronomical inverse problems.

Reason

Directly studies out-of-distribution generalization of neural networks for physical inverse problems, which is critical for applying AI to astronomical inference tasks like reconstructing galaxy properties from observations.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the edge-population diagnostics that distinguish Transformer vs. convolutional model behavior under distribution shifts.
  • Check whether the paper provides theoretical analysis or only empirical evidence for the architecture-dependent statistical priors.
  • Examine the experimental setup for how out-of-distribution shifts (topology and temperature) are defined and whether they are analogous to astronomical distribution shifts.
  • Look for any discussion of how these findings might generalize to more complex physical systems or astronomical data.
Abstract

Neural networks are increasingly used to infer hidden physical structure from dynamical observations, yet it remains unclear whether their out-of-distribution performance reflects transferable physical rule learning. We address this question in a controlled inverse problem: reconstructing interaction graphs of a kinetic Ising model from Glauber magnetization trajectories. Across convolutional, graph, Transformer, and hybrid architectures, we find that data-driven training produces distinct and reproducible statistical strategies under topology and temperature shifts. Edge-population diagnostics reveal that Transformer-based models tend to preserve the link density of the training ensemble, whereas convolutional models can collapse toward sparse- or no-link predictions that appear out-of-distribution stable by exploiting the majority no-link class. Thus, high in-distribution accuracy and apparent out-of-distribution robustness do not necessarily imply a learned dynamics-to-structure rule. Instead, neural reconstruction can be governed by architecture-dependent statistical priors. Our results identify a concrete failure mode of standard data-driven learning in physical inverse problems and motivate rule-guided principles for machine-learning-assisted scientific discovery.

Which Algorithm Specification Formats Help Language Models Implement Machine Learning Algorithms?

Masahiro Kato , Taka Kato

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks which written format of an algorithm specification helps LLMs most accurately implement machine learning algorithms from scratch. The authors compare prose, LaTeX pseudocode, YAML, JSON, Python stubs, and other formats across 5 ML tasks and 3 LLMs, testing 4,020 implementations against hidden tests that check subtle correctness details like tie-breaking and array shapes. They find that LaTeX algorithm-style pseudocode yields the largest accuracy improvement in the core-information setting, while code stubs do not consistently help despite specifying the function signature. For astronomy, this matters because researchers often need LLMs to implement data-reduction or fitting algorithms from papers (e.g., SED fitting, spectral extraction) where subtle implementation choices determine correctness. A concrete workflow would be using an LLM to implement a photometric redshift code from a paper's LaTeX pseudocode. A key caveat is that the study uses small ML tasks, not complex scientific pipelines, so transfer to astronomy-specific algorithms needs validation.

Reason

Directly studies how LLMs implement algorithms from research manuscripts, which is central to automated scientific discovery and reproducible research workflows in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the hidden test suite includes numerical edge cases common in astronomy (e.g., NaN handling, array shape mismatches, floating-point tolerance).
  • Look for the specific algorithm tasks tested—are any analogous to common astronomy workflows like interpolation, least-squares fitting, or matrix operations?
  • Examine the format effect sizes: does LaTeX pseudocode help more for longer or more nested algorithms, which are typical in astronomy?
Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to implement algorithms from research manuscripts, but papers often leave implementation choices implicit. This study examines how the written format of an algorithm specification affects first-pass LLM implementation accuracy. We compare ordinary prose, LaTeX algorithm-style pseudocode, PDF-like extracted pseudocode, Markdown fields, YAML-like specifications, JSON-like specifications, and Python code stubs across five machine learning tasks, three models, and four experimental settings, yielding 4,020 generated implementations. Hidden tests evaluate details that often determine correctness, including tie-breaking, array shapes, numerical rules, return structures, and invalid-input behavior. Under the core-information setting, LaTeX algorithm-style pseudocode has the largest average format effect, with YAML-like specifications and ordinary prose close behind. Under complete information, GPT-5.4 mini shows no format differences in the matched comparisons, whereas Gemma 3 4B and Llama 3.2 3B still do. Code stubs do not consistently improve correctness despite specifying the function signature. The results support a writing recommendation: authors should state the interface, computation steps, numerical rules, and boundary-case behavior explicitly, instead of relying on a particular surface format to carry those details.
