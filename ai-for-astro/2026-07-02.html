Autonomous Scientific Discovery via Iterative Meta-Reflection

Bingchen Zhao , Sara Beery , Oisin Mac Aodha

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces DiscoPER, an LLM-powered framework for autonomous scientific discovery that can explore datasets without predefined research questions. The system dynamically generates and executes code to test hypotheses, validates them with statistical tests, and uses a second-order reasoning mechanism to synthesize its own accumulated findings and redirect exploration. For astronomy, this could automate the process of discovering new patterns in large survey datasets like JWST or Rubin/LSST catalogs. A concrete workflow would be feeding DiscoPER a multi-wavelength catalog of high-redshift galaxies and letting it autonomously propose and test hypotheses about AGN-host galaxy correlations. A key caveat is that the benchmark is ecological, not astronomical, so the method's effectiveness on astronomical data (with complex noise, selection effects, and domain-specific priors) remains to be tested.

Reason

Directly addresses autonomous scientific discovery using LLMs, which is a core interest. The method involves iterative hypothesis generation, code execution, and multimodal data analysis, all highly relevant to automated astronomy workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the second-order meta-reflection mechanism is demonstrated on a dataset with complexity comparable to astronomical surveys (e.g., many features, missing data, selection biases).
  • Look for details on how the system handles multimodal data (images, spectra, tables) and whether the tool-use integration is general enough to plug in astronomical analysis tools (e.g., SED-fitting codes, photometric redshift estimators).
  • Examine the statistical testing framework: does it account for multiple testing corrections and realistic noise models common in astronomy?
Abstract

Autonomous scientific discovery systems offer the potential to accelerate research by automating the process of hypothesis generation and validation. However, current systems operate within constrained search spaces or require predefined research questions, limiting their capacity for true open-ended inquiry. Furthermore, while they generate hypotheses iteratively, they largely lack the ability to explicitly synthesize their own accumulated findings to uncover complex, interconnected phenomena. We introduce DiscoPER, an autonomous large language model-powered framework that conducts open-ended research by dynamically generating and executing code to explore datasets without pre-specified research objectives. To ensure rigorous scientific validity, every proposed discovery must pass statistical testing. To overcome the limitations of isolated search, our framework introduces a second-order reasoning mechanism that periodically analyzes its own accumulated discoveries. By treating prior discoveries as empirical data, DiscoPER identifies structural patterns, confounds, and epistemic gaps, actively redirecting hypothesis exploration toward uncharted regions of the search space. The search space is further expanded by incorporating tool use, enabling the system to explore hypotheses beyond structured metadata by seamlessly processing and extracting useful information from multimodal sources like images. Evaluated on iNatDisco, a new multimodal ecological knowledge benchmark with pattern-level ground truth obtained from peer-reviewed literature, DiscoPER recovers 8 of 9 known patterns with a 72.7% hypothesis support rate, outperforming both classical causal discovery and LLM-guided baselines. Ablations show that DiscoPER scales with more data, and confirms the benefits of second-order meta-reflection.

PHREEQC-MCQ-200: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Tool-Augmented Scientific Simulator Agents

Ke Zhang , Sahchit Chundur , Mohammad Javad Qomi , Maziar Raissi

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether tool access makes scientific computation more reliable or just more complex. It introduces PHREEQC-MCQ-200, a benchmark for evaluating tool-augmented LLM agents on aqueous-geochemistry simulations. The main result is that simulator access improves accuracy but also causes regressions (losing items answered correctly without tools), and output-access protocol matters. For astronomy, this framework is directly transferable to evaluating agents that use astronomical simulators (e.g., SED fitting, radiative transfer) or analysis tools (e.g., photometric redshift codes). A concrete workflow would be testing an LLM agent on tasks like running CIGALE or Prospector for SED fitting and interpreting outputs. The caveat is that the benchmark is specific to geochemistry, so the methodology needs to be adapted to astronomical simulators.

Reason

Directly relevant to LLM agents for scientific computation, tool use, and benchmarking, which aligns with automated scientific discovery.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the evaluation protocol: how are item-level retention and trajectory failures measured?
  • Check if the benchmark design (MCQ from simulator outputs) could be replicated for astronomical tools.
  • Assess the computational cost of running the agent with simulator access.
Abstract

Large language model agents are increasingly connected to scientific software, yet it remains unclear when tool access makes scientific computation more reliable rather than merely more complex. We introduce PHREEQC-MCQ-200, a benchmark for evaluating tool-augmented agents on deterministic aqueous-geochemistry simulations. The benchmark contains 200 multiple-choice questions derived from 21 validated PHREEQC scenarios, requiring agents to construct simulator inputs, execute PHREEQC, inspect structured outputs, and commit to final answers. Across multiple frontier and mid-tier model families, simulator access substantially improves aggregate accuracy, confirming that grounded execution is necessary for many scientific-computation tasks. However, the gains are not monotonic: tool-augmented agents also lose items they answered correctly without tools, revealing regressions that average accuracy alone hides. We further show that output-access protocol matters. A table-of-contents interface can reduce token cost while preserving or improving accuracy for stronger models, but it degrades performance for mid-tier models that cannot reliably navigate structured simulator outputs. PHREEQC-MCQ-200 therefore frames scientific tool use as an end-to-end diagnostic problem rather than a simple tool-calling capability. We argue that evaluations of scientific agents should report not only accuracy, but also item-level retention, output-access sensitivity, trajectory failures, and where the computation chain breaks.

When RAG Meets Query Planning: Logical Query Trees for Resolving Exploratory Reasoning Problems

Ganlin Xu , Linghao Zhang , Zhitao Yin , Hongda Xi , Chen Yang , Jiaqing Liang , Weijia Lu , Sihang Jiang , Yanghua Xiao , Deqing Yang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) struggling with exploratory reasoning problems (ERPs) that involve high uncertainty and ambiguity. It introduces PlanRAG, which models natural language queries as logical query trees (LQTs) using dynamic programming guided by a cost model. The method decomposes ERPs into atomic queries, organizes them into LQTs, and executes iterative aggregation, rewriting, retrieval, and generation. For astronomy, this could enable LLM agents to perform complex multi-step literature searches or data analysis tasks, such as finding galaxies with specific properties across multiple catalogs. The paper demonstrates improvements over state-of-the-art RAG systems on a new dataset, WikiWeb-ERP. However, the method is tested only on a Wikipedia-based dataset, not on astronomical literature or databases.

Reason

Directly addresses RAG with query planning for exploratory reasoning, which is highly relevant to astronomical literature review and data analysis workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for details on how LQTs are constructed from natural language, which is key for adapting to astronomical queries
  • Check if the cost model can be customized for domain-specific retrieval (e.g., ADS, NED, or MAST)
  • Examine the parallelization strategy for efficiency on large-scale astronomical databases
Abstract

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) effectively grounds large language models (LLMs) in external knowledge but struggles with \textbf{exploratory reasoning problems (ERPs)} that are the complex queries involving high uncertainty and ambiguity. Resolving ERPs requires complex reasoning with unclear paths, tending to result in retrieval noise and error accumulation. Furthermore, the absence of an end-to-end planning mechanism makes it difficult to generate effective trajectories for ERPs. Motivated by database query planning, we introduce \emph{PlanRAG}, an RAG framework that models ERPs of natural language as \textbf{logical query trees (LQTs)}. However, translating ERPs into LQTs is non-trivial due to representation and optimization gaps between structured SQL and unstructured natural language, making it highly challenging to construct high-quality LQTs. To address these problems, we first decompose ERPs into atomic queries and then organize them into LQTs using dynamic programming guided by a cost model involving multiple complementary dimensions. Finally, we execute iterative aggregation, rewriting, retrieval, and generation over LQTs, processing nodes concurrently and propagating intermediate results upward, with further parallelization across multiple threads for efficiency. Our experimental results show that PlanRAG outperforms state-of-the-art iteration-based and graph-based RAG systems on our newly constructed dataset, \textbf{WikiWeb-ERP}, thereby providing a new formulation for optimizing natural language queries. 8 2607.00510

Prototype Language Models

Dan Ley , Giang Nguyen , Himabindu Lakkaraju , Julius Adebayo

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks how to make large language models more interpretable by tracing their outputs back to specific training examples. The authors propose PRISM, a new architecture where each prediction is formed by a sparse, non-negative mixture of learned 'prototypes,' each anchored to a coherent neighborhood of training examples. Unlike standard dense LLMs, this design makes it possible to attribute which training data drove a given output, and to do so ~500x faster than existing post-hoc methods. For astronomy, this could enable a research agent that not only retrieves relevant papers but also explains which specific papers or data points influenced its answer, improving trust and reproducibility. A concrete workflow would be using PRISM-based models to build a literature-review agent that can cite its training-data sources for claims about high-redshift galaxy properties. A key caveat is that PRISM currently matches but does not exceed dense baseline accuracy, so there may be a small performance trade-off for interpretability.

Reason

Directly addresses interpretability and training-data attribution for LLMs, which is relevant to building trustworthy AI agents for scientific discovery and literature review in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the prototype neighborhoods correspond to semantically coherent groups (e.g., papers on similar galaxy types) or are dominated by surface-form features.
  • Look for experiments showing that the training-data attribution actually helps a human auditor correct or debug model outputs in a realistic scientific scenario.
  • Examine the scaling behavior: does the accuracy gap relative to dense baselines grow or shrink at larger model sizes (e.g., >1.6B parameters)?
Abstract

Knowing which training examples drive outputs is fundamental to auditing, correcting, and understanding language models, yet for modern LLMs this remains expensive, approximate, and largely post-hoc. Standard language models generate tokens through a dense network pathway, causing training data's influence to be distributed across parameters rather than organized along explicit, traceable components. We introduce a prototype language model architecture, Prototypes for Interpretable Sequence Modeling (PRISM), that forms each prediction via a sparse, non-negative mixture of learned prototypes, trained with clustering objectives that anchor each prototype to coherent neighborhoods of training examples. Across architectures from 130M to 1.6B parameters trained on up to 50B tokens, prototype language models either surpass or remain within 2.5 percentage points on average downstream accuracy of matched dense baselines. We show that sparse prototype structure localizes curvature in the loss landscape, yielding a more tractable Hessian and enabling training data attribution that is ~500x faster than post hoc baselines when consuming equivalent memory. Calibrating linear prototype controllers can improve downstream accuracy by roughly 3 points while tracing those corrections back to training neighborhoods, and targeted prototype suppression can remove model behaviors without finetuning or measurable loss in generation quality.

Graph-Native Reinforcement Learning Enables Traceable Scientific Hypothesis Generation through Conceptual Recombination

Subhadeep Pal , Shashwat Sourav , Tirthankar Ghosal , Markus J. Buehler

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper aims to generate scientifically valid hypotheses through multi-step, domain-grounded reasoning, addressing the problem that LLMs often produce fluent but untraceable responses. The main idea is Graph-PRefLexOR, a graph-native reasoning model fine-tuned with GRPO that organizes reasoning into explicit phases (mechanism exploration, graph construction, pattern extraction, hypothesis synthesis), linking neural language generation with symbolic relational structure. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because it provides a method for interpretable, traceable hypothesis generation that could be applied to problems like explaining little red dot spectra, AGN variability patterns, or galaxy evolution pathways. A concrete workflow would be: given a set of JWST observations of high-redshift galaxies, the model could propose causal mechanisms (e.g., AGN feedback, starburst quenching) and construct a graph of evidence linking observations to hypotheses. A key limitation is that the paper tests only on materials science questions, so performance on astronomical domain knowledge would need validation, and the graph construction may require adaptation to astronomical ontologies.

Reason

This paper directly addresses scientific hypothesis generation using graph-native reinforcement learning, with applications to materials science that could transfer to astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the graph-native reasoning can incorporate astronomical domain knowledge (e.g., SED templates, spectral line catalogs) as symbolic nodes.
  • Look for the evaluation metrics for reasoning traceability and how they measure causal coherence.
  • Examine the semantic diversity analysis to see if the model explores diverse hypotheses or converges on common explanations.
  • Check the test-time graph expansion analysis to understand how additional compute affects hypothesis quality in a bounded semantic space.
Abstract

Accelerating materials discovery requires AI systems that can generate scientifically valid hypotheses through multi-step, domain-grounded reasoning. Standard large language models often produce fluent but weakly traceable responses to open-ended materials design problems, making it difficult to determine whether final answers are supported by coherent intermediate reasoning. We develop Graph-PRefLexOR, a family of graph-native reasoning models fine-tuned with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to organize reasoning into explicit phases for mechanism exploration, graph construction, pattern extraction, and hypothesis synthesis. This design links neural language generation with symbolic relational structure, enabling causal connections to be constructed, inspected, and reused. On 100 open-ended questions from materials science and mechanics literature, Graph-PRefLexOR achieves 40-65% improvements over corresponding base models, with the largest gains in reasoning traceability. Embedding analyses show broader semantic exploration and approximately 2-3 times greater semantic diversity than baselines. Semantic backtracking and layer-wise hidden-state analyses further show stronger alignment between structured reasoning and final answers. Finally, test-time graph expansion reveals that additional compute primarily increases long-range conceptual recombination within a bounded semantic space, rather than simply expanding semantic coverage. These results establish graph-native reinforcement learning as a pathway toward interpretable AI systems for scientific hypothesis generation in materials design and other scientific applications.
