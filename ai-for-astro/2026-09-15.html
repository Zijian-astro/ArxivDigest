Multimodal-Multiresolution Foundation Model for Lunar Remote Sensing

Abstract

We present a multimodal foundation model for lunar remote sensing, pretrained from scratch on SomBench, a geographically partitioned corpus of nearly two million co-registered tile bundles spanning 11 modalities at two spatial scales (1 m/pixel and 100 m/pixel). The model adapts the TerraMind masked-token architecture with two lunar-specific extensions: acquisition geometry is provided as explicit context, and meter- and hundred-meter-scale tiles are trained jointly so that a single set of weights covers both resolutions. FlexiViT patch embeddings allow adaptation to different patch sizes without retraining, while modality-wise inputs enable flexible multimodal fine-tuning. Qualitative generation experiments suggest the model learns meaningful cross-modal correspondences, including terrain derivatives from elevation and illumination-consistent reflectance from geometry. We evaluate on four benchmarks: crater detection at WAC and NAC scales, irregular mare patch (IMP) segmentation, and polar ice prospectivity regression. Across tasks, the pretrained model matches or outperforms ImageNet-pretrained baselines and an architecturally identical random-init control. On multimodal ice prospectivity regression, pretrained variants achieve the best results, while the random-init model outperforms most baselines, suggesting gains arise from both the architecture and pretraining. Label efficiency is notable for WAC crater detection, where the pretrained model trained on 50% of the data exceeds the strongest ImageNet baseline trained on the full dataset. Among adaptation strategies, LoRA matches or surpasses full fine-tuning on crater detection and IMP segmentation while using far fewer trainable parameters, whereas full fine-tuning performs best for ice prospectivity regression. We release the pretrained checkpoint, benchmark datasets, and fine-tuning code to support reproducible lunar AI research.

Adapting Open-Weight MLLMs to Generate Point Prompts for Electron Microscopy Segmentation

Samia Mohinta , Albert Cardona

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether open-weight multimodal large language models can generate point prompts for a frozen promptable segmenter from natural-language requests, removing the need for manual clicks. The main idea is to convert masks from mitochondria datasets into training examples that pair images and instructions with centroid coordinates, then train LoRA adapters while freezing both the MLLM backbone and the microSAM segmenter. In plain terms, the language model learns to point at the right object in an image, and a separate segmentation model turns that point into a mask. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because promptable segmentation models like SAM are being used for JWST source extraction, and an MLLM that generates prompts from text could enable language-directed segmentation of galaxies, AGN, or artifacts. A concrete workflow would be to fine-tune an open-weight MLLM to output point prompts for JWST cutouts, then feed those points to a frozen astronomical segmenter for source masks. The caveat is that the paper uses electron microscopy data, so I should check whether the approach transfers to low-surface-brightness, blended, or multi-wavelength astronomical images.

Reason

This paper adapts open-weight multimodal LLMs to generate point prompts for electron microscopy segmentation, which is highly relevant to multimodal models combining images and language for segmentation, with clear transfer to JWST source segmentation and promptable segmentation workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the LoRA fine-tuning recipe and data conversion pipeline are described in enough detail to reproduce
  • Look for robustness tests under unseen natural-language formulations and whether they resemble astronomical queries
  • Check whether the method handles blended or overlapping sources, which is critical for JWST deblending
  • Verify whether the paper compares against supervised point detectors and discusses failure modes
Abstract

Promptable models such as microSAM segment electron microscopy (EM) images from point prompts, but automation requires generating prompts without user input. We ask whether open-weight multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can generate them from natural-language requests by returning coordinates to a frozen segmenter. To that end, we convert masks from three mitochondria datasets into training examples, pairing images and instructions with centroid coordinates, then train LoRA adapters while freezing the MLLM backbone and microSAM. We find that Qwen3-VL reaches segmentation AP$_{50}$ $0.736$ after supervised fine-tuning and reward optimization, up from $0.247$ without adaptation, while automatic prompt generation (APG) achieves $0.773$. In addition, two other MLLMs improve, reaching or exceeding APG. When compared with a supervised centroid-heatmap detector that reaches AP$_{50}$ $0.904$ for this mitochondria task, Qwen3-VL more closely matches the annotated point set and instance counts. Moreover, training on two public datasets transfers to an unseen third, while training on all three transfers to an independent EM volume. Robustness tests show stable performance under unseen formulations of the natural-language request, while the coordinates can be reused by a second segmenter. To our knowledge, this is the first feasibility study of open-weight MLLMs as EM point generators, providing an inspectable, language-directed link between localization and mask decoding.

OpenAl4S: Code as Action, Science as Sessions

Gongbo Zhang , Hao Li , Yu Wang , Mujie Lin , Liuzhenghao Lv , Yicheng Mao , Yimi Wang , Jun Zhu , Minhan Tang , Zhengxiang Jiang , Yusong Wang , Jiayu Yao , Kunpeng Ning , Dawei Pang , Yonghong Tian , OpenAI4S Community , Yuyang Liu , Li Yuan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how to build an AI research agent that remains inspectable, resumable, and reproducible over long computational studies. Its main idea is to treat code execution as the action space and research sessions as persistent state, with an append-only ledger recording every code cell, artifact, environment, and checkpoint. This is essentially a provenance-first agent architecture: instead of just chatting, the agent runs real Python or R kernels and keeps a full audit trail. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because JWST, Roman, and Rubin analyses involve long, multi-step pipelines that need reproducibility and branching. A concrete workflow could be an agent that runs photometric redshift or SED-fitting pipelines, records every step, and allows you to resume or branch a session. The main caveat is that the paper itself admits environment specification and full rerunnability remain weak, so reproducibility is still an open problem even in their system.

Reason

This is directly about AI co-scientists, research agents, reproducible scientific workflows, and persistent computational state, which aligns strongly with your interest in LLM agents for scientific discovery and astronomy workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the Action Ledger and workspace checkpoints handle external data dependencies, which is critical for astronomical pipelines
  • Look for whether the sandboxing and permission controls would allow access to large survey data or cluster resources
  • Check the evaluation scenarios for any spectroscopy or imaging analysis that resembles JWST workflows
  • Look for details on how session branching and recovery work in practice, since that matters for iterative scientific analysis
Abstract

AI co-scientists could accelerate computational research, but over a long-running study the workflow also has to stay inspectable, resumable and reproducible, which requires persistent computational state and provenance. Here we present OpenAI4S, an open-source scientific research agent built around the principle of \emph{Code as Action, Science as Sessions}. OpenAI4S combines a persistent computing runtime with research-session management: orchestration is handled through structured tool calls, while scientific actions are represented as complete code cells executed in persistent Python and R kernels. An append-only Action Ledger, per-cell execution records, versioned artifacts, environment records, and workspace checkpoints preserve how results were produced and support session recovery, branching, and extension. Configurable sandboxing, permission controls, and code and trajectory screening provide complementary safeguards. We evaluate OpenAI4S on 36 research scenarios spanning retrosynthesis, molecular dynamics, protein binder design, protein mutation, catalyst screening, and mineral spectroscopy, measuring scientific task accuracy, workflow completeness, and reproducibility of the resulting repositories. OpenAI4S achieves an overall score of 7.83, compared with 5.7--6.4 for a general-purpose coding harness evaluated with three frontier models, with the largest gains on long-horizon and computation-intensive workflows. These results suggest that integrating persistent execution with session-level provenance can improve the reliability of AI-assisted scientific workflows. Environment specification and full rerunnability remain weak for every evaluated system, ours included, so reproducibility is still an open problem for scientific agents. Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem that scientific methods are often lost when lab personnel change, making it hard to reproduce or build on prior work. LabAgent is a harness that lets AI agents reproduce and extend a lab's methods, with mechanisms to execute and verify skills and to record corrective experiences so similar errors can be avoided. It is evaluated on drug property prediction, biomedical problem analysis, protein variant effect prediction, and statistical genetics, ranking first against commercial generalist agents and reproducing a published figure. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because research groups face the same reproducibility problem with data-reduction pipelines, spectral-fitting methods, and survey-analysis workflows.   • Assess whether the corrective-experience memory could accumulate astronomy-specific failure modes and best practices
  • Check whether the harness supports multimodal inputs like images and spectra, or only tabular and text data
Abstract

Scientific research is a continuous process that emphasizes inheritance. Methods developed by predecessors are often expanded upon by new researchers to explore more novel and in-depth scientific questions. However, the change of lab staff, such as student graduation, leads to a lack of personnel capable of replicating methods. Methods that have been developed with significant effort and resources cannot be continued. To address these limitations, we propose LabAgent, a reproduce and discovery harness tailored for a lab's continuous work. LabAgent employs two mechanisms to guarantee that all skills can be executed and verified and to record the corrective methods and experiences, allowing for direct correction or avoidance of similar errors. We applied LabAgent to drug property prediction, biomedical problem analysis, protein variant effect prediction, and statistical genetics in life science domains. LabAgent ranks first over commercial generalist agents in every domain, and demonstrates accurate reproduction of a published figure. Overall, these results demonstrate that LabAgent can effectively integrate and reasonably expand laboratory knowledge.

Operational Range Bounding in Spectroscopy: A Safety Cage Framework for Machine Learning Models

Nikki Grens , Luís F. Simões , Kai Hou Yip , Theresa Lueftinger

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem of trusting black-box ML models in safety-critical space missions when ground truth is unavailable. It proposes a modular safety cage that runs in parallel to a prediction model and monitors uncertainty quantification, out-of-domain detection, and influence functions to constrain the model to a verified operational domain. The key result is that no single indicator catches all failure modes, but fusing indicators and rejecting about 20 percent of data reduces errors by 45 to 65 percent across domains. For astronomy, this is directly applicable to JWST and Ariel spectral retrieval, where instrument anomalies, stellar activity, and domain shifts can silently corrupt ML predictions. A concrete workflow would be wrapping a spectral fitting or transmission-spectrum extraction model with a safety cage that flags out-of-domain or high-influence predictions before they enter a science pipeline. The caveat is that the framework requires a validated in-domain reference and careful tuning of rejection thresholds, which may be costly for small datasets.

Reason

This paper directly addresses reliable ML for spectroscopy under domain shift, with a safety-cage framework for exoplanet transmission spectra, which is highly relevant to JWST spectral fitting and uncertainty estimation.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the specific uncertainty quantification and out-of-domain detection methods used and whether they are model-agnostic
  • Check the coverage-risk curves and how the 20 percent rejection translates to different scientific metrics
  • Look for whether the framework handles spectral data with noise, systematics, or missing wavelength coverage
  • Check whether the paper provides guidance on choosing indicator combinations for new instruments
Abstract

Ensuring the reliability of black-box machine learning models in safety-critical space missions remains a significant challenge, particularly when ground-truth is unavailable for validation. Although machine learning models offer a powerful means to augment standard pipelines by extracting transmission spectra from complex exoplanetary light curves, their susceptibility to unmodelled instrument anomalies, stellar activity, and domain shifts introduces unquantified risks. This study evaluates a modular safety cage architecture that operates as a parallel monitoring layer to assess the validity of a prediction without modifying the underlying estimator. By monitoring different runtime indicators, including uncertainty quantification, out-of-domain detection, and influence functions, the framework constrains the model's operational domain to a verified region. A controlled evaluation is conducted under both in-domain and cross-domain conditions, using datasets from the 2019 and 2021 editions of the Ariel Data Challenges. The results reveal that model failure is multifaceted and that no single indicator captures all failure modes, demonstrating the need for indicator fusion. The application of safety-driven rejection strategies shows that a modest 20% reduction in data coverage results in error reductions between 45% and 65% across different domains and evaluation metrics. Using a formalised coverage-risk framework, a systematic analysis of indicator combinations is performed to identify configurations that maximise risk-ranking accuracy and optimise the trade-off between data coverage and scientific performance. Safety cages provide a transparent mechanism for detecting unreliable predictions and represent a critical step towards the safe deployment of data-driven models in scientific applications, such as astrophysics, where ground truth is seldom available.

GeoSkill:Experience-Driven Hierarchical Skill Learning with Collaborative Revision forGeospatialAgents

Han Luo , Xian Xu , Yinhe Liu , Yanfei Zhong

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem that geospatial agents need to reuse prior execution experience for recurring analytical tasks, but existing memory-augmented methods struggle to summarize both long tool chains and tool-level constraints. It proposes GeoSkill, which builds a hierarchical skill bank separating planning skills from tool-usage skills, and a collaborative revision mechanism where judge, critic, and refiner agents identify and fix errors in the skill bank. The core idea is to distill past executions into reusable procedural knowledge and to avoid polluting that knowledge with unreliable self-reflection. This matters for astronomy because LLM agents for JWST or survey analysis will similarly need to accumulate reusable skills for tasks like data reduction, source extraction, SED fitting, and catalog cross-matching. A concrete workflow would be an agent that learns from repeated photometric-redshift or spectral-fitting runs and reuses validated tool chains on new datasets. The main caveat is that the evaluation is on geospatial benchmarks, so I should check whether the skill representation is general enough to transfer to astronomical tools and data formats.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the hierarchical skill bank represents planning versus tool-level knowledge and whether it is domain-agnostic
  • Look for details on the collaborative revision mechanism and how it prevents error propagation
  • Check whether the framework is evaluated on unseen tasks and whether skills transfer across task families
  • Assess whether the approach could integrate astronomical tools and data formats without redesign
Abstract

Geospatial agents are increasingly expected to support recurring and evolving analytical tasks rather than execute isolated workflows. In such settings, effective agents must distill prior execution experience into reusable geospatial procedural knowledge to guide future planning and tool use. However, existing memory-augmented paradigms struggle to summarize both long-horizon tool-chain orchestration experience and tool-level invocation constraints in geospatial analysis, while directly relying on LLM self-reflection to update experience often leads to misattribution and unreliable revisions. To address these challenges, we propose GeoSkill, an experience-driven hierarchical skill learning framework for geospatial agents. GeoSkill comprises two core components: (i) a Hierarchical Skill Bank (HSB), consisting of a Planning Skill Bank and a Tool Skill Bank, which respectively distill high-level task-planning experience and tool usage constraints, enabling structured representation and cross-task reuse of historical execution experience; and (ii) a Collaborative Trace-driven Skill Revision (CTSR) mechanism, where Judge, Critic, and Refiner collaboratively perform error identification, skill-level defect localization, and targeted modification, preventing misattributed and unreliable revisions from polluting the skill bank. GeoSkill learns and validates skills from historical executions during development, and freezes the skill bank for retrieval-only guidance on unseen tasks during deployment. Extensive experiments on EarthBench and ThinkGeo demonstrate that GeoSkill effectively transforms historical execution experience into reusable hierarchical skills, improving both end-to-end task accuracy and tool-execution reliability in geospatial tasks.

Positioning manuscripts in the scientific landscape with agentic AI

Jiawen Chen , Zichen Zhang , Bingxuan Li , Quan Sun , Yiyan Zhang , Edric Tam , Jinjie Lin , Didong Li , Yun Li , Bingxin Zhao

Short Digest

The paper asks whether agentic AI can help researchers navigate the publication process by inferring a manuscript's likely publication venue from its content and literature context. The main idea is PASS, an agentic system that reconstructs a manuscript's local scientific neighborhood, traces its topic trajectory, and reasons over field-specific journal spaces to predict top-matched venues. Evaluated on over 2,000 preprints across 16 biomedical fields, PASS achieves 50.3% Top-1 and 86.1% Top-5 accuracy, outperforming LLM baselines and journal-selection tools, with the literature retrieval module contributing most to performance. For astronomy, this is relevant because it demonstrates an LLM agent that builds a literature landscape and reasons over it, which could be adapted to finding relevant papers, collaborators, or observing proposals. A concrete workflow could be using a similar agent to position a JWST paper within the astrophysics literature or to recommend journals and referees. The main caveat is that the system is evaluated only in biomedical fields, so domain transfer to astronomy is untested.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the literature retrieval module is domain-general or relies on biomedical-specific resources
  • Look for how they construct the local scientific neighborhood and whether it could use ADS or arXiv data
  • Assess whether the agent's reasoning rationale is interpretable enough for scientific users
  • Check whether the benchmark leakage audit is rigorous and whether the method could be reproduced for astronomy
Abstract

Publishing a research manuscript is a routine yet demanding part of scientific life: time-consuming, stressful, and often uncertain in outcome. Recent advances in large language model (LLM)-based agentic AI have shown promise across a range of scientific tasks, and here we ask whether agentic AI can help researchers navigate the publication process itself by reliably inferring a manuscript's eventual publication venue from its content and literature context. We introduce PASS (Publication-oriented Agentic Scientific System), an agentic system that understands manuscripts within their domain-specific literature context and predicts top-matched publication venues. PASS positions each manuscript within its surrounding literature landscape by reconstructing its local scientific neighborhood, tracing its topic trajectory, and reasoning over field-specific journal spaces. Evaluated on a leakage-audited benchmark of over 2,000 preprints across 16 biomedical fields, PASS achieved Top-1 accuracy of 50.3% and Top-5 accuracy of 86.1%, outperforming state-of-the-art LLM baselines and established journal-selection tools. PASS-produced quality scores, such as impact potential and novelty, aligned with independent measures of publication outcome. We also found that the designed literature retrieval module is the strongest performance contributor, particularly for positioning manuscripts relative to nearby work, and that PASS maintained near-full performance from the abstract alone, whereas LLM baselines required the full manuscript text. An independent human evaluation found strong researcher agreement with PASS's manuscript understanding and recommendation rationale. PASS has been released as a public platform ( this https URL ) for broad researcher access.

Measuring the Creativity of Frontier LLMs in Automated Research

Yiheng Zhao , Mengzhuo Chen , Chengming Hu , Pengyi Liao , Yiran Pang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks how creative frontier LLMs actually are when they conduct automated research, since current evaluations mostly measure task success rather than novelty. The main idea is a set of metrics that separate valueness, whether an idea is useful, from novelty, measured in three ways: exact-match novelty, variable-level novelty, and whether the idea follows or departs from retrieved external knowledge. In plain terms, they try to quantify whether an LLM proposes genuinely new research directions or just recombines known ones. For astronomy, this matters because automated research agents are increasingly proposed for literature review, hypothesis generation, and data analysis, and we need to know whether they can generate novel scientific ideas rather than only summarize. A concrete workflow would be to apply these metrics to an LLM agent generating JWST follow-up proposals or anomaly hypotheses, checking whether the ideas explore new variables or just retrieve known results. The caveat is that the metrics rely on retrieved external knowledge and may be domain-sensitive, so I should check whether they were validated on scientific domains beyond the paper's test set.

Reason

This paper directly evaluates the creativity of frontier LLMs in automated research, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery methods and LLM research agents for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the correlation analysis between variable-level novelty and research performance
  • Check whether the metrics require a large external knowledge base and how that is constructed
  • Look for any discussion of applying the framework to scientific discovery in physical sciences
Abstract

Frontier LLMs are increasingly capable of conducting automated research, yet their creativity in this setting has not been systematically evaluated. In this paper, we propose a set of metrics to evaluate creativity along the two dimensions of valueness and novelty. Valueness assesses whether each proposed idea is useful, while novelty is evaluated from three perspectives: whether the same idea has appeared before (Exact-Match P-Novelty), whether a previously unexplored variable or variable combination is explored (Variable-level P-Novelty), and whether the idea directly follows retrieved external knowledge or departs from it (H-Novelty). Our evaluation shows that the models achieve relatively similar scores on most creativity metrics, but differ substantially in Variable-level P-Novelty, which reflects the breadth of research-space exploration. Further correlation and idea-level performance analyses show that Variable-level P-Novelty is the creativity dimension most strongly associated with research performance.
