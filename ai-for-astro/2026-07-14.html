An Autonomous Scientific Knowledge Generation Framework for AI-Driven Scientific Discovery

Dibakar Datta

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper presents an Autonomous Scientific Knowledge Generation Framework that transforms unstructured scientific publications into a structured, AI-ready knowledge base. It integrates ontology-guided literature acquisition, hybrid knowledge extraction, semantic harmonization, and validation, demonstrated on electro-optic materials. For astronomy, this is highly relevant: it could be used to build a knowledge base from the vast astronomical literature, enabling AI agents to answer questions, suggest observations, or design experiments based on published results. A concrete workflow could be using this framework to extract all known properties of high-redshift galaxies from JWST papers, then using that knowledge base to train a model or inform a new observing proposal. The main caveat is that the proof-of-concept is on a small scale (8 publications) and in materials science; scaling to the full astronomical literature and handling its complexity (e.g., diverse data formats, contradictory results) is a major challenge.

Reason

Directly addresses automated scientific knowledge generation from literature, a core component of AI-driven scientific discovery for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for details on the 'hybrid scientific knowledge extraction' method: how does it handle equations, tables, and figures common in astronomy papers?
  • Check if the ontology is domain-specific and how easily it could be adapted for astronomy (e.g., defining concepts like 'redshift', 'SED', 'AGN').
  • Examine the validation step: how does the framework ensure the extracted knowledge is accurate and consistent with the original publication?
  • Look for a discussion of scalability: can the framework handle thousands or millions of papers, and what are the computational costs?
Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming scientific discovery, but its effectiveness is fundamentally limited by the availability of structured scientific knowledge. Although existing databases have accelerated data-driven materials research, much of the knowledge needed for predictive modeling and inverse design remains embedded in unstructured scientific literature. We present an Autonomous Scientific Knowledge Generation Framework that transforms scientific publications into a Unified AI-Ready Scientific Knowledge Base. The framework integrates ontology-guided literature acquisition, hybrid scientific knowledge extraction, semantic harmonization, knowledge fusion, and validation within a unified workflow. Rather than treating literature retrieval, information extraction, and database construction as separate tasks, the framework progressively converts scientific publications into structured, semantically consistent, and provenance-preserving knowledge suitable for AI-driven reasoning. As a proof of concept, the framework was applied to electro-optic materials. Autonomous literature acquisition retrieved and validated about 1,000 publications from multiple scholarly repositories. A representative subset of eight publications was processed through the complete workflow, generating 29 structured scientific records that were harmonized into 7 canonical scientific records. The results demonstrate the complete transformation from scientific literature to an AI-ready scientific knowledge base while preserving quantitative measurements, operating conditions, provenance, and scientific context. The proposed framework provides a scalable, domain-independent foundation for predictive AI, generative AI, and closed-loop AI-driven scientific discovery.

GRASP: GRanularity-Aware Search Policy for Agentic RAG

Varun Gandhi , Jaewook Lee , Shantanu Todmal , Franck Dernoncourt , Ryan Rossi , Zichao Wang , Andrew Lan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the challenge of when and how to retrieve information in agentic RAG systems. It introduces GRASP, a reinforcement learning framework that trains agents to adaptively use semantic search, keyword search, and paragraph reading. The agent learns to skim and scan, using different tools for different needs. This is directly applicable to building astronomy research agents that need to retrieve relevant papers, data, or code. The interpretable skimming and scanning behavior is a strong advantage. However, the experiments are on multi-hop reasoning benchmarks, not astronomy-specific tasks. The method requires training with a reward function, which may need careful design for astronomy.

Reason

Directly addresses agentic RAG with adaptive retrieval strategies, highly relevant for building LLM agents for astronomy literature review and data analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the specific reward function design; assess if it can be adapted for astronomy tasks (e.g., finding papers that describe a specific method).
  • Check the qualitative analysis of skimming and scanning behavior; see if the learned policy is interpretable and controllable.
  • Examine the ablation studies on the different retrieval tools; understand when each tool is most useful.
Abstract

Agentic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) extends static RAG by allowing language models to iteratively reason, generate search queries, retrieve evidence, and predict answers. However, it remains challenging for models to decide when to retrieve, whether to use lexical matching or semantic similarity, and how to control context granularity to prevent irrelevant tokens from interfering with agent reasoning. In this paper, we introduce GRASP, a reinforcement learning (RL) framework for training agents to adaptively coordinate complementary retrieval tools during multi-step reasoning. GRASP provides the agent with semantic search, keyword search, and paragraph-reading actions, enabling it to retrieve sentence-level evidence and expand further context only when needed. We train the policy with a reward that jointly accounts for answer accuracy, grounded reading, complementary search, and turn efficiency. Experiments on multi-hop reasoning benchmarks show that GRASP improves both retrieval recall and downstream question answering performance compared with single-step retrieval, prompting-based agentic RAG, and RL-based retrieval baselines. Qualitative and ablation analyses show that the learned policy develops interpretable skimming and scanning behavior: it uses semantic search for broad exploration, paragraph reading for local verification, and keyword search for entity-specific evidence. These results suggest that learning to coordinate retrieval signals and context granularity is critical for agent's correct reasoning.

NVAITC AI Scientist: A Governed End-to-End Research System -- A Hypertension GWAS Case Study

Eddie Huang (1), Ken Liao (1), Iven Fu (1), Yang-Hsien Lin (1), Chao-Shun Zhan (1), Andy Liao (1), Virginia Chen (1), Johnson Sun (1), Pika Wang (1), Richard Huang (1), Jiun-Cheng Jiang (1), Ting-Yuan Liu (2 and 3), Hsing-Fang Lu (2, 3, and 4), Ray Y. Short Digest

This paper asks whether a governed agentic research system can support end-to-end scientific workflows while keeping protected data within institutional privacy boundaries. It presents NVAITC AI Scientist (NAIS), which integrates proposal review, execution planning, governed computational routing, reproducible workflow orchestration, evidence generation, and scientist-in-the-loop oversight. The system was validated on a real-world hypertension GWAS using data from 286,422 individuals, reproducing established loci and achieving a multimodal graph neural network AUC of 0.842 for drug-induced liver injury prediction. For astronomy, this architecture could be adapted to coordinate JWST data analysis workflows, such as automated SED fitting, source classification, and literature cross-referencing, while maintaining reproducibility and human oversight. An astronomer could imagine using NAIS to orchestrate a multi-step analysis of little red dots: querying catalogs, running spectral fitting, cross-matching with literature, and generating publication-ready outputs. A caveat is that the system is designed for biomedical data with privacy constraints, so adapting it to astronomical data (which is typically public) may require rethinking the governance and routing components.

Reason

Presents a governed end-to-end agentic research system for biomedical discovery, directly relevant to building AI scientists for astronomy with human oversight.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the system handles proposal review and execution planning: is there a template that could be adapted for astronomy research proposals?
  • Look for details on the scientist-in-the-loop oversight mechanism and how it handles discrepancies or iterative refinement.
  • Examine the reproducibility features: does the system log all steps, parameters, and intermediate outputs for full traceability?
  • Check whether the multimodal graph neural network component could be repurposed for astronomical multi-wavelength data fusion.
Abstract

Agentic research systems are emerging as a new paradigm for coordinating scientific workflows beyond isolated model inference, code generation, or statistical analysis. However, deployment in institutional biomedical environments requires governed mechanisms for research planning, data access, workflow orchestration, evidence tracking, reproducibility, and human oversight. We present NVAITC AI Scientist (NAIS), a governed end-to-end agentic research system designed to support domain-general scientific workflows while keeping protected data within institutional privacy boundaries. NAIS integrates proposal review, execution planning, governed computational routing, reproducible workflow orchestration, evidence generation, and scientist-in-the-loop oversight. We validate NAIS in a real-world hypertension genome-wide association study (GWAS) using hospital-linked genotype and electronic health record (EHR) data from 286,422 individuals under an aggregate-only data policy. The agent planned cohort extraction, orchestrated GWAS execution, generated quality-control summaries, and drafted publication-oriented outputs. Human-AI review identified phenotype discrepancies and enabled iterative refinement of the hypertension definition. After reconciliation, the agent-orchestrated GWAS reproduced established hypertension loci, including FGF5, ATP2B1, CNNM2, FTO, and GRB14, with the strongest signal at FGF5 reaching $-\log_{10}(p) \sim 70$. As a secondary demonstration, NAIS also supported a drug-induced liver injury prediction workflow, achieving a multimodal graph neural network AUC of 0.842. These results demonstrate that governed agentic research systems can support scalable AI-assisted biomedical discovery while producing outputs comparable to expert-led workflows.

RAGU: A Multi-Step GraphRAG Engine with a Compact Domain-Adapted LLM

Mikhail Komarov , Ivan Bondarenko , Stanislav Shtuka , Oleg Sedukhin , Roman Shuvalov , Yana Dementyeva , Matvey Solovyov , Nikolay O. Nikitin

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem of noisy entity extraction and brittle retrieval in existing GraphRAG systems. RAGU separates extraction from consolidation using two-stage typed extraction, DBSCAN deduplication, LLM summarization, and Leiden community detection. A key insight is that a compact 7B model (Meno-Lite-0.1) optimized for language skills can match or outperform much larger models on knowledge graph construction and GraphRAG tasks. For astronomy, this could enable building structured knowledge graphs from the astronomical literature, linking papers, objects, methods, and datasets. A concrete workflow would be using RAGU to ingest a corpus of JWST papers and automatically construct a queryable knowledge graph of high-redshift galaxy findings, AGN properties, and little red dot discoveries. The main caveat is that the evaluation is on medical and general English tasks; performance on astronomy-specific terminology and multi-modal content (figures, spectra) is not tested. The system runs on a single GPU and is open-source, making it accessible.

Reason

GraphRAG engine with domain-adapted LLM directly relevant to retrieval-augmented generation for scientific literature and knowledge graph construction in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the two-stage extraction pipeline handles astronomy-specific entity types (e.g., galaxy names, instrument modes, spectral lines) or requires custom schema definition.
  • Look for evaluation of the knowledge graph quality on a scientific domain (medical is used; astronomy is not tested).
  • Verify that the system can ingest PDFs and extract entities from figure captions and tables, not just plain text.
  • Check the modularity: can the extractor be swapped for a domain-adapted model (e.g., fine-tuned on astronomy papers)?
Abstract

Graph retrieval-augmented generation (GraphRAG) enhances large language models with structured knowledge, yet existing systems construct knowledge graphs in a single extraction pass, producing noisy entities and brittle retrieval. RAGU, an open-source modular GraphRAG engine, addresses this by separating extraction from consolidation: entities and relations pass through two-stage typed extraction, DBSCAN-backed deduplication, LLM summarization, and Leiden community detection. A key insight motivates a compact extractor: the skills an in-pipeline LLM needs - comprehension, extraction, reasoning over context - are language skills that grow only weakly with model size, unlike factual world knowledge. Accordingly, we train Meno-Lite-0.1, a 7B model optimized for language skills, which outperforms Qwen2.5-32B on knowledge-graph construction (+12.5% relative harmonic mean) and matches it on English GraphRAG tasks. On GraphRAG-Bench (Medical), RAGU retrieves the most complete context at every factoid level (evidence recall up to 0.84 vs. $\leq$0.76) and overtakes HippoRAG2 on synthesis tasks; on multi-hop factoid QA, the apparent HippoRAG2 advantage is shown to be largely an answer-format artifact. RAGU is installable via $\texttt{pip install graph_ragu}$, runs on a single GPU, and is released under MIT. The source code is publicly available at this https URL , and the Meno-Lite-0.1 model can be obtained from this https URL .

Imaging-101: Benchmarking LLM Coding Agents on Scientific Computational Imaging

Short Digest

This paper introduces Imaging-101, a benchmark of 57 computational imaging tasks across six scientific domains, each requiring a pipeline of preprocessing, forward physics modeling, inverse solver, and visualization. It evaluates seven frontier LLMs on planning, function-level unit tests, and end-to-end reconstruction, revealing systematic challenges in algorithm selection and pipeline integration. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because JWST and other observatories produce computational imaging problems (e.g., deblending, PSF modeling, source reconstruction). An astronomer could imagine using an LLM agent to help build a reconstruction pipeline for JWST data, but the benchmark reveals current LLMs struggle with domain-specific physics and conventions. The caveat is that the benchmark tasks are not astronomy-specific, so transferability needs verification.

Reason

This paper directly benchmarks LLM coding agents on computational imaging tasks, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check which scientific domains are included and if any are astronomy or astrophysics
  • Look for the specific failure modes of LLMs on physics modeling tasks
  • Examine the evaluation metrics to see if they align with astronomical reconstruction quality
Abstract

Computational imaging, which recovers hidden signals from indirect, noisy measurements, underpins quantitative discovery across scientific disciplines, yet building a correct reconstruction pipeline demands deep domain expertise and remains laborious even for domain scientists. We introduce Imaging-101, a benchmark of 57 expert-verified computational imaging tasks spanning six scientific domains, each grounded in a peer-reviewed paper and canonicalized into a standardized four-stage pipeline (preprocessing, forward physics modeling, inverse solver, and visualization) Three evaluation tracks (planning, function-level unit tests, and end-to-end reconstruction) probe distinct agent capabilities across the full pipeline. Evaluating seven frontier LLMs uncovers systematic challenges in applying coding agents to computational imaging that go beyond those exposed by general coding benchmarks, spanning algorithm selection, physical convention handling, and pipeline integration. These findings highlight concrete capability gaps and point toward skill-augmented, domain-specialized agents as a practical path to reliable computational imaging assistance.

TabPFN beyond Tabular Data: Calibration and Accuracy on Multimodal Embeddings

Jingxiang Zhang , Lujia Zhong , Zijie Zhu , Shuo Huang , Yuang Xu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper evaluates TabPFN, a transformer-based model for tabular data, as a plug-and-play classification head for frozen image, text, and audio encoders. It shows that TabPFN provides better calibration (lower ECE) and competitive accuracy compared to linear heads or k-NN, especially at moderate shot counts. For astronomy, this is highly relevant: one could use a pretrained JWST image encoder and replace the final linear classifier with TabPFN for better-calibrated source classification (e.g., star/galaxy/AGN). A caveat is that TabPFN's accuracy advantage diminishes at low shot counts and high feature dimensions, which may occur with rare source types or high-dimensional embeddings.

Reason

TabPFN as a calibration-friendly classification head for multimodal embeddings; directly applicable to astronomical source classification with frozen encoders.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the feature dimension threshold (d <= 32) where TabPFN excels; see if astronomical embeddings exceed this.
  • Look for the calibration metric (ECE) and whether it is evaluated on out-of-distribution data.
  • See if TabPFN can handle multi-label or hierarchical classification common in astronomy.
Abstract

Few-shot multimodal classification commonly attaches a lightweight head, such as $k$-nearest neighbors, logistic regression, or a linear SVM, to a frozen pretrained encoder. Although computationally efficient, these heads can produce poorly calibrated confidence scores, limiting their reliability in calibration-sensitive applications. We evaluate TabPFN as a plug-and-play, zero-gradient classification head for frozen image, text, and audio encoders. Across 22{,}820 evaluation episodes spanning 14 datasets, 11 encoders, and three modalities, TabPFN achieves the best mean rank among nine classification heads on both negative log-likelihood (NLL) and expected calibration error (ECE). At a representative setting, it reduces NLL by 48--62\% and ECE by 2.1--5.3$\times$ relative to the average of the eight baselines while matching or exceeding their average accuracy. Its accuracy advantage is conditional, concentrating at moderate-to-high shot counts and low-to-moderate feature dimensions ($k \ge 50$, $d \le 32$), and diminishing when labeled data are scarce, feature dimensions are high, or competing methods approach ceiling accuracy. In targeted backbone-adaptation experiments, replacing the trained linear head with TabPFN substantially improves calibration while preserving competitive accuracy. These results provide empirical guidance for using TabPFN as a training-free head in calibration-sensitive multimodal classification. To support transparency and reproducibility, we publicly release the source code, experiment configurations, and evaluation scripts in our GitHub repository: this https URL .

ResearchQA: Benchmarking Citation-Grounded Question-Answering on Scientific Papers

Saba Imran , Debanjum Singh Solanky

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether LLM answers to scientific questions are actually supported by verifiable citations from the source papers. It introduces ResearchQA, a benchmark of 6,211 question-answer pairs from 494 open-access papers, designed to test lookup, comprehension, multi-hop, and adversarial reasoning. The main finding is that citation-based metrics (section coverage, citation accuracy) separate model performance much more clearly than generic LLM evaluator scores. For astronomy, this benchmark provides a template for building citation-grounded QA systems that could help researchers verify claims about JWST sources or high-redshift galaxies. An astronomer could imagine using a similar approach to audit an LLM agent tasked with summarizing the literature on little red dots. A caveat is that the benchmark currently covers general scientific domains, not astronomy-specific papers, so transferability to astrophysical literature needs verification.

Reason

Directly benchmarks LLMs on citation-grounded QA from scientific papers, which is a core capability for AI research agents in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the benchmark includes any astronomy or astrophysics papers, or if the domain coverage can be extended.
  • Look for the specific rubric used by the LLM evaluator to assess answer correctness and citation grounding.
  • Examine the types of adversarial questions included and whether they test for hallucination or citation misuse.
Abstract

Large language models are increasingly used to assist scientific reading, but existing evaluation methods often fail to detect whether answers are supported by verifiable citations. We introduce ResearchQA, a benchmark of 6,211 single-paper question-answer pairs from 494 open-access papers spanning eight domains and four question types: lookup, comprehension, multi-hop, and adversarial. ResearchQA is designed for citation-grounded evaluation: it permits multiple valid supporting passages for a claim and rewards grounded refusal when the source paper does not support an answer. We evaluate eight leading closed- and open-weight models in a citation-grounded chat-with-paper setting using a deterministic citation matcher and an LLM-based rubric evaluator. Citation-based metrics separate systems more clearly than LLM-evaluator scores: section coverage and citation accuracy vary substantially across models, while evaluator scores remain tightly compressed. We further find that open-weight models approach the best closed-model citation accuracy while achieving 3 to 6 times lower per-example latency. We release the benchmark, evaluation harness, and evaluator prompt.

Neural Discovery of Memory and Nonlocal Kernels in Integro-Differential Equations with Constrained Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks

Aruzhan Tleubek , Salah A Faroughi

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the inverse problem of discovering the unknown kernel in integro-differential equations from sparse, noisy data. It uses a differentiable solver with a Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) to represent the kernel, enforcing physical constraints like positivity and monotonicity. For astronomy, this could model nonlocal effects in radiative transfer or wave propagation in astrophysical plasmas. You could apply it to infer memory kernels from time-series data of stellar oscillations or supernova light curves. A limitation is that the method is demonstrated on 1D and 2D toy problems, and scaling to high-dimensional astronomical data may require significant adaptation.

Reason

The paper discovers memory/nonlocal kernels in integro-differential equations using neural networks, which is directly relevant to modeling physical processes in astrophysics, such as radiative transfer or plasma dynamics. The use of constrained KANs for interpretable symbolic regression is a strong methodological match.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the robustness of kernel recovery under varying noise levels and sparsity of observations.
  • Look for examples of symbolic regression output and whether the closed-form kernels are physically interpretable.
  • Examine the computational cost of the differentiable solver for larger spatial domains.
Abstract

Discovering the memory or nonlocal kernel governing an integro-differential equation (IDE) from sparse and noisy observations is an ill-posed inverse problem. Existing identification methods often rely on problem-specific analytical derivations, specialized observation requirements, or restrictive assumptions about the kernel, limiting their applicability across different classes of IDEs. In this work, we propose a differentiable-solver-based framework for discovering memory and nonlocal kernels directly from spatiotemporal observations. Within the solver, the unknown kernel is represented using a constrained Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN) parameterization, with the physical constraints imposed through two different approaches: a Bernstein-polynomial-based Monotone--Convex KAN (MC-KAN), whose coefficient constraints enforce positivity, monotonic decrease, and convexity by construction, and a Chebyshev-based KAN (Cheb-KAN), in which the same properties are encouraged through soft penalty terms. After training, symbolic regression is applied to the learned kernels to obtain interpretable closed-form representations. We evaluate both methods on benchmarks spanning a one-dimensional Volterra equation, a one-dimensional viscoelastic wave partial integro-differential equation, and a two-dimensional nonlocal reaction-diffusion equation with an anisotropic coupled kernel. For the 1D problems, both methods recover the correct kernel functional form and achieve comparable solution-reconstruction accuracy. In contrast, for the sparse and noisy 2D nonlocal problem, the hard-constrained MC-KAN consistently achieves lower kernel reconstruction errors than the soft-constrained Cheb-KAN. Our results demonstrate that enforcing physically motivated shape constraints by construction provides greater robustness than soft penalties for multidimensional kernel discovery from sparse and noisy observations.
