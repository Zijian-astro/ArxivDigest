AI Research Preference Models

Thomas Simon Foster , Bassel Al Omari , Tingchen Fu , Thomas Mann , Carl Domond , Lucia Cipolina-Kun , Bhavul Gauri , Muna Aghamelu , Alexander D. Goldie , Eryk Helenowski , Jean-Christophe Gagnon-Audet , Alberto Pepe , Saba Nazir , Daniel Izcovich , Noam Levi , Rishi Hazra , Karen Hambardzumyan , Nicolas Baldwin , Xian Li , Martin Josifoski , Paris Giampouras , Masoud Jalili Sabet , Anya Sims , Hela Momand , Tatiana Shavrina , Despoina Magka , Jason Weston , Yulin Wang , Anirudh Goyal , João Henriques , Yoram Bachrach , Emily McMilin , Jakob Nicolaus Foerster

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the bottleneck of evaluating many candidate solutions in AI research agents, where running experiments is costly. It introduces AI Research Preference Models (RPMs) that predict which candidates are worth executing, using frozen language models either by reasoning over plans and prior results or by running small pilot experiments. RPMs improve agent performance and reduce execution time, which could help astronomers prioritize which data analysis or model-fitting approaches to try first. For JWST or survey data, you could imagine using RPMs to select among different SED-fitting or source-classification pipelines before running expensive computations. However, the method is demonstrated on ML research tasks, so you should check if it transfers to domain-specific evaluation metrics and whether the pilot experiments are feasible for your data.

Reason

Directly targets AI research agents and automated scientific discovery, with a focus on efficient experiment selection under budget constraints—highly relevant to your interest in research agents and automated workflows for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether RPMs are evaluated on tasks with domain-specific metrics (e.g., accuracy vs. physical plausibility) or only generic ML benchmarks.
  • Look for details on how pilot experiments are designed—could they be adapted to run on small subsets of astronomical data?
  • Inspect the budget allocation strategy: does it assume a fixed number of candidates, and how does it scale with larger candidate pools?
Abstract

AI research agents (AIRA) can now propose, implement, and evaluate their own machine learning experiments, but progress on frontier tasks is throttled by cost: a candidate solution can be written in minutes, whereas evaluating it can take hours to days of GPU time. An agent can therefore propose far more candidates than it can afford to run, and its progress depends on its research preference: how it allocates a fixed execution budget across many candidates. We introduce AI Research Preference Models (RPMs) that predict which of multiple candidate solutions are most worth executing, without paying the cost of executing them all. We build RPMs from frozen pretrained language models (with no task-specific training), in two forms: an inference-only model that reasons over candidate plans, code, and prior executed solutions, and an agentic model that additionally runs small-scale pilot experiments before deciding. We integrate both into the AIRA-dojo search agent and evaluate on AIRS-Bench, a recent benchmark of machine learning research tasks for AI research agents. The two variants raise the average normalized score from 0.684 to 0.711 and 0.729 respectively, and reach the unguided agent's 24-hour performance in roughly 15 hours, using less than two-thirds of its execution budget. Our best RPMs also yield new state-of-the-art results on two AIRS-Bench tasks.

Catching the Imposter: Self-Supervised Learning of Physical Coherence with Cross-Entity Feature Permutations

Aleksei Rozanov , Arvind Renganathan , Vipin Kumar

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem of learning representations from scientific data where features obey physical laws. It proposes a pretext task that swaps features between entities and trains an encoder to detect the swap, forcing it to learn cross-feature dependencies. This matters for astronomy because it offers a way to pre-train models on multi-wavelength or multi-variable astronomical data, potentially improving downstream tasks like classification or regression. I could imagine applying it to JWST or multi-wavelength catalogs to learn physically coherent embeddings for galaxies or AGN. Before investing time, check if the method scales to large astronomical datasets and if the physical coherence assumption holds for noisy observations.

Reason

Introduces a self-supervised learning method exploiting physical coherence in scientific data, directly applicable to astronomical datasets and foundation models.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the method is demonstrated on data with missing values or noise, common in astronomy
  • Look for details on how the permutation strategy handles categorical vs continuous features
  • See if the downstream tasks include regression or classification that could map to photometric redshift or SED fitting
Abstract

Scientific data often describe entities whose features are jointly governed by the laws of physics, yet existing self-supervised learning (SSL) objectives largely ignore this physical coherence. We introduce imposter, a discriminative pretext task that replaces subsets of an entity's features with real observations donated by another entity and trains the encoder to identify the swapped features. Because every donated value is individually plausible, the task can only be solved by learning cross-feature physical dependencies. We evaluate the proposed objectives on global ERA5-Land reanalysis data using 21 environmental variables and assess the learned representations on seven downstream tasks spanning climate classification, carbon flux estimation, and streamflow prediction. Our study includes, to our knowledge, the first systematic comparison of self-supervised objectives for land-surface modeling under a shared architecture and pre-training budget. We find that the most effective pretext task depends on the downstream task family rather than any single objective's superiority, and that imposter provides complementary information when combined with existing SSL objectives. These results suggest that physical coherence is a valuable new source of self-supervision for scientific foundation models.

The Past and Future of AI Scientists

Ross D. King

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper surveys the past and future of AI Scientists, machines that can automate the entire scientific process from hypothesis generation to experiment execution. It argues that the central challenge is no longer automating individual components but integrating them into coherent systems, using examples like Adam and Eve. For astronomy, this suggests a roadmap for building agents that can autonomously analyze JWST data, form hypotheses about high-redshift galaxies, and design follow-up observations. One could imagine using such an integrated agent to manage a full workflow from literature review to SED fitting and interpretation. However, the paper is a survey and does not provide concrete implementations, so one should check for specific architectural details or case studies that could be adapted.

Reason

Directly addresses the concept of AI scientists and automated scientific discovery, which is a core interest. It surveys the integration of foundation models, agents, and robotics for automating science, relevant to building research agents for astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for concrete examples of how AI Scientists integrate literature, data analysis, and experimentation in practice
  • Check whether the paper discusses specific challenges or solutions for handling large, multimodal datasets like those from JWST
  • Assess the feasibility of adapting the proposed architectures to a single-domain astronomy workflow
Abstract

We present a survey of the past and future of AI Scientists: machines capable of automating science. AI Scientists can originate hypotheses, deduce their consequences, design and execute experiments, interpret their results, and revise their beliefs. Such systems are integrated scientific agents, connected to the literature, formal knowledge, mathematical models, simulations, data-analysis systems and physical laboratories. Adam was the first machine to make novel scientific discoveries through cycles of hypothesis formation and physical experimentation. Eve established the architecture of the modern self-driving laboratory. Foundation models, autonomous agents and laboratory robotics now make it possible to build systems far more general than either Adam or Eve. The central problem is no longer whether individual components of science can be automated. They can. The problem is integration. AI Scientists must combine neural learning with logic, probability, mathematics, causal reasoning, simulation, experimental design, robotics and formal scientific records. AI Scientists have the potential to transform science: to make science faster, cheaper, more systematic and more reproducible. AI Scientists could investigate systems too complicated for unaided human science, and enable thousands of AI scientists to work together on single problems. The Nobel Turing Challenge sets the goal of developing by 2050 AI systems capable of automating Nobel-quality discoveries. Progress is ahead of schedule. When we succeed it will create a new form of science and transform the world.

Evaluating Agentic Learning Harness Capabilities Without Labels via the Scaling Hypothesis

Aryan Luthra , Kshitij Jain , Siddharth Arya , Bobby Filar , Anna Bertiger

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem of evaluating agentic learning harnesses (LLMs paired with retrieval/memory) when labeled benchmarks are unavailable or unreliable, common in operational settings. The main idea is to use a stronger teacher model to provide sparse corrections to a smaller student, and score the harness by how much the student converges toward the teacher over time. This matters for astronomy because JWST and survey data often lack complete labels, and this method could help assess whether an AI agent improves from feedback in such settings. A concrete workflow could be using a teacher model to guide a student model in classifying high-redshift galaxies or AGN from JWST images, with sparse human corrections. A caveat is that the method assumes a stronger teacher exists and that teacher-relative improvement correlates with true improvement, which may not hold for all astronomical tasks.

Reason

The paper introduces a teacher-student framework for evaluating continual learning harnesses without labels, which is directly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents that need to improve from feedback. The scaling hypothesis and teacher-relative lift could transfer to astronomy workflows where labeled data are scarce.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the teacher-student framework is validated on tasks beyond cybersecurity, such as classification or regression relevant to astronomy.
  • Look for details on how sparse corrections are sampled and whether the method handles noisy or biased corrections.
  • Examine the correlation between teacher-relative lift and gold-standard improvement across different model families and harness designs.
Abstract

Agentic "Continual Learning Harnesses", systems that pair an LLM with retrieval or memory to improve from feedback without retraining, have shown growing value in cybersecurity. But their value is conventionally measured by gains against labeled benchmarks, an approach that often fails in operational security settings. Benchmark labels are scarce, stale, and unrepresentative, so a practitioner often cannot tell whether a given harness helps at all or which of two is better for their task. Traditional LLM-as-a-judge offers little signal because it is no stronger than the agent it evaluates, and distillation is unreliable on scarce, sporadic, and biased labels. We propose a framework for evaluating learning harnesses end-to-end without a labeled benchmark, grounded in the scaling hypothesis. A stronger teacher model provides sparsely sampled corrections to a smaller student with a continual learning harness. We score a harness by how much its student converges toward the teacher over time. Across security tasks, model families, and harness designs, we show that improvement relative to the teacher correlates with improvement relative to a held-out gold standard, validating teacher-relative lift as a proxy for true harness uplift when labels are absent. We further show that LLM-as-a-judge between similarly powered models yields no usable signal. These results suggest that a teacher-sized model can be improved through the same harness when humans provide the same kind of sparse, high-precision corrections.

Consensus-gated Multi-Agent Neural Architecture Search for Seismic Fault Segmentation

Shehram Baig , Ahmad Mustafa

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem of designing neural networks for seismic fault segmentation under limited labeled data, a common challenge in geophysical applications. The main idea is to use a panel of three large language models (LLMs) that debate and reach consensus on candidate architectures, then write and validate PyTorch code in an automated loop. This matters for astronomy because it demonstrates a low-cost, agentic approach to architecture discovery that could be applied to JWST or survey data with limited labels. A concrete workflow could involve using this method to optimize a model for classifying high-redshift galaxies or detecting anomalies in imaging data. However, the paper focuses on seismic data and a specific parameter budget, so you should check if the approach generalizes to your data modalities and compute constraints.

Reason

The paper uses LLM agents for automated architecture search, which is directly relevant to your interest in research agents and automated scientific discovery. The method of consensus-gated multi-agent NAS could transfer to optimizing models for astronomical data analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the LLM debate and consensus mechanism is described in enough detail to replicate for other domains
  • Look for details on how the search space is defined (since it operates on source code, not a predefined operation menu)
  • Examine the validation loop and whether it could handle multi-modal astronomical datasets
Abstract

Neural networks for seismic fault segmentation are often borrowed from computer vision and medical imaging domains where they train under relatively much larger labeled data resources. Optimizing their architecture under tight labeled data budgets as are common in geophysical applications is not a trivial problem. Manually designing data-optimal architectures is time-consuming while classical neural architecture search (NAS) is restricted to hand-crafted search spaces and large compute budgets. We present an agentic NAS system in which a panel of three large language models (Claude, GPT-5.1, and Gemini~2.5~Pro) debates each candidate architecture to unanimous consensus, authors the complete PyTorch implementation, cross-reviews it, and submits it to an automated validate-train-score loop with a hard 450K parameter budget, keep-or-revert lineage, and a memory of failed mechanisms. Operating on source code rather than a predefined operation menu, the search ran on a single consumer GPU and trained only eight candidates. It discovered \ours{}: a 425K-parameter encoder-decoder with a strip-pooling bottleneck, squeeze-and-excitation gating, an asymmetric one-conv decoder, and a feature-pyramid fusion neck. Trained under a protocol identical to all baselines on sections derived from the Thebe fault dataset, it attains the highest F1 (0.578) and IoU of all models tested while being the smallest, outperforming a published-capacity U-Net (31M parameters, F1 0.484), DeepLabV3-ResNet50 (39.6M, 0.516), an Attention U-Net(1.83M, 0.502). The search cost 101 LLM calls ($\sim$1.15M input / 0.39M output tokens) and roughly one GPU-day, making consensus-gated LLM panels a practical, low-cost route to domain-specific architecture discovery.
