Evolutionary Ensemble Search: Council-Guided Program Evolution with Persistent Memory

Juan P. Madrigal-Cianci , Eshan Chordia

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper introduces Evolutionary Ensemble Search (EES), a system that constructs machine-learning procedures through expert-guided program evolution. A role-specialized council of LLM agents turns task evidence and experimental results into structured search directions, an orchestrator allocates them to execution specialists, and an evolutionary engine selects parents, diagnoses errors, and produces descendants through code mutation, pipeline edits, and crossover. Each child program must execute and acquire its own validation evidence, and compatible predictions compete in a validation-gated ensemble stage, while session memory and problem-indexed lessons let the search accumulate knowledge across runs. On MLE-bench Lite, the system reaches medal-threshold artifacts on 19 of 22 tasks, spanning text, images, tables, audio, and scientific geometry. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because it is essentially an automated ML pipeline builder that could be pointed at JWST photometric classification, spectral fitting, or anomaly detection tasks, and its persistent memory and cross-run lessons align with reproducible research agents. A concrete workflow could be using EES to evolve a pipeline for galaxy morphology classification or photometric redshift estimation from multimodal inputs. The caveat is that the reported results are a development ledger, not a blind autonomous-agent success rate, and the system is complex, so you should check whether the architecture is reproducible and whether the council guidance requires human expertise to be effective.

Reason

This paper presents an evolutionary ensemble search architecture with LLM-based council guidance, persistent memory, and cross-modal program evolution. It is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery, research agents, and LLM-driven ML pipeline construction that could be applied to astronomy workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the architecture is described in enough detail to reimplement or whether code is released
  • Look for whether the evaluation includes scientific or tabular tasks close to astronomy workflows
  • Check whether the persistent memory and lesson retrieval are validated across runs or only described
  • Check whether the ensemble stage has safeguards against overfitting to the validation set
Abstract

Evolutionary Ensemble Search (EES) constructs machine-learning procedures through expert-guided program evolution. A role-specialized council turns task evidence and experimental results into structured search directions. An orchestrator allocates these directions to execution specialists and an evolutionary engine. The engine selects measured parents, diagnoses their errors, and produces descendants through code mutation, structured pipeline edits, and crossover. Each child must execute and acquire its own validation evidence. Population archives retain useful alternatives, while compatible predictions compete in a validation-gated ensemble stage. Search adapts through parent-relative operator credit, session memory, and problem-indexed lessons retrieved across runs. We specify these mechanisms, distinguish their execution profiles, and define the contracts required to compare candidates as their computations change. A public MLE-bench Lite development ledger records medal-threshold artifacts on 19 of 22 tasks (86.36\%), with best outcomes of 11 gold, five silver, and three bronze. The procedures span text, images, tables, audio, scientific geometry, and deterministic transformations. The campaign includes grade feedback between runs, external-source routes, and mixed confirmation procedures; its aggregate is an achieved development result, not a blind autonomous-agent success rate. The report contributes a concrete architecture for cumulative executable search and a versioned account of its cross-modal development outcomes.

Confidence Comes from Experience: Experiential Confidence Estimation from Reasoning to Agents

Caiqi Zhang , Xiaochen Zhu , Chengzu Li , Yulong Chen , Dharshan Kumaran , Nigel Collier

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper argues that current confidence estimators for language models are insufficient because they only look at the current inference process. It proposes XConf, which estimates confidence by retrieving similar past episodes, reading off their historical success rates, and having the model reflect on its recurring failure modes. The method requires no logit access or weight updates and costs only one answer generation, yet it beats or matches ten-sample self-consistency on many benchmarks. For astronomy, this matters because LLM agents used for literature review, data analysis, or pipeline coding need calibrated confidence to decide when to escalate to a human. A concrete workflow is an agent that estimates its confidence in spectral-line identifications or photometric redshift estimates based on past performance on similar tasks. The caveat is that the method requires a store of graded past episodes, which may be expensive to build for specialized astronomical tasks, and the paper does not test on astronomical data.

Reason

This paper proposes experiential confidence estimation for LLMs and agents, which is highly relevant to uncertainty estimation, abstention, and reliable LLM agents for astronomy workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the method can be applied without logit access and with limited past episodes.
  • Look for details on how episodes are graded and stored in specialized domains.
  • Assess whether the approach could be integrated into an astronomy LLM agent for classification or retrieval.
  • Determine whether the benchmarks include multimodal or scientific tasks beyond general QA.
Abstract

Reliable confidence estimation is increasingly central to the trustworthy deployment of language models: a calibrated estimate of the probability that an output is correct decides what to ship, what to escalate, and what to retry. Existing confidence estimators, however, share one design premise: they only read the current inference process, either by introspecting on it, scoring its token probabilities, or resampling it. We argue that the current inference is not a sufficient basis for confidence. We propose XConf (eXperiential Confidence): estimating confidence together with the model's accumulated experience. The experience is stored as a record of the model's own graded past episodes, each holding the task, the model's reflection, its stated confidence, the outcome, and a lesson written once the grade arrived. Given a new task, XConf's Recall stage retrieves past episodes on similar tasks met with a similar stated confidence, and reads off their historical success rate; its Reflect stage shows the model this record, has it name its recurring failure mode, and restate a confidence now informed by its own track records. Our estimator is format-general, requiring no logit access or weight updates, and costs only one answer generation. Across nine benchmarks spanning reasoning, coding, multimodal QA, and interactive agents, and four models from three families, XConf beats or matches ten-sample self-consistency in discrimination (AUROC) on 23 of 24 comparisons, with much lower calibration error (ECE), at a tenth of the generation cost. Used for selective prediction, abstaining on the 10% least-confident episodes raises the delivered success rate by up to 8.7 points on agent tasks. We therefore see experiential confidence estimation as a new paradigm for future general-purpose confidence estimation.

AgenTeeth: A Model-Agnostic Framework for Suppressing Hallucination in Frozen Vision-Language Models on Dental X-Rays via Tool Evidence Injection

Ahmed Rafid , Fariya Ahmed , Rumman Adib , Mehedi Ahamed , Ajwad Abrar , Tareque Mohmud Chowdhury

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the unreliability of vision-language models on panoramic dental radiographs, where models can rely on learned anatomical priors rather than image evidence, especially for tooth localization and spatial reasoning. AgenTeeth is a model-agnostic, tool-augmented framework that grounds frozen VLMs using seven specialized dental vision experts, with a question-aware orchestrator selecting relevant tools whose detections are mapped to tooth numbers or anatomical regions and returned as structured findings plus annotated overlays. A fresh synthesis call then answers the question using this evidence without fine-tuning the underlying VLM, improving four backbone VLMs by 12.9-23.0 percentage points and reaching 65.66% on open-ended VQA. This matters for astronomy because it demonstrates a general recipe for grounding a frozen multimodal model with domain-specific expert tools and structured evidence, which could reduce hallucination in JWST image or spectrum interpretation. A concrete workflow would be an astronomy agent that calls source-detection, morphology, and SED-fitting tools, maps outputs to catalog identifiers, and synthesizes an answer with image overlays. The caveat is that the domain is dental radiology, so the orchestrator and tool interfaces would need redesigning for astronomical instruments and data formats.

Reason

Tool-augmented, model-agnostic grounding of frozen VLMs with structured evidence injection is directly relevant to multimodal astronomical agents and reducing hallucination in scientific image analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the orchestrator design and how it selects tools based on the question
  • Check how tool outputs are mapped to structured identifiers and returned as evidence
  • Examine the hallucination reduction evidence and whether it is measured beyond accuracy
  • Assess whether the framework is truly model-agnostic and could wrap an astronomical VLM with survey-specific tools
Abstract

Vision-language models (VLMs) remain largely unreliable on panoramic dental radiographs and can rely on learned anatomical priors rather than evidence in the image. This is particularly problematic for tooth localization and spatial reasoning, and fine-tuned dental VLMs can retain the same spatial biases. We present AgenTeeth, a model-agnostic, tool-augmented framework that grounds frozen VLMs using seven specialized dental vision experts. A question-aware orchestrator selects the relevant tools, whose detections are mapped to FDI tooth numbers or anatomical regions and returned as structured findings together with annotated image overlays. A fresh synthesis call then answers the question using this evidence, without fine-tuning the underlying VLM. On MMOral-OPG-Bench, AgenTeeth improves four backbone VLMs by 12.9-23.0 percentage points over their baselines. Our strongest configuration reaches 65.66% on open-ended VQA, compared with 45.35% for OralGPT-Plus. The advantage also holds at matched scale: a frozen Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct with AgenTeeth reaches 48.11%, exceeding OralGPT-Plus built on the same backbone after supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning for tool use. We release the framework, all seven expert models, and a dentist-annotated dataset for alveolar bone-loss detection in panoramic radiographs.

PrimeScientist: Strategic Allocation of Research Effort in Autonomous Research

Xinle Yu , Fan Bai , Kaiser Sun , Hengshuo Miao , Abhay Anand , Zhongyan Luo , Kun Zhou , Zhen Wang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem that autonomous research agents can propose more directions than they have resources to pursue, and each attempt may consume substantial resources. The authors introduce PrimeScientist, which formulates strategic research effort allocation as a sequential decision problem where remaining resources explicitly guide the research policy. They introduce an executable plan tree that preserves competing plans and outcomes across attempts, and an adaptive Monte Carlo tree search allocation policy that balances exploration and exploitation using experimental feedback and remaining resources. Evaluations across AI research, systems and code optimization, and machine learning engineering show that strategic allocation improves research quality and sample efficiency, with a 10.3 percent average reward improvement and 50.6 percent fewer research attempts than AutoResearch under the same budget. For astronomy, this could be tried on automated workflows that decide which follow-up observations, model fits, or literature searches to prioritize under limited telescope or compute time. The caveat is that the paper is evaluated on AI research tasks, not astronomical discovery, so the transfer requires adapting the plan tree and reward to astronomical objectives.

Reason

This paper is directly about autonomous research agents and strategic allocation of research effort, which is highly relevant to LLM agents for scientific discovery, literature review, and automated astronomy workflows. The plan-tree representation and MCTS-based allocation policy could inform how to build research agents that manage limited compute and time.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the executable plan tree representation is general enough to encode astronomical research plans
  • Look for the MCTS allocation policy details and whether it can incorporate domain-specific cost models
  • Assess whether the evaluation includes any scientific discovery tasks beyond AI research and code optimization
  • Check whether the method handles noisy or delayed feedback from experiments
Abstract

Autonomous research agents aim to automate scientific workflows, from proposing ideas to conducting experiments and analyzing results. Yet current AI and research agents can propose more directions than available resources allow them to pursue. Moreover, each attempt could consume substantial resources, requiring agents to reconsider how to invest in subsequent research. Thus, deciding how to invest research effort strategically should be a defining capability of autonomous research agents. Accordingly, we introduce PrimeScientist, which jointly determines research direction and resource investment across successive research attempts. Specifically, we formulate this challenge of strategic research effort allocation as a sequential decision problem where remaining resources should explicitly guide the research policy. We first introduce an executable plan tree that preserves competing plans and their outcomes across attempts. Building on this representation, we propose an adaptive MCTS-based allocation policy that balances exploration and exploitation using experimental feedback and remaining resources. Comprehensive evaluations across AI research, systems and code optimization, and machine learning engineering show that strategic allocation improves research quality and sample efficiency together. Across 12 AI research tasks, PrimeScientist improves average reward by 10.3% with 50.6% fewer research attempts than AutoResearch under the same resource budget. We believe making research effort allocation an explicit optimization target establishes effective resource use as a core research capability for autonomous agents to drive scientific breakthroughs at scale.

TabPFN-3.5: Technical Report

Benjamin Jäger , Nick Erickson , Léo Grinsztajn , Felix Birkel , Klemens Flöge , Oscar Key , Kürşat Kaya , Jonas Kübler , Adèle Frankel , Tobias Schröder , Anurag Garg , Jan Hendrik Metzen , David Salinas , Simon Bing , Kristina Collins , Tuana Çelik , Vahid Balazadeh , Lydia Sidhoum , Tomás Pereda , Brendan Roof , Andrej Tschalzev , Siyuan Guo , Philipp Singer , Lennart Purucker , Jake Robertson , Marie Salmon , Philipp Jund , Jerry Chen , Diana Kriuchkova , Arthur Cahu , Eliott Kalfon , Adrian Hayler , Georg Grab , Vitor Monteiro , Lilly Wehrhahn , Dominik Safaric , Clara Cornu , Alan Arazi , Rylee Grace , Simone Alessi , Mihir Manium , Bernhard Schölkopf , Yann LeCun , Madelon Hulsebos , Sauraj Gambhir , Noah Hollmann , Frank Hutter

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces TabPFN-3.5, a new foundation model for tabular prediction that aims to work well across many kinds of tables without task-specific training. The core idea is that a pretrained model can perform prediction on a new table by conditioning on the training rows, similar to in-context learning in LLMs but for rows and columns instead of text. It extends earlier TabPFN versions to harder practical settings such as temporal or grouped splits, string and text columns, high-cardinality categories, wide tables, and even tables containing images. For astronomy, this is directly relevant because much of our work is tabular: photometric catalogs, spectral feature tables, classification labels, redshifts, and multi-survey crossmatches. A concrete workflow would be to test TabPFN-3.5 on JWST or Rubin-like catalogs for photometric redshift regression, AGN versus galaxy classification, or anomaly detection, comparing it against gradient-boosted trees and neural networks. The main caveat is that foundation models for tables can be sensitive to feature encoding, missing data, and distribution shift, so you should check whether it handles astronomical selection effects and uncertainties properly.

Reason

Tabular foundation models are highly relevant to astronomical catalogs, photometric redshift estimation, source classification, and multimodal tables combining photometry, spectra, and metadata.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for benchmarks or examples involving scientific tabular data, missing values, or high-cardinality categorical features like source IDs or filters
  • Check whether the model supports uncertainty estimates or probabilistic predictions, which are important for photometric redshifts and classification
  • Check how it handles multimodal tables with images or text, since that could connect to joint image-catalog-spectrum workflows
  • Look for practical details on inference cost, memory, and whether it can scale to millions of astronomical sources
Abstract

We introduce TabPFN-3.5, our new flagship Tabular Foundation Model. It significantly outperforms its predecessor, TabPFN-3, and all existing baselines across a broad range of tabular problems. TabPFN-3.5 sets a new state of the art on standard tabular prediction in TabArena, and extends it to the data practitioners encounter in practice: non-i.i.d. data with temporal or grouped splits, tables with strings, text and images, high-cardinality categorical features, and wide tables with many features. These gains carry over to our task-specific harnesses: state of the art on relational data and stronger time-series forecasting. For faster inference, our variant TabPFN-3.5-Fast runs up to 3x faster than TabPFN-3 while keeping most of the accuracy gains. In addition, we upgrade TabPFN-3.5-Plus, expanding our multimodal capabilities with advanced text and date handling alongside proprietary inference optimizations. Finally, we release a new version of our Thinking mode, TabPFN-3.5-Thinking, which scales inference-time computation to push the state of the art further. It benefits from our stronger base model and from inference-time improvements that make it up to 12x faster than TabPFN-3-Thinking.

Locating Hidden Failures Makes Long-Horizon Agents More Reliable

Salman Rahman , Yubin Kim , Mihir Parmar , A. Ali Heydari , Genglin Liu , Simon A. Lee , Weizhi Zhang , Arian Hosseini , Ahmed A. Metwally , Yuzhe Yang , Baharan Mirzasoleiman , Xin Liu , Pavel Izmailov , Saadia Gabriel , Mark Malhotra , Shwetak Patel , Daniel McDuff , Hamid Palangi

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper studies how long-horizon AI agents fail when they perform autonomous tasks in software engineering, computer use, and science. It analyzes thousands of agent trajectories and classifies thousands of mistakes into many failure types, finding that agents often fail to recover after their first mistake and rarely catch errors themselves. The key contribution is a human-verified benchmark called Traverse, plus a small 4B verifier called Scout that can locate the first mistake much better than frontier judges and can be used at test time to select better agent runs. For astronomy, this is directly useful because research agents that reduce JWST data, fit SEDs, or write analysis code need to be monitored for hidden failures, not just final success. A concrete workflow would be to wrap an astronomy LLM agent with Scout-like verification, so that when it writes a spectral fitting script or queries a catalog, the system can detect where the run went wrong and correct it. The main caveat is that the benchmark domains are software engineering, computer use, and science, but not specifically astronomy, so you should check whether the failure taxonomy and verifier transfer to astronomical tools and data formats.

Reason

This paper is highly relevant to building reliable long-horizon LLM agents for scientific workflows, including astronomy research agents that perform multi-step data analysis, coding, and literature review.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the failure taxonomy includes categories relevant to data analysis, coding, and tool use in scientific pipelines
  • Look for details on how Scout is trained and whether it can be adapted to astronomy-specific agent traces
  • Check whether the verifier requires access to intermediate states or only final outputs
  • Look for evidence that test-time selection improves reliability without retraining the agent
Abstract

As AI agents take on long, autonomous tasks, we increasingly oversee rather than perform the work, yet we still judge them almost entirely by whether they finally succeed. An outcome cannot reveal where a run went wrong, whether the agent recovered, or the irreversible harm it caused along the way, and where long-horizon agents fail remains unmapped. We study $2518$ agent trajectories across software engineering, computer use, and science, close to real deployment, and classify $6967$ mistakes into $78$ failure types. Failure follows a recurring signature: after its first mistake an agent often fails to recover and rarely catches the error itself, so the run continues unchecked while still looking correct; whether an agent recovers depends on the task and the environment's feedback, not on the agent framework running it. Long-horizon agents can do real harm on the way to a passing result: even runs scored as solved delete data, corrupt systems, or fabricate success rather than earning it. We release these human-verified annotations as Traverse, a benchmark on which six frontier judges struggle to locate failure regardless of scale: even the strongest correctly identifies the first mistake in fewer than a third of runs. Yet Scout, a $4$B verifier we trained, locates failure far better than these judges and transfers to domains it never saw. Used at test time to select among an agent's candidate runs, it raises task success above the agent's own single-attempt performance, without retraining the agent. By making failure cheap to locate and correct, this work is a foundation for more trustworthy long-horizon agents that learn from their own mistakes, and a practical path to overseeing increasingly autonomous AI.

TuiML: Machine Learning for AI Agents

Nilesh Verma , Nick Lim , Albert Bifet , Bernhard Pfahringer

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that current machine-learning libraries like scikit-learn and Weka were built for humans, so LLM agents must recall APIs from memory and write code that often fails at runtime. TuiML is a self-contained ML library where every component exposes machine-readable metadata and parameter schemas, allowing an agent to search, inspect, compose, and validate workflows programmatically. It also validates and seeds every call, traces experiments, and exports sessions as runnable notebooks, making reproducibility automatic. For astronomy, this is directly useful because research agents could autonomously run classification, regression, clustering, or time-series analysis on JWST or survey data with less brittle code generation. A concrete workflow could be an agent that selects a photometric-redshift model, tunes it, evaluates it, and exports a reproducible notebook without a human writing the boilerplate. The main caveat is that TuiML is a general ML library, so domain-specific astronomical preprocessing, SED fitting, or spectral pipelines would still need to be integrated or wrapped.

Reason

TuiML is an ML library designed for AI agents with machine-readable metadata, validated workflows, and reproducible sessions, which is highly relevant to LLM agents for astronomical data analysis and automated scientific workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether TuiML supports the specific algorithms used in astronomical classification, regression, and time-series analysis
  • Look for the metadata and schema design and whether it is expressive enough for domain-specific preprocessing
  • Assess how the Model Context Protocol integration works and whether it can connect to external astronomical tools
  • Check the benchmark comparisons with scikit-learn and Weka for predictive performance and reproducibility guarantees
Abstract

Machine-learning libraries such as Weka and scikit-learn were designed for human programmers. Language-model agents now use these same libraries by recalling APIs from memory and writing code, an approach that hides what a library offers, delays errors until runtime, and loses experimental state between turns. We present TuiML, a self-contained machine-learning library built for AI agents, with native algorithms across supervised, unsupervised, time-series, data handling, tuning, and evaluation tasks. Every component describes itself through machine-readable metadata and parameter schemas, so an agent can search the library, inspect components, compose validated workflows, and register new ones that become discoverable in turn. Every call is validated, seeded, and traced, and sessions export as runnable notebooks, making experiments reproducible by construction. One specification layer drives the Model Context Protocol (MCP), agent-framework adapters, a Python API, a CLI, and local model serving, while data and models never leave the machine. Benchmarks show TuiML remains predictively competitive with scikit-learn and Weka. While looking like a conventional library to a human user, TuiML is designed for agents first, allowing them to read, extend, and operate machine learning autonomously. TuiML is open source, with documentation at this https URL .

PageRecall: Measuring Page Selection in Literature-Grounded Question Answering

Aaditya Chauhan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses literature-grounded question answering, where a system must retrieve relevant papers, cite the page and table or figure containing the answer, and answer in a requested format. Its main finding is that evidence grounding is limited by retrieval rather than reading: the page selector found the gold page only about half the time, while the evidence locator was highly accurate when given the right page, and failures were often silent. For astronomy, this is directly useful for building literature-review agents that must find and cite specific results, figures, or tables from papers. A concrete workflow could be an agent that answers questions about JWST high-redshift results by retrieving and citing the exact pages and figures. The main caveat is that the system depends on a closed model without seed control, so reproducibility is limited, though the authors release a verification harness. Check whether the whole-paper-in-context strategy scales to very long papers and whether the evidence metric is robust.

Reason

This studies page selection and evidence grounding in literature-grounded QA, directly relevant to LLM agents for literature review and scientific evidence retrieval.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the page selector and evidence locator are evaluated and whether the gold-page recall metric is well defined
  • Look for the failure-mode analysis and whether silent failures are quantified
  • Assess whether the whole-paper-in-context approach scales to long astronomy papers with many figures
  • Check the released harness and whether the central claims are verifiable
Abstract

We describe our system for LitTraceQA (GroundLM @ EMNLP 2026): given a research question, retrieve the relevant papers from a pool of 27,487, cite the page and the table or figure where the answer lives, and answer in a requested format. Our main finding is that evidence grounding is limited by retrieval, not by reading. The page selector put the annotator's page, which we call the gold page, in front of the model that locates evidence only about half the time (52.6% gold-page recall), while that model, given the page, cited the right one in 45 of the 48 locators it emitted (94%). When the page was missing it rarely said so: of 45 such cases it returned nothing 14 times, a wrong page 24 times, and a correct page 7 times, so the pipeline failed quietly almost twice as often as it failed visibly. Since the failure was that the right page was never shown, the fix is to stop choosing: each retrieved paper fits in the model's context, so we show it whole. Page ranking survives only as a fallback inside papers too long to fit, which no test-split paper was, and gold-page recall reaches 100% on the papers we can parse. Separately, questions that identify their target by position rather than content, such as "the first author of the 24th reference", are served by parsing rather than retrieval: we resolve the bibliography into an addressable list, which also supplies identifiers the evidence metric scores. The final system scores 0.762 paper $F_1$, 0.441 evidence $F_1$ and 0.920 multiple-choice accuracy on the held-out test split. Because the pipeline depends on a closed model without seed control, we release a harness that verifies the paper's central claims against committed artifacts.
