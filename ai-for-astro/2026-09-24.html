Large Knowledge Model: From Papers to a Scientific Reasoning Landscape

Yuan Huang , Sihan Hu , Hongyu Gu , Chao Ma , Jiaxing Zhang , Zhiyong Zou , Caiyu Fan , Yan Xiao , Mingjun Xu , Chenyu Xie , Mingzhen Ju , Zhehao Ma , Qi Zhang , Baozong Wang , Yu Li , Zhiyuan Yao , Ruoxue Liao , Xinyu Li , Linfeng Zhang , Kun Chen , Weinan E

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that scientific knowledge is growing faster than researchers can connect problems, methods, conclusions, and evidence across papers. It introduces the Large Knowledge Model, which represents papers as source-grounded reasoning graphs and couples structural traversal with semantic retrieval over the same objects, aligning related questions, claims, and reasoning chains across papers. This forms a Scientific Reasoning Landscape with three views: a Question Landscape for research problems, a Workflow Landscape for reusable procedures, and an Evidence Landscape connecting conclusions to support, disagreement, and conditions. For astronomy, this is directly applicable to building a literature agent that can retrieve work by scientific intent, synthesize answers with inspectable supporting arguments, and plan research using established workflows and unresolved evidence. A concrete workflow would be an agent that, given a question about little red dots or high-redshift AGN, retrieves relevant papers, compares their claims, and surfaces disagreements or missing evidence. The caveat is that the evaluation is on chemistry, biomedicine, and science benchmarks, so I would check whether the graph schema and retrieval can handle astronomy-specific entities, figures, and multi-wavelength data.

This paper builds a scientific knowledge infrastructure that turns literature into reasoning graphs linking questions, workflows, and evidence, with retrieval and question answering evaluated on scientific benchmarks. It is highly relevant to LLM agents for literature review, evidence-grounded question answering, and automated scientific discovery in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the reasoning graph schema and whether it can represent astronomical entities, observations, and physical quantities
  • Look for the retrieval evaluation setup and whether it can be reproduced on an astrophysics corpus
  • Check how evidence, disagreement, and conditions are extracted and whether figures or tables are included
  • Look for the research planning capability and whether it produces actionable, inspectable plans rather than generic summaries
Abstract

Accumulated scientific knowledge advances inquiry when prior findings help researchers choose new questions, design investigations, and interpret results. Realizing this value at scale requires access to the reasoning that connects research problems, scientific procedures, conclusions, and evidence. We introduce the Large Knowledge Model (LKM), a scientific knowledge infrastructure that transforms the literature into a shared, computationally accessible reasoning resource. LKM represents papers as source-grounded reasoning graphs, couples structural traversal with semantic retrieval over the same objects, and aligns related questions, claims, and reasoning chains across papers. This representation forms a Scientific Reasoning Landscape with three connected views: a Question Landscape that organizes research problems and open directions, a Workflow Landscape that exposes reusable scientific procedures, and an Evidence Landscape that connects conclusions to their support, disagreement, and conditions. The unified substrate supports reasoning-aware scientific search, evidence-grounded question answering, comparative evidence analysis, and research planning. Researchers and agents can retrieve relevant work through its scientific intent, synthesize answers with inspectable supporting arguments, and develop research plans informed by established workflows and unresolved evidence. We describe a corpus-scale system and evaluate scientific retrieval and knowledge-intensive question answering. With the answering model fixed, LKM retrieval improves accuracy by 9.30%, 4.20%, and 14.69% on ChemBench, PubMedQA, and SciBench, respectively. By connecting knowledge access to scientific reasoning and action, LKM provides a common foundation for discovering relevant research, reusing scientific knowledge, and coordinating cumulative inquiry across researchers, agents, and research cycles.

Just-in-Time Memory: Learning to Curate Task-Adaptive Memory for LLM Agents

Yefan Zhou , Yang Li , Zeyu Leo Liu , Semih Yavuz , Shafiq Joty

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles a core problem in agentic memory: most systems decide what to remember at write time, distilling a trajectory into a fixed artifact before the future query is known, which irreversibly discards information. Its main idea, Just-in-Time Memory (JitMem), keeps raw trajectories and defers curation to read time, when the current task is known, so a memory curator can synthesize a compact payload tailored to the immediate need. Because that payload is consumed on the same task, the curator can be trained directly from immediate task success, avoiding long-horizon credit assignment. JitMem outperforms no-memory and write-time memory baselines on ALFWorld, WebShop, and tau-squared-bench, and even an untrained curator is competitive, showing that read-time curation itself drives much of the gain. For astronomy, this is relevant to research agents that accumulate experience across many data-analysis sessions, such as repeated SED fitting, spectral line searches, or catalog cross-matching, where the right past experience depends on the current target. A concrete workflow would be to store full analysis traces and let a curator assemble task-specific context when a new JWST target or question arrives. The caveat is that the benchmarks are text-based interactive environments, so it is untested whether read-time curation handles numerical, image, or spectral evidence well.

Reason

This paper is directly about LLM agent memory curation, proposing read-time task-adaptive memory instead of write-time summarization. It is highly relevant to research agents and automated scientific workflows that must reuse past analysis experience.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how the memory curator is trained and whether it requires task-specific fine-tuning or works zero-shot
  • Look for the comparison between untrained and trained curators to gauge how much training actually adds
  • Check whether the method handles multimodal or numerical traces, or only text-based trajectories
  • Assess whether the read-time curation cost is acceptable for long scientific workflows
Abstract

Agentic memory systems reuse past experience to improve future performance, yet most existing designs curate memory at write time: once a task is completed, its trajectory is distilled into a fixed artifact, such as a reflection, workflow, skill, or reasoning strategy, that is later retrieved by similarity. This forces the system to decide what is worth remembering before the future query is known, irreversibly discarding information and producing a query-independent summary that must serve many possible downstream tasks. Learning such a write-time curator is also difficult because the value of a storage decision may only become apparent when a relevant query arrives, potentially many tasks later, creating a long-horizon credit-assignment problem. We instead retain raw trajectories and defer curation until read time, when the current task is known. Given the retrieved traces and the new task, a memory curator synthesizes a compact, task-adaptive payload tailored to the immediate need. Because this payload is consumed on the same task, the curator can be trained directly from immediate task success, avoiding delayed utility signals and the need to artificially group related tasks. Across ALFWorld, WebShop, and $\tau^2$-bench, our Just-in-Time Memory (JitMem) consistently outperforms no-memory agents as well as heuristic and learned write-time memory methods, improving over the strongest baseline by 16.2, 16.3, and 3.9 absolute success-rate points, respectively. Notably, even an untrained curator is already competitive with or surpasses these baselines, showing that task-adaptive read-time curation itself is a major source of the gain; training the curator further compounds the improvement.

WhatWorkedBench: Benchmarking Experimental Understanding in AI Agents

Jingjie Ning , Xueqi Li , Yibo Kong , Dongting Li

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether AI research agents can reliably predict how changes to experimental components affect outcomes, and introduces WhatWorkedBench to measure this experimental understanding. Agents inspect code, select measurements, and submit a response surface, a table predicting scores for every configuration of component settings, with exhaustive CPU execution providing reference effects for changing each component while holding others fixed. The benchmark covers 36 tasks from 30 data sources and 8 workflow types with 1248 configuration records, and core evaluation combines 4206 numerical-control records with 108 agent episodes. The main findings are that pair-effect ridge selects an optimum on 15 of 22 sources at eight new measurements, and that fitting a Gaussian process to the same agent observations raises effect recovery from 0.632 to 0.698 in one cohort and from 0.621 to 0.720 in another, with encoding code equivalences further improving recovery. For astronomy, this is directly relevant to building LLM agents that can run and interpret experiments, such as tuning photometric redshift pipelines, SED-fitting configurations, or anomaly-detection hyperparameters, and to adaptive experimental design for survey analysis. A concrete workflow would be to use the benchmark's response-surface formulation to evaluate an agent's ability to predict how changes in a JWST reduction pipeline affect output metrics. The main caveat is that the benchmark is CPU-based and generic, so its tasks may not capture the full complexity of astronomical data reduction or instrument-specific workflows.

Reason

This paper directly targets AI research agents and experimental understanding, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and LLM agents for scientific workflows. The benchmark measures whether agents can predict how component changes affect outcomes, a core capability for autonomous experimentation in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the exact task families and whether any involve scientific data analysis or pipeline configuration
  • Check how the response-surface and effect-recovery metrics are defined and whether they could be adapted to astronomical pipelines
  • Look for details on the Gaussian process fitting and code-equivalence encoding that drive the reported gains
  • Assess whether the benchmark code and tasks are released for extension to new domains
Abstract

AI research agents need reliable knowledge of how their experiments change outcomes. We introduce WhatWorkedBench to measure experimental understanding, the accuracy of predictions about component changes after budgeted experimentation. Agents inspect code, select measurements, and submit a response surface, a table predicting scores for every configuration of component settings. Exhaustive CPU execution supplies reference effects for changing each component while holding the others fixed. These effects capture combinations of changes across 36 tasks from 30 data sources and 8 workflow types, with 1248 configuration records. Core evaluation combines 4,206 numerical-control records across all eight families and 108 agent episodes across the original six. At eight new measurements, pair-effect ridge selects an optimum on 15 of 22 sources and limits every effect error to 10% of score range on three. Fitting a Gaussian process (GP) to the same agent observations raises effect recovery, accuracy relative to true effect magnitude, from 0.632 to 0.698 in the original Flash cohort and from 0.621 to 0.720 in an additional cohort. On six completed beat-detection and graph submissions, the same-observation GP raises family-macro recovery from 0.303 to 0.455. On six workflows with six binary options at 20 new measurements, encoding code equivalences, configurations with identical behavior, raises GP recovery from 0.248 to 0.462. WhatWorkedBench supports research on experimental agents, adaptive experimental design, numerical inference, and use of program structure.

False-science induction in autonomous scientific discovery

Hanbing Liang , Fujun Liu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses a subtle failure mode in closed-loop autonomous discovery, where legitimate physical objects and measurements are paired incorrectly, causing neural surrogates to learn record-induced associations that do not reflect the true object-outcome relationship while marginal data distributions remain unchanged. It calls this false-science induction and shows across green fluorescent protein fitness and materials band-gap prediction loops that coherent paired misbinding systematically redirects experimental budgets toward low-performing basins, whereas same-volume random swaps have negligible effects. The key finding is that error coherence, not raw error frequency, controls budget misallocation, and the resulting binding identifiability boundary supports monitored-axis quarantines and feedback-conflict triage. For astronomy, this is directly relevant to AI-driven discovery loops that pair observations with labels, spectra with redshifts, or images with catalog entries, where a systematic misbinding could silently corrupt a neural surrogate and waste telescope time. A concrete workflow would be a closed-loop system that selects high-redshift galaxy candidates for follow-up spectroscopy, where a misbinding between photometric measurements and object identities could redirect observations toward a low-value region. The caveat is that the domains are biology and materials, so the specific diagnostics would need to be re-derived for astronomical data streams, but the conceptual warning is strong and transferable.

Reason

This paper is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery, showing how record-integrity failures can mislead closed-loop discovery systems, which directly applies to AI-driven astronomy pipelines and neural surrogates for physical measurements.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the misbinding mechanism is formalized in a way that maps onto astronomical data association problems
  • Look for the monitored-axis quarantine and feedback-conflict triage procedures and whether they are implementable
  • Assess whether the experiments include any surrogate model architecture resembling astronomical SED or image models
  • Check whether the paper provides diagnostics for detecting coherent misbinding before budget is wasted
Abstract

Closed-loop discovery systems increasingly execute experiments and update decisions autonomously, turning record integrity into part of the experimental apparatus. We show that false-science induction arises when legitimate physical objects and measurements are paired incorrectly, driving neural surrogates to faithfully learn record-induced associations that do not correspond to the true object-outcome relationship while marginal data distributions remain unchanged. Across green fluorescent protein fitness and materials band-gap prediction loops, coherent paired misbinding systematically redirects experimental budgets toward low-performing basins, whereas same-volume random swaps have negligible effects. These observations identify error coherence, rather than raw error frequency, as the primary variable controlling this budget misallocation in the tested loops. The resulting binding identifiability boundary supports monitored-axis quarantines and feedback-conflict triage, which intercept over-concentrated proposals before execution and isolate the corrupted hypothesis axis.

Dual-Hypergraph Indexing: Bridging Knowledge Islands for Multi-Hop Reasoning in Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Qi Sun , Xingliang Hou , Caibo Li , Yijia Zhang , Qiang Li , Yu Guo

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses a weakness in hypergraph-based retrieval-augmented generation, where extracted facts are treated as isolated assertions and form knowledge islands that block multi-hop reasoning. It introduces Dual-Hypergraph Indexing, which couples a factual entity-relation hypergraph with a higher-level deep-insight hypergraph using importance-driven hub aggregation and temporal chunk-chain aggregation. The main claim is state-of-the-art performance on several benchmarks, including improved logical coherence and strong results on complex medical pathology reasoning. For astronomy, this could support literature-review agents that connect results across papers, instruments, and wavelengths, or that trace how a claim about a high-redshift galaxy evolved over time. A concrete workflow would be indexing an astronomy paper corpus into factual and insight hypergraphs so an LLM agent can answer multi-hop questions like which JWST detections support or contradict a given SED interpretation. The caveat is that the benchmarks are not astronomical, and building reliable hypergraphs from messy scientific text and tables is nontrivial.

Reason

This paper improves retrieval-augmented generation for multi-hop reasoning by building dual hypergraphs that connect fragmented facts. This is directly relevant to LLM agents for literature review, scientific knowledge synthesis, and connecting multi-wavelength or multi-paper evidence in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check how hyperedges are extracted from source documents and whether the pipeline handles scientific notation and tables
  • Look for ablations showing the contribution of the deep-insight hypergraph versus the factual one
  • Assess whether temporal aggregation could track evolving claims in astronomy literature
  • See whether the method reports retrieval precision and not only downstream QA accuracy
Abstract

While hypergraph-based Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) effectively captures higher-order multi-entity correlations, existing paradigms treat extracted hyperedges as isolated factual assertions. This structural fragmentation engenders rigid "knowledge islands" that bottleneck multi-hop causal inference, temporal tracking, and narrative synthesis. To systematically address these challenges, we introduce Dual-Hypergraph Indexing (DHI), a hierarchical representation framework that elevates discrete facts into structured analytical insights. DHI couples a foundational entity-relation factual hypergraph ($H_K$) with an elevated deep-insight hypergraph ($H_D$) via a dual-pathway aggregation algorithm. Specifically, DHI employs: (1) importance-driven hub aggregation via 5-metric topological profiling and adaptive thresholding to capture spatial semantic clusters; and (2) temporal chunk-chain progressive aggregation via sliding-window greedy exploration to track chronological evolutions. Across five benchmarks, DHI achieves state-of-the-art performance, boosting logical coherence by +1.53 on the multidisciplinary Mix benchmark and scoring 85.78\% on complex medical pathology reasoning tasks. DHI provides a robust architecture for next-generation multi-hop RAG.
