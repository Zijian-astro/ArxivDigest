Agentic Publication Protocol: An Attempt to Modernize Scientific Publication

Sirui Lu , Xiao-Liang Qi

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks how to modernize scientific publication to include not just static manuscripts but also operational know-how (code, data, reproducibility instructions) that can be used by LLM agents. It proposes the Agentic Publication Protocol (APP), a lightweight repository format that packages a paper with code, data, environment info, and an agent-facing instruction file. The protocol allows an agent to explain the work, reproduce results, and support follow-up research. For astronomy, this is extremely relevant because it could enable fully reproducible workflows for JWST data analysis, where an agent could re-run SED fitting or spectral extraction from a published paper. A concrete workflow could be using APP to publish a paper on high-redshift galaxy classification, allowing other researchers to run the same analysis on new data. A key caveat is that the protocol is new and may require community adoption and tooling support.

Reason

Directly proposes an agentic publication protocol for packaging papers with code, data, and agent instructions, highly relevant for reproducible research and scientific workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the detailed specification of the agent-facing instruction file and how it handles environment setup.
  • Look for example repositories or case studies that demonstrate the protocol in action.
  • Examine the discussion of agent skills and how they are evaluated.
Abstract

Scientific publication is still organized primarily around static manuscripts, even though much of scientific progress depends on tacit know-how: how to run code, reproduce figures, interpret edge cases, choose useful follow-up directions, and avoid failed paths. Large language model agents create an opportunity to publish not only knowledge, but also operational know-how in a form that future readers and researchers can directly use. This paper outlines the Agentic Publication Protocol (APP), a lightweight repository format for packaging a paper together with code, data, environment information, reproducibility instructions, and an agent-facing instruction file. APP treats a version-controlled repository as the publication object and uses \texttt{ this http URL } and optional skills to define a paper agent that can explain the work, reproduce key results when possible, and support follow-up research. We describe the design principles and details of the protocol, as well as the agent skills useful for publishing papers under the protocol. We also describe development tools for evaluating and improving the protocol and associated agent skills. Finally, we provide a broader discussion of the future of scientific research in the agent era.

CalBrief: A Pilot Diagnostic Benchmark for Evidence-Calibrated Scientific Briefing with Large Language Models

Yu Fu , Yongqi Kang , Yong Zhao

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper asks whether LLMs can calibrate research takeaways to the strength and scope of supporting evidence, a task called evidence-calibrated scientific briefing. It introduces CalBrief, a framework for generating package-level takeaways with evidence strength, scope boundaries, and missing-evidence caveats. The main finding is that structured organization improves role and gap reasoning, but an explicit strength-calibration policy is systematically over-conservative. For astronomy, this is highly relevant because it could help automate literature review and synthesis, e.g., summarizing findings from multiple papers on high-redshift galaxies. A concrete workflow could be using CalBrief to generate a briefing on JWST observations of little red dots, with calibrated confidence statements. A key caveat is that the benchmark uses only 16 evidence packages, and the over-conservatism issue may require tuning for astronomy-specific literature.

Reason

Directly addresses evidence-calibrated scientific briefing with LLMs, relevant for literature review and research agent workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the specific evaluation schema and how 'evidence strength' is defined and annotated.
  • Check whether the paper provides examples of generated takeaways and how they compare to human-written ones.
  • Examine the diagnostic analysis that separates conservatism into label space expansion vs. pipeline policy.
Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as research assistants, yet it remains unclear whether they can calibrate research takeaways to the strength and scope of the supporting evidence. We study evidence-calibrated scientific briefing: given a bounded package of related papers, a system should generate package-level takeaways with evidence strength, scope boundaries, and missing-evidence caveats. We contribute a verified pilot benchmark of 16 heterogeneous scientific evidence packages and 96 human-verified takeaways, and we use CalBrief, an auditable role/gap/strength framework, as a diagnostic probe to locate where briefing breaks down. Under a fair-schema evaluation, structured organization improves role and gap reasoning, but an explicit strength-calibration policy is systematically over-conservative and falls below majority and direct-LLM baselines. To explain why, we run a controlled diagnostic across three closed-model backbones (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet, Gemini Flash) that separates three potential causes of conservatism. Approximately 63% of the conservatism gap is attributable to expanding the label space from binary {moderate, weak} to four-way {moderate, weak, uncertain, insufficient_evidence} (p < 0.001 across all backbones); only 1% is attributable to gap/scope signal injection (not significant); the remaining 36% arises from the pipeline policy itself. We also find that 4-way predictions can be post-hoc collapsed back to binary and then match or exceed direct binary prompting, so the extra labels carry information that strict matching hides. Label-level strength judgment and auditable evidence organization are distinct abilities currently in tension, and should be evaluated separately for LLM research assistants.

When Search Agents Should Ask: DiscoBench for Clarification-Aware Deep Search

Yiling Tao , Shihan Deng , Meiling Tao , Pengzhi Wei , Zhichao Hu , Zhihao Zhu

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper addresses the problem that LLM-based search agents often fail when user queries are vague, underspecified, or factually incorrect, because ambiguity propagates along multi-step reasoning chains. It introduces DiscoBench, a benchmark for evaluating whether search agents can proactively identify ambiguity, ask effective clarification questions, and recover correct reasoning paths through user interaction. The benchmark covers 211 samples across 11 domains with four ambiguity types. For astronomy, this is directly relevant for building research agents that assist astronomers with literature review, data analysis, or experimental design. A concrete workflow would be to use a clarification-aware agent to help an astronomer refine a vague query like 'find papers on high-redshift galaxies' into specific criteria (e.g., redshift range, JWST data, AGN content). A key limitation is that the benchmark is domain-general and may not cover astronomy-specific ambiguities (e.g., instrument names, coordinate systems). The paper does not provide a ready-to-use agent, only an evaluation framework.

Reason

The paper directly addresses a key limitation of LLM-based search agents: handling ambiguous or underspecified queries. This is highly relevant for building research agents for astronomy that can interact with users to clarify scientific questions.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the ambiguity types covered (e.g., missing constraints, conflicting information) and whether they map to astronomy use cases.
  • Look for the user simulator design and whether it can be adapted to astronomy-specific interactions.
  • Examine the evaluation metrics (task utility, ambiguity detection, interaction strategy, cost efficiency) and whether they are relevant for scientific research agents.
  • Check if the benchmark includes any scientific or technical domains that could inform astronomy adaptation.
Abstract

Search agents powered by large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to solve complex information-seeking tasks, requiring multi-step retrieval and reasoning to fulfill user goals. However, existing benchmarks often assume that user queries are complete and explicit, overlooking the fact that real-world search requests are frequently vague, underspecified, or even factually incorrect. In deep search scenarios, such ambiguity can propagate along multi-step reasoning chains and lead agents toward incorrect search trajectories. To address this gap, we introduce DiscoBench, a benchmark for clarification-aware deep search, designed to evaluate whether search agents can proactively identify ambiguity, ask effective clarification questions, and recover correct reasoning paths through user interaction. DiscoBench contains 211 samples and 463 ambiguity instances across 11 real-world domains, covering four ambiguity types. We further design a user simulator for multi-turn interaction and evaluate model performance from four perspectives: task utility, ambiguity detection, interaction strategy, and cost efficiency. Experiments on representative LLMs show that ambiguity detection and effective clarification are distinct capabilities, and that repeatedly searching instead of asking for clarification often performs worse than direct guessing, highlighting a critical gap between retrieval ability and interactive problem-solving in current search agents.

The Weakest Link Tells It All: Outcome-Supervised Process Reward Modeling via Learnable Credit Assignment

Tianyu Jia , Yue Fang , Hongxin Ding , Rihong Qiu , Zhibang Yang , Zhijing Wu , Xu Chu , Junfeng Zhao , Yasha Wang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of training process reward models (PRMs) for LLM reasoning without expensive stepwise annotations. It proposes LCA, an outcome-supervised PRM framework that uses a learnable credit assignment mechanism based on the 'weakest link' principle, formalized as a Multiple Instance Learning problem. This could enable building research agents that can identify which reasoning steps are incorrect when solving complex astronomical problems, such as interpreting spectra or planning observations. One could imagine using LCA to train an agent that checks its own logic when fitting SEDs or classifying sources. A caveat is that the method is evaluated on math reasoning tasks and may need adaptation for scientific reasoning with domain-specific knowledge.

Reason

Addresses process reward modeling for LLM reasoning, which is directly relevant for building research agents that can verify their own reasoning steps in scientific workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for experiments on multi-step reasoning tasks with long chains, similar to scientific workflows.
  • Check whether the credit assignment can handle heterogeneous reasoning steps (e.g., mixing numerical calculations with logical deductions).
  • Examine the Bayes consistency proof: does it rely on assumptions that hold for scientific reasoning?
Abstract

Process reward models (PRMs) enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by providing fine-grained feedback, yet training PRMs typically requires expensive stepwise annotations. Outcome-supervised PRMs offer a scalable alternative by learning from final-answer correctness alone, but this introduces a fundamental *credit assignment* challenge, i.e., attributing outcomes to responsible reasoning steps. Existing approaches rely on either uniform or causal assignment, both of which fail to anchor credit in step correctness and thus hinder process error identification. In this work, we propose Outcome-Supervised Process Reward Modeling via **L**earnable **C**redit **A**ssignment (**LCA**), an outcome-supervised PRM framework that jointly learns credit assignment and reward modeling under the principle of *Weakest Link Assignment: a reasoning chain is as strong as its weakest link*. To address mutual dependence between credit assignment and reward modeling, we formalize outcome-supervised PRM as a Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) problem and introduce Softmax-Weighted-Sum (SWS) pooling, an MIL pooling technique tailored for strong dependence and redundancy among reasoning states. We prove Bayes consistency of our algorithm under mild assumptions. Extensive experiments demonstrate that **LCA** consistently outperforms state-of-the-art outcome-supervised PRMs across multiple tasks and backbones. Code is available at this https URL .

SHIFT: Gate-Modulated Activation Steering for Knowledge Conflict Mitigation in Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Ruochang Li , Pengcheng Huang , Zhenghao Liu , Yukun Yan , Huiyuan Xie , Yu Gu , Ge Yu , Maosong Sun

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper tackles the problem of conflicts between retrieved context and parametric knowledge in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. It introduces SHIFT, a framework that reformulates neuron-level modification as learnable gate modulation, using fewer than 0.01% trainable parameters to adaptively regulate internal activations. The main result is effective mitigation of knowledge conflicts across six datasets while preserving general model capabilities. This matters for astronomy because research agents that retrieve papers or data from archives (e.g., ADS, MAST) and combine them with pre-trained knowledge could benefit from this conflict resolution. A concrete workflow could be an agent that retrieves JWST papers and synthesizes findings with its own knowledge about galaxy formation. A limitation is that the method is evaluated on general-domain QA datasets, not on scientific or astronomical data.

Reason

Directly addresses knowledge conflict in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which is highly relevant for building research agents that combine retrieved astronomical literature with parametric knowledge.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the gate modulation approach is domain-agnostic or requires task-specific tuning.
  • Look for any discussion of scaling to long-context retrieval scenarios common in scientific literature review.
  • Check whether the method handles conflicting numerical or spectral data, not just textual facts.
Abstract

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) enhances LLMs by incorporating external knowledge to support response generation. However, conflicts between retrieved context and parametric knowledge have emerged as a critical challenge in RAG systems. To mitigate such conflicts, numerous studies have attempted to identify and edit knowledge-related internal neurons, aiming to improve the ability of LLMs to rely on contextual evidence during generation. However, these neuron-level approaches may introduce unintended cascading effects that compromise the general capabilities of LLMs, as the modified neurons are often entangled with broader model behaviors and functionalities. In this paper, we introduce SHIFT, a novel framework that reformulates neuron-level modification as learnable gate modulation, allowing LLMs to adaptively regulate internal activations for knowledge conflict resolution. Technically, our SHIFT equips LLMs with a lightweight gate module and optimizes fewer than 0.01% trainable parameters while keeping the backbone model frozen. During generation, the gate module adjusts the model's internal representations to adaptively leverage contextual and parametric knowledge. Extensive experiments on six datasets validate the effectiveness of our SHIFT in comparison with various competing baselines. All datasets and code are available at this https URL .
