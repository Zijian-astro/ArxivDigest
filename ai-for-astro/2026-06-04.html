Can Generalist Agents Automate Data Curation?

Feiyang Kang , Hanze Li , Adam Nguyen , Mahavir Dabas , Jiaqi W. Ma , Frederic Sala , Dawn Song , Ruoxi Jia

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether generalist coding agents can automate the data-curation loop, introducing Curation-Bench for vision-language instruction-tuning. It finds that out-of-the-box agents reach strong baselines, but scaffolded agents that cite and adapt prior methods outperform them, composing a policy that beats published baselines at one-tenth the data budget. This is highly relevant for astronomy, where data curation is a major bottleneck. For example, an agent could curate training data for galaxy classification or spectral fitting models. A caveat is that the benchmark uses vision-language data, not astronomical data, so domain-specific adaptation is needed.

Reason

Directly relevant to automating data curation with LLM agents, which could be applied to curating astronomical datasets for training or analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for details on the agent's command-line access and whether it can be extended to astronomical data formats.
  • Check if the scaffolded method adaptation approach is domain-agnostic and can be applied to scientific data curation.
Abstract

Curating training data is among the most consequential yet labor-intensive parts of modern AI development: practitioners iteratively propose, implement, evaluate, and revise data policies against noisy benchmark feedback. We ask whether generalist coding agents can automate this data-curation loop. We introduce *Curation-Bench*, an agent-centric benchmark that fixes the model, training recipe, and evaluation suite while giving agents command-line access to inspect data, implement policies, submit them to a fixed training/evaluation pipeline, and revise. In a vision-language instruction-tuning instantiation, out-of-the-box agents reach strong published data-selection baselines within ten iterations. However, trajectory analysis reveals a persistent *execution-research gap*: agents mainly tune local policy variants rather than explore new policy families, even when given strategy guides and paper references. Scaffolds requiring each iteration to cite, instantiate, and adapt a prior method shift agents toward method-guided exploration. The scaffolded agent autonomously composes -- without human design input -- a data-selection policy that outperforms strong published baselines at one-tenth their data budget. Overall, current agents can run the curation loop, but reliable data research requires scaffolded method adaptation, not open-ended prompting alone. Code and benchmark are open-sourced.

The Meta-Agent Challenge: Are Current Agents Capable of Autonomous Agent Development?

Xinyu Lu , Tianshu Wang , Pengbo Wang , zujie wen , Zhiqiang Zhang , Jun Zhou , Boxi Cao , Yaojie Lu , Hongyu Lin , Xianpei Han , Le Sun

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces the Meta-Agent Challenge (MAC), a benchmark to test whether AI models can autonomously develop agent systems. It provides a sandboxed environment where a meta-agent must iteratively program an agent artifact to maximize performance on held-out tasks. This is highly relevant to astronomy because it evaluates the very capability needed for automated scientific discovery: an AI that can design and improve its own analysis tools. A concrete workflow could be a meta-agent that designs a pipeline for classifying JWST sources, then iteratively improves it. The main caveat is that current meta-agents rarely match human-designed baselines, and the benchmark reveals emergent adversarial behaviors like reward hacking.

Reason

Directly evaluates the capability of AI agents to autonomously develop other agents, which is highly relevant to automated scientific discovery and research agents.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check the five domains used in the benchmark and whether any are scientific or data-analysis oriented.
  • Look for analysis of the meta-agent's design process, including variance and emergent behaviors.
Abstract

Current AI benchmarks evaluate agents on task execution within human-designed workflows. These evaluations fundamentally fail to measure a critical next-level capability: whether models can autonomously develop agent systems. We introduce the Meta-Agent Challenge (MAC), an evaluation framework designed to test the capacity of frontier models for autonomous agent development. Specifically, a code agent (the meta-agent) is given a sandboxed environment, an evaluation API, and a time limitation to iteratively program an agent artifact that maximizes performance on a held-out test set across five domains. To ensure evaluation integrity, this framework is secured by multi-layer defenses against reward hacking. Leveraging this framework, we demonstrate that meta-agents rarely match human-engineered baseline policies, and the few that do are dominated by proprietary frontier models. Moreover, the design process exhibits high variance, and high optimization pressure surfaces emergent adversarial behaviors like ground-truth exfiltration-highlighting critical deficits in both robustness and model alignment. Ultimately, MAC provides a rigorous, open-source benchmark for autonomous AI research and development, offering an empirical proxy for evaluating recursive self-improvement. Simulate, Reason, Decide: Scientific Reasoning with LLMs for Simulation-Driven Decision Making

Yuhan Yang , Ruipu Li , Alexander Rodríguez

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

This paper introduces MechSim, a framework that enables LLM agents to reason about the mechanisms, assumptions, and execution behavior of scientific simulators. Unlike prior work that treats simulators as black boxes, MechSim represents simulators through a structured schema capturing variables, dependencies, and execution traces, allowing LLMs to generate evidence-grounded explanations linking outcomes to underlying mechanisms. For astronomy, this could enable automated reasoning about simulation codes like radiative transfer models (e.g., CLOUDY, SKIRT), N-body simulations (e.g., GADGET, AREPO), or SED fitting tools (e.g., CIGALE, BAGPIPES). The framework could help researchers understand why a simulation produces certain results and guide decisions about model parameters. However, the paper evaluates on high-stakes domains like climate and epidemiology, and adapting to astronomical simulators would require building the structured schema for each tool.

Reason

The paper directly addresses using LLMs for simulation-driven decision-making with scientific simulators, which is highly relevant for automated scientific discovery in astronomy.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check whether the schema representation can capture the complex parameter spaces and dependencies of astronomical simulators
  • Look for details on how the LLM agent generates and validates mechanistic explanations
  • Evaluate whether the framework supports interactive refinement of simulator assumptions based on observational data
  • Check if the system can handle simulators with stochastic outputs or multiple physical processes
Abstract

Scientific simulators are increasingly being integrated into LLM-driven systems for high-stakes simulation-driven decision-making. However, existing frameworks primarily use LLMs to generate, calibrate, or execute simulators, treating them as black-box interfaces rather than as structured mechanistic systems that can be reasoned about. As a result, current approaches lack the ability to identify, represent, and reason about the assumptions and mechanisms underlying simulator behavior, limiting transparency, auditability, and decision justification. We introduce MechSim, a mechanism-grounded neuro-symbolic reasoning framework for executable scientific simulators. Unlike prior neuro-symbolic approaches that primarily reason over static symbolic structures, MechSim enables LLM agents to reason about the mechanisms, assumptions, and execution behavior of scientific simulators. Our framework represents simulators through a shared structured schema capturing assumptions, variables, mechanism dependencies, and execution traces. On top of this representation, LLM agents operate as constrained reasoning engines that generate structured, evidence-grounded explanations linking simulator outcomes to their underlying mechanisms. We evaluate our approach across multiple high-stakes domains and show that it improves mechanism-level explanation quality, simulator analysis, and downstream decision-making reliability.

Overview of the EReL@MIR 2025 Multimodal Document Retrieval Challenge (Track 1)

Jingbiao Mei

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper describes a multimodal document retrieval challenge with two tasks: closed-set document page retrieval within long documents and open-domain retrieval of Wikipedia-style passages from image or image-plus-text queries. Winning systems use decoder-based Multimodal-LLM embedders from the Qwen2-VL family. This is highly relevant for astronomy, where researchers often need to retrieve papers or data based on images (e.g., galaxy images) and text queries. The training-free system achieving near-winner performance suggests practical applicability. A caveat is that the challenge uses general document datasets, not astronomical ones, so domain adaptation may be needed.

Reason

Directly relevant to multimodal document retrieval, which could be applied to astronomical literature and data retrieval, especially for papers with figures and tables.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for details on the datasets used and whether they include scientific or technical documents.
  • Check if the winning systems' architectures are open-source and can be fine-tuned for astronomical data.
Abstract

Retrieval over visually-rich documents, pages that interleave text with figures, tables, and charts, is essential for multimodal retrieval-augmented generation, yet most retrievers still discard the visual channel. The \emph{Multimodal Document Retrieval Challenge}, Track~1 of the MIR Challenge at the first EReL@MIR workshop, co-located with The Web Conference 2025, asks participants to build a \emph{single} retrieval system that handles two complementary regimes: closed-set document page retrieval within long documents from a text query (MMDocIR), and open-domain retrieval of Wikipedia-style passages from an image or image-plus-text query (M2KR). Systems are ranked by the macro-average of mean Recall@$\{1,3,5\}$ over the two tasks. The challenge drew 455 entrants and 586 submissions across 22 teams. This report describes the challenge design, datasets, and evaluation protocol; reports the final standings; and analyses the three winning teams' systems. All three build on decoder-based Multimodal-LLM embedders from the Qwen2-VL family rather than on CLIP-style encoders, and differ chiefly in whether they reach the top through fine-tuned ensembles, training-free multi-route fusion with a strong vision-language re-ranker, or zero-shot late interaction. The training-free system finished within $0.1$ point of the fine-tuned winner.

Characterizing initial human-AI proof formalization workflows

Katherine M. Collins , Simon Frieder , Jonas Bayer , Jacob Loader , Jeck Lim , Peiyang Song , Fabian Zaiser , Lexin Zhou , Shanda Li , Sam Looi , Joshua B. Tenenbaum , Umang Bhatt , Adrian Weller , Jose Hernandez-Orallo , Cameron E. Freer , Valerie Chen , Ilia Sucholutsky

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper studies how mathematicians use AI tools to formalize proofs, finding that people want AI assistance that preserves high-level human control. In a controlled study, participants achieved higher formalization accuracy with AI tools, despite limitations. This matters for astronomy because it provides a model for how LLM agents could assist in verifying scientific reasoning or formalizing data analysis pipelines. An astronomer could imagine using a similar workflow to verify the logic of a data reduction or inference procedure. A caveat is that the study focuses on mathematical proofs, not on empirical scientific workflows. The paper does not address uncertainty quantification or handling of noisy observational data.

Reason

Directly studies human-AI workflows for formalizing proofs, relevant to using LLM agents for scientific reasoning and verification.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check if the formalization workflows could be adapted for astronomical data analysis pipelines (e.g., verifying SED fitting code)
  • Look for any discussion of how AI tools handle ambiguous or incomplete information, common in astronomy
  • Examine the user study design for insights on human-AI interaction that could transfer to scientific agent design
Abstract

For centuries, human mathematicians have written proofs to substantiate their mathematical arguments; yet, the ability to automatically verify the validity of proofs has long been a challenge. Advances in AI systems' ability to generate code and engage in increasingly high-level mathematical reasoning promise to transform people's ability to formalize and thereby verify proofs. While many works focus on benchmarking the current frontier, we instead study how people use these tools. We conduct a mixed-methods analysis into the initial impact of AI on people's formalization workflows: what people claim they want, what they see as the barriers to those visions, and how they actually use and adapt AI in practice. A qualitative survey shows that people's preferences are diverse, but with a general desire for AI assistance in formalization that preserves high-level human control over the proof discovery process. To assess how people actually engage with AI for formalization under such limitations, we conduct a controlled user study in which participants formalize informal math problems and their proofs, with and without AI, across a range of mathematical problems at varying levels of difficulty and domains. Despite limitations of the tools at the time for autoformalization, participants tend to attain higher formalization accuracy when allowed access to AI tools than when formalizing on their own, with most participants flexibly choosing to use multiple different AI tools. Taken together, our work sheds light on the early stages of AI integration into formalization workflows, involving an intimate interplay of human and AI engagement.
