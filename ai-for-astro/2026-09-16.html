Predicting Partial Answer Quality and Utility in Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Fangzheng Tian , Debasis Ganguly , Craig Macdonald

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that agentic retrieval-augmented generation, where a reasoning model iteratively queries a retriever and incorporates new context, is usually evaluated only by final answer quality, giving little visibility into how the answer state evolves during the process. The main idea is an in-trajectory probing framework that forces the agent to stop after each retrieval-reasoning iteration and produce an intermediate answer, allowing the authors to define partial answer quality and partial utility as the change in quality across iterations. They find that partial answer quality often plateaus before natural termination, and they build supervised models that predict quality and utility, using these predictions for early stopping that reduces iterations by about 11 percent while preserving about 98 percent of final answer quality. This matters for astronomy because LLM agents doing literature review or multi-hop question answering over astronomical literature and catalogs could be made much more efficient by stopping early when additional retrieval is unlikely to help. A concrete workflow could be an astronomy research agent that iteratively retrieves papers and data, with a quality predictor deciding when to stop and synthesize an answer. The main caveats are that the benchmarks are generic multi-hop QA, the prediction accuracy for utility is modest, and the method assumes access to intermediate answer states during generation.

Reason

This paper studies partial answer quality and utility in agentic retrieval-augmented generation, which is directly relevant to LLM agents for literature review and multi-step scientific workflows, and the early-stopping idea could improve efficiency of astronomy research agents.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for how partial answer quality is defined and whether it could be adapted to scientific question answering with citations or data
  • Check whether the early-stopping method requires task-specific training or generalizes across domains
  • Assess whether the paper reports any evaluation on scientific or technical literature rather than generic QA
  • Look for whether the trajectory-derived signals could be used to detect unproductive retrieval loops in an astronomy agent
Abstract

Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has become a promising paradigm for multi-hop question answering, where a reasoning model iteratively issues queries to a retriever and incorporates newly retrieved context into subsequent reasoning steps. While this iterative process can improve final answer quality, current evaluations of agentic RAG largely focus on end-to-end outcomes and provide limited visibility into how a model's answer state changes during generation. In this work, we introduce an in-trajectory probing framework to study intermediate answer states in agentic RAG. Specifically, after each retrieval-reasoning iteration, we force an agentic model to stop reasoning and generate an intermediate answer based on its current state. This allows us to define two iteration-level measures: partial answer quality at each iteration, and partial utility as the change in partial answer quality across iterations. Our analysis across multi-hop QA benchmarks reveals that partial answer quality often plateaus before natural termination, with many later iterations contributing only small measurable improvements. Accordingly, we formulate two prediction tasks, partial answer quality prediction and partial utility prediction, and study trajectory-derived signals from intra-iteration, inter-iteration, and query-iteration perspectives. Experiments show that partial answer quality is more predictable than partial utility, with supervised models achieving Pearson's r above 0.43 for quality prediction. Finally, using predicted answer quality and utility for early stopping reduces average iteration count by about 11% while preserving about 98% of the final answer quality achieved by natural stopping.

AquiLLM: Evaluating Faithfulness in Open-Weight RAG-LLM Systems for Scientific Research

Bernie Boscoe , Srinath Saikrishnan , Vikram Seenivasan , Jack Stark , Andrew Lizarraga , Morgan Himes , Jonathan Soriano , PJ Allen , Tuan Do

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper asks whether open-weight retrieval-augmented generation systems can faithfully support scientific research, where faithfulness means responses stay grounded in retrieved context without unsupported claims or omissions. The main idea is AquiLLM, an offline open-weight RAG-LLM platform that gives natural-language access to a curated scientific knowledge collection, evaluated by domain experts on astronomy retrieval and analysis tasks. In plain language, it builds a private, locally hosted chatbot that answers questions by retrieving documents first, and then it checks how often the answers are actually supported by those documents. This matters directly for astronomy because research groups want LLM assistants that can search literature and generate analysis code without sending data to external services, and because the paper shows where such systems fail. A concrete workflow would be to deploy AquiLLM-style RAG over an astronomy paper collection and pipeline documentation, then test it on literature-review and code-generation tasks. The key caveats are that faithfulness degrades for synthesis and ambiguity-resolution queries, that the evaluation is a case study rather than a broad benchmark, and that open-weight models may lag proprietary ones on complex reasoning.

Reason

This paper is directly about open-weight RAG-LLM systems for scientific research with an astronomy case study, matching the interests in retrieval-augmented generation, LLM agents, and astronomical research workflows.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the exact definition and scoring rubric for faithfulness used by the domain experts
  • Check which retrieval and analysis tasks were evaluated and how the RAG collection was constructed
  • Look for failure-mode examples where synthesis or ambiguity caused unsupported claims
  • Assess whether the platform and evaluation protocol are released so you could replicate it on your own astronomy corpus
Abstract

Scientific research increasingly relies on large, heterogeneous data sources, motivating interest in retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems that provide natural language access to scientific knowledge and research workflows. Researchers are exploring the viability of these systems as natural language interfaces for document search and for generating analysis code and pipeline components. At the same time, concerns about data privacy and control over research infrastructure have motivated interest in open-weight models and open-source deployments hosted within research institutions. In astronomy, this development follows a long history of computational infrastructure development, from archival databases and SQL-based systems to LLM-assisted research tools. This paper presents a domain-expert evaluation of faithfulness for AquiLLM, an open-weight, offline RAG-LLM platform designed to support scientific research groups in the use and preservation of tacit and formal knowledge. We define faithfulness as the extent to which generated responses remain grounded in retrieved scientific context without unsupported claims or omissions. We report results from an astronomy case study evaluating AquiLLM across retrieval and scientific analysis tasks. AquiLLM performs most reliably on explicit retrieval-oriented questions grounded in the RAG collection, while faithfulness degrades for queries requiring synthesis or ambiguity resolution. These results highlight both the promise and limitations of open-weight RAG-LLM systems for scientific research and demonstrate the importance of domain-expert evaluation beyond standard benchmark leaderboards.

ScienceBuddy: Recursive-in-Recursive Self-Improvement for Interactive Scientific Agents

Shuhan Xue , Jianyuan Zhong , Ziyuan Nan , Wenbin Li , Zhaochen Yu , Jinchao Ding , Qiang Gao , Pengyu Zhan , Yuntong Zhang , Tian Cheng , Zhenfei Yin , Yingcheng Wu , Ling Yang

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper introduces ScienceBuddy, an interactive scientific research workspace where agents improve over time from researcher requests, feedback, and execution evidence. The core idea is recursive-in-recursive self-improvement: an inner loop improves the agent harness while the model is fixed, and an outer loop trains the model under the improved harness, so harness evolution and model learning reinforce each other. The authors present case studies across four scientific task families and release the system as a research product. For astronomy this is directly relevant because it sketches how to build agents that help with literature review, data analysis, and coding while learning from an astronomer's feedback. A concrete workflow would be deploying ScienceBuddy on a JWST analysis task, such as spectral fitting or catalog cross-matching, and letting it refine its own tools and prompts from your corrections. The caveat is that the abstract does not specify which scientific domains or benchmarks were used, so you should check whether the case studies include physical sciences and whether the self-improvement is validated rather than anecdotal.

Reason

This is directly about interactive scientific research agents with recursive self-improvement, which matches the interest in LLM agents for scientific workflows and automated discovery. The harness-evolution plus model-RL paradigm is highly relevant to building astronomy research agents.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Check which four scientific task families are covered and whether any are astronomy or physics
  • Look for quantitative evidence that recursive self-improvement beats static harnesses or standard fine-tuning
  • Check whether the harness evolution is reproducible and whether the released system can be run locally
  • Look for evaluation of failure modes, such as reward hacking or drift during self-improvement
Abstract

We introduce and release ScienceBuddy, an interactive scientific research workspace that brings continually improving scientific agents into researchers' everyday workflows. ScienceBuddy supports researchers in carrying out scientific tasks while transforming their requests, feedback, and execution evidence into tasks and evaluation rubrics for continual learning. At its core is recursive-in-recursive self-improvement, a paradigm that couples harness evolution with model reinforcement learning: the inner recursion improves the harness with the model fixed, while the outer recursion trains the model under the improved harness. Harness evolution shapes training experience, and model learning creates new opportunities for harness adaptation. We present case studies of researcher interaction, harness refinement, and model learning, with the benchmark cases spanning four scientific task families. By releasing ScienceBuddy as a research product, we make this paradigm available to the scientific community and take a step toward discovery intelligence: scientific AI that advances through sustained collaboration with researchers and evolves alongside the research it supports. Multi-Label Proportion Learning for Sea-Ice Type Prediction

Samira Alkaee Taleghan , Younghyun Koo , Andrew P. Barrett , Farnoush Banaei-Kashani

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses sea-ice type prediction, where the main label source is manually produced ice charts that provide only polygon-level annotations, while deep learning models typically need patch-level or pixel-level labels. It redefines the task as weakly supervised multi-label proportion learning, using a two-module framework where Multiple Instance Learning handles water-ice classification and multi-label proportion learning predicts ice-type composition, then extends this with a multimodal model that integrates SAR imagery with AMSR2 brightness temperatures and ERA5 reanalysis through modality-guided auxiliary regularization. The main claim is that the SAR-only model reduces MAE by 14.5% and more than doubles mean ice-class F1 over the best supervised baseline, while the multimodal model further reduces MAE by 21.5% and raises mean F1 by 41.2% over the SAR-only model and by 52.7% over the supervised multimodal baseline. This matters for astronomy because it shows how to learn from weak, region-level labels and how to fuse heterogeneous modalities, which is directly applicable to survey data where labels are often available only for regions or parent objects rather than individual pixels or sources. A concrete workflow could be to treat a galaxy survey with region-level classifications and combine imaging, spectra, and catalog data through a similar multimodal proportion-learning framework. The main caveat is that the method is evaluated on the AI4Arctic dataset and assumes that the proportion labels are meaningful, so one should check whether the same assumptions hold for astronomical label sources such as visual classifications or catalog flags.

Reason

This paper is about weakly supervised multi-label proportion learning for sea-ice type prediction using SAR, AMSR2, and ERA5 data, which is directly relevant to multimodal remote sensing, weak labels, and environmental prediction. The methods could transfer to astronomical survey data with polygon-level or region-level labels and multimodal fusion.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for the MIL and multi-label proportion learning modules and how they handle label noise
  • Check the modality-guided auxiliary regularization and whether it can be adapted to astronomical modalities
  • Examine the MAE and F1 improvements and whether they are robust across ice types and seasons
  • Assess whether the framework can scale to large survey datasets with region-level labels
Abstract

Sea-ice type prediction is important for climate monitoring, maritime navigation, and decision-making in polar regions. The main source of label data for this task is the ice chart, produced manually by ice analysts who interpret satellite imagery to delineate ice zones into polygons. Although ice charts are valuable, their production is labor-intensive and expensive, motivating recent efforts to automate the process using deep learning. However, deep learning models require patch-level (or pixel-level) label data for training, while ice charts provide only polygon-level annotations. As a workaround, supervised approaches often create approximate patch-level labels from polygon-level ice chart labels by assigning each sample the dominant ice type of its parent polygon. This approach enables supervised training but creates an ill-posed learning problem with intrinsically approximate solution. In this paper, we redefine sea-ice type prediction as a weakly supervised multi-label proportion learning problem to be able to directly use the polygon-level ice chart labels and avoid unnecessary label approximation for improved prediction accuracy. To address this problem, we propose a two-module framework where first Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) is used for water--ice classification, and then a multi-label proportion learning (MLPL) is introduced for ice-type composition prediction. We further extend this framework with a multimodal model that integrates SAR imagery with AMSR2 brightness temperatures and ERA5 reanalysis data through modality-guided auxiliary regularization. Evaluated on the AI4Arctic dataset, the SAR-only model reduces MAE by 14.5\% and more than doubles mean ice-class F1 over the best supervised baseline. The multimodal model further reduces MAE by 21.5\% and raises mean F1 by 41.2\% over the SAR-only model, and by 52.7\% over the supervised multimodal baseline.

Reasoning with Image Generation

Nishad Singhi , Hector Garcia Rodriguez , Aditya Arora , Marcus Rohrbach , Anna Rohrbach

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper tackles the problem that chain-of-thought reasoning in large language models is mostly textual, which limits tasks that require manipulating visual representations. The main idea, called ReImaGin, is to let a multimodal LLM call an image generation model as a flexible visual reasoning tool, so instead of relying on narrow fixed tools like depth estimation or object detection, the model can issue natural-language commands to generate or transform images, such as removing an occlusion or generating a floorplan from multiple views. This matters for astronomy because JWST and other surveys produce complex imaging where reasoning may require transforming, combining, or re-rendering visual information, for example deblending overlapping sources or reasoning about multi-band cutouts. A concrete workflow could be an LLM agent that, given JWST cutouts and catalog text, generates intermediate visual representations to test hypotheses about morphology or artifacts before producing a classification. The main caveats are that the tasks are natural-image and spatial-reasoning benchmarks, not astronomical data, and that using generative models as reasoning tools raises questions about hallucination and whether generated images preserve true physical information.

Reason

This paper uses image generation models as a flexible visual reasoning mechanism inside multimodal LLMs, which is directly relevant to multimodal models that combine images with text and could transfer to astronomical image analysis and reasoning.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for how the image generation model is interfaced with the multimodal LLM and whether the interface is general enough for astronomical image cutouts
  • Check whether the paper reports any evaluation on scientific or remote-sensing imagery that would be closer to astronomy than natural images
  • Look for diagnostics on hallucination or faithfulness of the generated intermediate images
  • Check whether the method requires task-specific fine-tuning or works zero-shot with existing models
Abstract

Chain-of-thought reasoning has revolutionized natural language processing by enabling large language models (LLMs) to decompose problems into intermediate steps before answering. Yet confining reasoning to the textual domain presents limitations for tasks requiring direct manipulation of visual representations. Recent efforts augment multimodal LLMs with external visual expert tools such as depth estimation or object detection modules, but these remain fundamentally limited by their reliance on narrow, rigid operations that cannot flexibly generate or transform visual content. We propose ReImaGin, which leverages image generation models as a flexible visual reasoning mechanism for multimodal LLMs: unlike fixed-function tools, they accept natural language commands and can perform open-ended visual operations, like removing an occlusion or generating a floorplan from multiple disjoint views of a room. Across six diverse visual reasoning tasks including multi-view spatial reasoning and collision prediction, ReImaGin consistently outperforms both text-only reasoning and specialist vision-tool baselines, with gains of up to 25\%, demonstrating the advantage of flexible, generative visual reasoning.

OPD-Aha: From Linguistic Momentum to Visual Reflection in Multimodal On-Policy Distillation

Chenhao Qiu , Dawei Li , Yechao Zhang , Lei Gong , Zhen Tan

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses a failure mode in privileged on-policy distillation for multimodal reasoning, where a teacher model evaluates student trajectories using training-only visual evidence, but when the student misinterprets an image early, the accumulating erroneous rationale pulls the teacher into the same hallucination. The main idea, called OPD-Aha, is to reconstruct the distillation target from the teacher's isolated visual preference, comparing predictions given the real image versus a visual null, rather than relying on the fragile teacher-student discrepancy. This reconstructed target suppresses continuations that contradict the image, and students learn to interrupt flawed reasoning with reflection tokens like wait and actually, leading to better use of visual evidence. For astronomy, this matters because multimodal models analyzing JWST images or spectra could hallucinate interpretations, and a method that forces reflection and visual grounding could improve reliability. A concrete workflow could be training a multimodal astronomy agent to analyze image cutouts and spectra while learning to pause and re-check when its reasoning contradicts the visual data. The main caveats are that the benchmarks are natural-image perception and reasoning tasks, the method requires a privileged teacher with access to ground-truth visual evidence during training, and it is not tested on astronomical data.

Reason

This paper introduces a multimodal on-policy distillation method that uses visual evidence to correct reasoning, which is directly relevant to multimodal models combining images and text and could transfer to astronomical image reasoning and agentic analysis.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for how the visual null comparison is implemented and whether it requires paired ground-truth images or can work with unlabeled data
  • Check whether the reflection-token mechanism could be adapted to astronomical image analysis where artifacts and noise are common
  • Assess whether the paper reports any evaluation on scientific or remote-sensing imagery
  • Look for whether the method requires task-specific fine-tuning and how much data is needed
Abstract

Privileged on-policy distillation improves multimodal reasoning by allowing a teacher to evaluate student trajectories using rich, training-only visual evidence. Both models score these trajectories while conditioning on the same student-generated prefix. When a student misinterprets an image early in a response, this accumulating erroneous rationale eventually pulls the teacher away from its visual evidence. The teacher and student converge on the same hallucination, causing standard cross-model supervision to collapse precisely where correction is most needed. We find that the teacher's visual corrective preference is not lost under this misleading agreement. Comparing the predictions of the identical teacher given the real image and a visual null reveals that the privileged evidence still pushes the model toward the correct interpretation. We introduce OPD-Aha, which reconstructs the distillation target directly from this isolated visual preference rather than relying on the fragile teacher-student discrepancy. This reconstructed target aggressively suppresses continuations that contradict the image. Trained with this objective, students learn to naturally interrupt their own flawed reasoning with reflection tokens such as wait and actually. After reflection, subsequent generation relies less on the accumulated erroneous text and more on the visual evidence. Correcting these trajectories mid-generation fundamentally alters the reasoning process, yielding broad and consistent improvements across diverse fine-grained perception and complex multimodal reasoning benchmarks. Protocol-Preserving Context Trimming for Agentic Workflows: Benefits, Failure Regimes, and Budget Guardrails

Harish Gaggar

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses the problem that agentic LLM systems need long interaction histories to preserve instructions, tool states, and unresolved dependencies, but unrestricted context growth increases cost and can reduce efficiency. The main idea is protocol-preserving context trimming, where five strategies are compared: recency-based, relevance-based, summarization, protocol-aware trimming, and adaptive budget guardrails, with the goal of preserving protocol-critical state rather than maximizing token removal. The main result is that adaptive guardrails achieve 96 percent task success and 96.3 percent protocol adherence with 56 percent token savings, while conventional strategies achieve similar token savings but much lower task success and protocol adherence. This matters for astronomy because LLM agents doing multi-step literature review, data analysis, or coding workflows need to maintain tool states and instructions over long sessions, and reliable context reduction makes such agents more scalable and affordable. A concrete workflow could be an astronomy research agent that queries catalogs, runs code, and reads papers over many steps, using protocol-aware trimming to stay within context limits without losing critical state. The main caveats are that the evaluation is on generic agentic workflows rather than scientific tasks, the protocol adherence metric is task-specific, and the method requires defining what counts as protocol-critical state.

Reason

This paper studies protocol-preserving context trimming for agentic LLM workflows, which is directly relevant to LLM agents for scientific workflows, literature review, and reproducible research where long interaction histories and tool states must be preserved.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for how protocol-critical state is defined and whether that definition could be adapted to astronomical tool workflows
  • Check whether the paper reports any evaluation on scientific or coding agents rather than generic workflows
  • Assess whether the adaptive guardrail method requires task-specific tuning or generalizes across workflow types
  • Look for whether the paper discusses failure modes such as losing tool state or instruction context, which are critical for reproducible research
Abstract

Agentic large language model (LLM) systems rely on long interaction histories to preserve instructions, tool states, intermediate decisions, and unresolved dependencies, but unrestricted context growth increases computational cost and can reduce efficiency. This study evaluates protocol-preserving context trimming as a reliability-constrained approach for multi-step agentic workflows. Five trimming strategies - recency-based, relevance-based, summarization, protocol-aware trimming, and adaptive budget guardrails - were compared across retained-context levels and workflow-complexity classes using task success, protocol adherence, valid tool calls, token savings, latency reduction, cascading failures, and critical context thresholds. Conventional strategies achieved about 60% mean token savings but lower task success (66.6-77.3%) and protocol adherence (85.5-88.6%). Protocol-aware trimming improved task success to 92.2%, while adaptive guardrails achieved 96.0% task success, 96.3% protocol adherence, and 1.0% cascading failure with 56.0% mean token savings. Retained-context budgets of 25% or less increased failure odds 10.92-fold relative to budgets of 50% or more (p < 0.001). Protocol-aware trimming produced 5.24-fold greater odds of successful completion than conventional methods under aggressive budgets, while adaptive guardrails further increased success odds 2.11-fold versus fixed protocol-aware trimming (p < 0.001). Critical context thresholds also increased with workflow complexity. These findings indicate that reliable context reduction depends more on preserving protocol-critical state than on maximizing token removal, and that adaptive guardrails can improve efficiency, scalability, and reliability in long-horizon agentic systems.

RegRet: Enhancing Region-Level Retrieval in Large Multimodal Models

Xun Liang , Honghui Yang , Weihang Pan , Ruisi Zhao , Boyuan Pan , Yao Hu , Wenxiao Wang , Binbin Lin , Deng Cai

arXiv PDF

Short Digest

The paper addresses region-level retrieval, where the goal is to align a user-specified image region with relevant regions or text, which is harder than global image retrieval. RegRet is an LMM-based framework that adds a Region-Aware Encoder to capture detailed regional features while balancing global context, and it uses multi-stage training with localized captioning and regional contrastive learning. The authors also introduce REGMB, a benchmark with 225k contrastive pairs across four multimodal retrieval tasks. For astronomy, this is promising for tasks such as retrieving similar galaxy morphologies, matching a JWST cutout to a catalog entry, or finding literature descriptions of a specific region. A concrete workflow would be to build region-level embeddings of JWST cutouts and use them to retrieve similar objects, spectra, or text from papers. The caveat is that the benchmark is natural-image and e-commerce oriented, so you should check whether the region encoder handles astronomical dynamic range, PSF effects, and multi-band inputs.

Reason

This paper is directly about region-level retrieval in large multimodal models, which is highly relevant to JWST imaging, source classification, and multimodal astronomy workflows. Region-level alignment could help match image cutouts to catalogs, spectra, or literature descriptions.

Full-Text Inspection Checklist

  • Look for whether the region encoder can handle multi-band or spectral inputs
  • Check whether the benchmark includes fine-grained or scientific imagery
  • Assess whether the method supports retrieval across images, catalogs, and text
  • Determine whether the training pipeline can be adapted with astronomical captions or metadata
Abstract

Region-level retrieval aims to align user-specified image regions with relevant regions or textual descriptions, playing a crucial role in realworld applications such as e-commerce product search and RAG. Although recent Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) have made significant strides in multimodal retrieval, they primarily focus on global-level tasks and struggle to capture effective region-level representations. To bridge this gap, we present RegRet, an LMM-based Region-level Retrieval framework that enhances the regional representations without compromising overall global retrieval performance. At its core, RegRet integrates a Region-Aware Encoder to capture detailed regional features while balancing them with the global background context. To further enhance the fine-grained understanding and discriminability of representations, we design a multi-stage training pipeline that includes detailed localized captioning and regional contrastive learning tasks. In addition, considering the absence of region-level contrastive training data and the limited diversity of evaluation tasks in current benchmarks, we introduce the REGMB benchmark. It comprises 225k contrastive pairs, covering four multimodal retrieval tasks. Extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of our approach. RegRet outperforms strong baselines in the zero-shot setting. Further training with contrastive learning leads to an average improvement of more than 20\% on both REGMB and public benchmarks, while achieving comparable or better results on global-level retrieval tasks.
